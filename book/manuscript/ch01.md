On my desk sits a cheap plastic torch that a first-year student handed to me many years ago. It had stopped working, and she wanted to know why. She had already put in fresh batteries. She had checked the bulb against the light. She had even shaken it, which, to be fair, is what most of us do first. What she had not done was think about the torch as a *circuit*: a closed path that charge must travel round, with something pushing it and something resisting it. Ten minutes with a meter showed that a spring contact had corroded, leaving a gap in the path. Scrape the contact clean, close the gap, and the torch lit.

I keep that torch because it reminds me of something important. Almost every electrical problem you will ever meet, from a dead torch to a misbehaving sensor in a car, comes down to the same three ideas: **charge**, **current** and **voltage**. If you understand these properly – not as words to memorise, but as physical things you can picture – everything else in this book will feel like common sense applied carefully. If you skip them, Ohm's law will feel like a magic spell, and Thévenin's theorem will feel like witchcraft.

So we begin at the beginning. Take your time with this chapter. It is the foundation for the whole house.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- explain what electric charge is and state its unit, the coulomb;
- define current as the rate of flow of charge and calculate it using I = Q / t;
- explain voltage as energy per unit charge and calculate it using V = W / Q;
- distinguish between conventional current and electron flow;
- use SI prefixes confidently to convert between milliamps, kilovolts and so on;
- connect an ammeter and a voltmeter correctly in a simple circuit.
:::

## 1.1 Why Circuit Analysis Matters

Circuit analysis is the skill of working out what every part of an electrical circuit is doing: how much current flows in each wire, what voltage appears across each component, and how much power each part takes. It sounds modest. In reality it is the language that every electrical and electronic engineer speaks, whether they design power stations, mobile phones, electric vehicles, medical monitors or the controller inside your washing machine.

There are two reasons to learn it properly.

The first is **design**. If you are building something, you need to choose component values before you pick up a soldering iron. Which resistor will stop an LED from burning out? How thick must a cable be to carry the current of an electric shower without overheating? Will a battery last a day or a week? Each of these questions is answered with the methods in this book.

The second is **diagnosis**. When something goes wrong, analysis tells you what you *should* measure. If you know a point in a circuit ought to sit at 5 volts and your meter reads 0.3 volts, you have found a clue. Without the analysis, a meter reading is just a number. With it, the number tells a story.

Throughout this book I will treat circuit analysis as a problem-solving discipline. Each chapter gives you a small number of tools, explains why they work, and then shows you, step by step, how to use them on realistic problems. The worked examples are not decoration. They are where the learning actually happens, so please work through them with a pencil in hand rather than simply reading them.

## 1.2 Charge: Where Everything Starts

All matter is made of atoms, and atoms contain smaller particles. For our purposes, two matter: the **proton**, which carries a positive electric charge, and the **electron**, which carries an equal and opposite negative charge. A normal atom has the same number of each, so its total charge is zero. We say it is *electrically neutral*.

Charge is a fundamental property, rather like mass. You cannot see it, but you can see what it does. Like charges repel one another; unlike charges attract. If you have ever rubbed a balloon on a jumper and stuck it to the wall, you have moved electrons from one material to another and watched the attraction at work.

The unit of charge is the **coulomb**, symbol **C**, named after the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb. One electron carries a tiny charge:

::: eq
charge on one electron, e = 1.602 × 10^−19^ C
:::

That number is so small that one coulomb represents an enormous crowd of electrons. To find out how many, we divide:

::: eq
number of electrons in 1 C = {{frac|1|1.602 × 10^−19^}} ≈ 6.24 × 10^18^
:::

Six point two four million million million electrons. You will never need to count them individually, but it helps to know that when we talk about a coulomb we are talking about a vast number of tiny charged particles moving together.

The symbol for an amount of charge is **Q**. You will see it in equations such as Q = 0.5 C, meaning half a coulomb of charge.

### Conductors and insulators

Why does electricity flow easily through copper but not through plastic? The answer lies in how tightly atoms hold on to their outer electrons.

In a metal such as copper, each atom gives up one or more of its outermost electrons to a shared "sea" of **free electrons** that can wander through the whole piece of metal. The atoms that remain are fixed in a regular arrangement called a lattice, and because they have lost an electron each, they are positive ions. Figure 1.1 shows the picture.

![Figure 1.1 — Inside a copper wire. Positive ions sit fixed in a lattice while free electrons wander between them. When a voltage is applied, the electrons drift slowly towards the positive end; by convention, we say current flows the other way.](fig/ch01_lattice.svg){width=100%}

Materials with plenty of free electrons are called **conductors**. Copper, aluminium, silver and gold are good examples, which is why they are used for wires, tracks on circuit boards and connector contacts.

In materials such as glass, rubber, PVC and dry wood, the electrons are held tightly by their atoms. There are almost no free electrons, so charge cannot move easily. These materials are **insulators**, and they are just as important as conductors. The plastic sleeve round a cable keeps the current where you want it, and keeps it out of you.

Between the two sit **semiconductors** such as silicon. Their behaviour can be controlled very precisely, which is the basis of every diode, transistor and microchip. We will not need semiconductor physics in this book, but you will meet these devices later in your studies, and the analysis methods you learn here apply to them too.

## 1.3 Current: Charge on the Move

Free electrons in a wire are always jiggling about at random, because of heat. On average, though, they go nowhere, so there is no overall flow. Connect the wire to a battery and something changes: the electrons start to drift, on average, in one direction. That steady movement of charge is an **electric current**.

Current is defined as the *rate* at which charge passes a point in a circuit. Imagine standing beside a wire and counting how many coulombs pass you every second. That count is the current. Its symbol is **I** (from the French *intensité*), and its unit is the **ampere**, usually shortened to **amp**, symbol **A**.

::: keyeq
**Current**

I = {{frac|Q|t}}

where I is current in amperes (A), Q is charge in coulombs (C) and t is time in seconds (s).
:::

One ampere means one coulomb of charge passing a point every second. So a current of 2 A means 2 coulombs per second, and 0.1 A means one tenth of a coulomb per second.

The equation can be rearranged to find either of the other quantities:

::: eq
Q = I × t          t = {{frac|Q|I}}
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 1.1 — Charge delivered by a current**

A current of 0.25 A flows through a small motor for 2 minutes. How much charge passes through the motor?

**Step 1: Write down what you know, in SI units.** I = 0.25 A. The time is 2 minutes, which must be converted to seconds: t = 2 × 60 = 120 s.

**Step 2: Choose the equation.** We want charge, so Q = I × t.

**Step 3: Substitute and calculate.** Q = 0.25 × 120 = 30 C.

**Step 4: Sense-check.** A quarter of a coulomb every second, for 120 seconds, gives 30 coulombs. That is a reasonable answer.

**Answer:** 30 C of charge passes through the motor.
:::

Notice the habit of converting minutes to seconds *before* substituting. Unit mistakes are the single most common cause of wrong answers in electrical calculations, and they are completely avoidable. I will keep reminding you.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.2 — Counting the electrons**

How many electrons pass through the motor in Worked Example 1.1?

**Solution.** Each electron carries 1.602 × 10^−19^ C. The number of electrons is the total charge divided by the charge on one electron:

number = {{frac|30|1.602 × 10^−19^}} ≈ 1.87 × 10^20^ electrons.

**Answer:** about 1.9 × 10^20^ electrons – roughly two hundred billion billion.
:::

### How fast do the electrons move?

Here is a question that surprises almost everyone. When you switch on a light, it comes on instantly. So the electrons must be racing round the circuit at enormous speed, surely?

They are not. In a typical household copper wire carrying one amp, the average drift speed of the electrons is well under a tenth of a millimetre per second. At that pace an electron would take more than an hour to travel a single metre.

So why is the light instant? Because the wire is already *full* of free electrons, end to end. When the switch closes, the electric field that pushes them travels along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, and every electron in the circuit begins to drift almost at once. Think of a garden hose that is already full of water. Turn on the tap and water comes out of the far end immediately, even though the water that left the tap has only moved a few centimetres.

This idea – that the *push* travels quickly while the *charge* moves slowly – is worth holding on to. It explains why current is the same all the way round a simple loop: the electrons are a continuous chain, and the chain cannot bunch up in one place.

### Conventional current and electron flow

Look again at Figure 1.1 and you will notice two arrows pointing in opposite directions. This deserves an explanation, because it confuses many beginners.

In the eighteenth century, long before the electron was discovered, scientists decided that current flowed from the positive terminal of a source, round the circuit, to the negative terminal. This direction is called **conventional current**. When the electron was discovered in 1897, it turned out that in metals the moving particles are negatively charged and actually travel the *other* way, from negative to positive. This is **electron flow**.

By then, the convention was everywhere: in textbooks, in equations, in the symbols on circuit diagrams. Rather than rewrite everything, engineers kept conventional current. And here is the reassuring part: **for circuit analysis it makes no difference**. A negative charge moving left has exactly the same electrical effect as a positive charge moving right. Every law and every method in this book gives correct answers using conventional current.

::: tip
**In Practice**

Throughout this book, and throughout almost all of engineering, current arrows show **conventional current**: out of the positive terminal of a source, round the circuit, and back into the negative terminal. The arrows on diode and transistor symbols follow the same convention. Learn it once and you will never have to think about electron flow again.
:::

Figure 1.2 shows a simple circuit with the conventional current marked. The cell pushes current out of its positive terminal (the longer plate in the symbol), through the closed switch and lamp, and back to the negative terminal.

![Figure 1.2 — The simplest useful circuit: a 9 V cell, a switch and a lamp. The arrow shows the direction of conventional current. Open the switch and the path is broken, so no current flows anywhere.](fig/ch01_simple_circuit.svg){width=62%}

### The idea of a closed circuit

Figure 1.2 also illustrates the most basic rule of all: **current only flows round a complete, closed path**. Open the switch and the path is broken. The cell still has its push, but the charges have nowhere to go, so the current falls to zero everywhere in the loop – not just at the switch.

This is precisely what had happened to my student's torch. The corroded spring acted like an open switch. A complete circuit is the first thing to check whenever "nothing happens".

## 1.4 Voltage: The Push Behind the Current

Charge does not move round a circuit by itself. Something must push it, and that something is supplied by a battery, a generator, a solar panel or a power supply. The push is called **voltage**, and it is the idea beginners most often find slippery. Let us pin it down carefully.

A battery uses a chemical reaction to separate charge, piling up extra electrons at one terminal and leaving a shortage at the other. This separation stores energy, rather as lifting a weight stores energy. When a circuit connects the two terminals, charge flows through it and the stored energy is released – as light in a lamp, as heat in a kettle element, as motion in a motor.

**Voltage** measures how much energy each coulomb of charge gains from the source, or gives up to a component. Its formal name is **potential difference**, and its unit is the **volt**, symbol **V**, named after the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta, who built the first battery in 1800.

::: keyeq
**Voltage**

V = {{frac|W|Q}}

where V is voltage in volts (V), W is energy in joules (J) and Q is charge in coulombs (C).
:::

So one volt means one joule of energy per coulomb of charge. A 9 V battery gives each coulomb that passes through it 9 joules of energy. A 230 V mains supply gives each coulomb 230 joules, which is why mains electricity is so much more dangerous than a torch battery.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.3 — Energy from a battery**

A 12 V car battery delivers 1800 C of charge to the starter motor. How much energy does it supply?

**Step 1: List the known quantities.** V = 12 V, Q = 1800 C.

**Step 2: Rearrange V = W / Q to make energy the subject.** Multiply both sides by Q: W = V × Q.

**Step 3: Calculate.** W = 12 × 1800 = 21 600 J, or 21.6 kJ.

**Answer:** the battery supplies 21.6 kJ of energy.
:::

### Voltage is always *between* two points

This is the single most important thing to understand about voltage, and it is where many people go wrong. **Voltage is always measured between two points.** It is a *difference* in electrical potential, in exactly the way that the height of a hill is a difference between the top and the bottom.

Asking "what is the voltage at this wire?" is like asking "how high is this step?" The question only makes sense if you say "compared with what?" When engineers do talk about the voltage "at" a point, they always mean the voltage between that point and an agreed reference point, called **ground**, **earth** or the **0 V rail**. We will make a lot of use of that idea in later chapters, especially in nodal analysis.

It follows that:

- voltage appears *across* a component – between its two ends;
- current flows *through* a component – in at one end and out of the other.

Say those two phrases to yourself until they feel natural. "The voltage across the resistor" and "the current through the resistor" are correct. "The current across the resistor" and "the voltage through the resistor" are not just clumsy; they show a misunderstanding that will lead to errors when circuits become more complicated.

### Polarity

Because voltage is a difference, it has a direction, called its **polarity**. One end of a component is at a higher potential than the other. We mark the higher end with a + sign and the lower end with a − sign. When conventional current flows through a resistor, it always enters at the + end and leaves at the − end, because charge loses energy as it passes through. In a battery that is delivering power, the opposite happens: current enters at − and leaves at +, because the charge *gains* energy inside the source.

That simple observation will be the key to Kirchhoff's voltage law in Chapter 5, and to every sign decision you make in mesh analysis later on. Keep it in mind.

## 1.5 The Water Analogy – and Where It Breaks Down

Electricity is invisible, so teachers have long used water flowing in pipes to help students picture it. Figure 1.3 shows the version I find most helpful.

![Figure 1.3 — The water analogy. The height difference between the tanks plays the part of voltage, the rate of flow plays the part of current, and the narrow section of pipe plays the part of resistance. A pump, like a battery, lifts the water back up to keep it flowing.](fig/ch01_water.svg){width=100%}

Water in the high tank has more potential energy than water in the low tank. The *difference in height* drives the flow, just as a *difference in electrical potential* drives a current. The *rate of flow* – litres per second – corresponds to current, coulombs per second. A narrow section of pipe restricts the flow, just as a resistor restricts current. And a pump that lifts water back to the top is doing the job of a battery.

The analogy predicts several things correctly:

- No height difference, no flow. No voltage, no current.
- A bigger height difference gives a faster flow. A bigger voltage gives a larger current through the same resistance.
- A narrower pipe slows the flow. A larger resistance reduces the current.
- Water is not used up as it flows; it is simply moved. Charge is not used up in a circuit either. What is "used" is *energy*.

That last point is worth stressing. People often say that a lamp "uses up current". It does not. The current flowing into a lamp is exactly equal to the current flowing out. What the lamp uses is energy, which the charge carries and gives up as it passes through.

But all analogies have limits, and it is useful to know where this one stops working. Electric current does not "fall" under gravity, so the orientation of a circuit on the page means nothing. An open switch is not like a closed valve holding back pressurised water: there is no build-up of charge pushing on the gap in the same way. And electricity has effects – magnetism, for example – that water does not. Use the analogy to build your intuition, then let it go when the real equations take over.

## 1.6 Direct and Alternating Current

So far we have imagined a battery pushing current steadily in one direction. That is **direct current**, or **DC**. The voltage of a battery or a laboratory power supply stays essentially constant over time.

The mains supply in your home is different. In the United Kingdom, the voltage rises and falls in a smooth wave, reversing direction fifty times every second. This is **alternating current**, or **AC**. Figure 1.4 compares the two.

![Figure 1.4 — Direct current stays constant over time; alternating current rises, falls and reverses. UK mains alternates at 50 Hz, completing one full cycle every 20 milliseconds.](fig/ch01_dc_ac.svg){width=100%}

This book concentrates on DC circuits made of resistors and sources. That is not a limitation; it is the right place to start. Every technique you will learn – Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, nodal and mesh analysis, Thévenin and Norton – carries straight over to AC circuits later, with only one change: resistances are replaced by a more general quantity called impedance. Master the methods with DC and AC will feel like an extension rather than a new subject.

## 1.7 Units and Prefixes: The Engineer's Shorthand

Real circuits involve a huge range of values. A sensor might produce a few millionths of a volt; a transmission line carries 400 000 volts. A hearing-aid battery supplies a thousandth of an amp; a car's starter motor draws a couple of hundred amps. To keep numbers manageable, engineers use **SI prefixes**, each representing a power of ten.

::: {.table-wrap}
| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Example |
|:-------|:------:|:-----------|:--------|
| giga   | G      | 10^9^ (one thousand million) | 1.5 GHz processor clock |
| mega   | M      | 10^6^ (one million) | 2.2 MΩ resistor |
| kilo   | k      | 10^3^ (one thousand) | 4.7 kΩ resistor, 11 kV cable |
| —      | —      | 10^0^ = 1 | 12 V, 3 A |
| milli  | m      | 10^−3^ (one thousandth) | 20 mA LED current |
| micro  | µ      | 10^−6^ (one millionth) | 50 µA sensor current |
| nano   | n      | 10^−9^ | 100 nA leakage current |
| pico   | p      | 10^−12^ | 22 pF capacitor |

: Table 1.1 — The SI prefixes you will meet most often in circuit work.
:::

Notice that the prefixes go up and down in steps of a thousand. This is called **engineering notation**, and it matches the way component values are printed and the way meters display their readings.

Two small points of style matter. First, the case of the letter is significant: **m** means milli (one thousandth), while **M** means mega (one million). Confusing the two is a mistake of a factor of a thousand million. Second, the prefix sits directly in front of the unit with no space, as in 4.7 kΩ or 20 mA, while a space separates the number from the unit.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.4 — Converting with prefixes**

Express (a) 0.0035 A in milliamps, (b) 47 000 Ω in kilohms, and (c) 250 mV in volts.

**(a)** One milliamp is 10^−3^ A, so to convert amps to milliamps we multiply by 1000: 0.0035 × 1000 = 3.5 mA.

**(b)** One kilohm is 1000 Ω, so we divide by 1000: 47 000 ÷ 1000 = 47 kΩ.

**(c)** 250 mV = 250 × 10^−3^ V = 0.25 V.
:::

::: tip
**A Habit Worth Forming**

When you substitute values into an equation, convert everything to base units first: volts, amps, ohms, seconds, joules. Do the calculation. Then, at the very end, choose a sensible prefix for the answer. Mixing prefixes part-way through a calculation is asking for trouble.
:::

### Battery capacity: a real-world use of Q = I × t

The label on a phone or laptop battery often gives its capacity in **milliamp-hours** (mAh). This is a unit of charge in disguise. A current of one milliamp flowing for one hour delivers one milliamp-hour.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.5 — What is inside a phone battery?**

A phone battery is rated at 4000 mAh and 3.85 V. (a) How much charge does it store, in coulombs? (b) How much energy does it store? (c) If the phone draws an average of 250 mA, roughly how long will a full charge last?

**(a)** Convert to base units. 4000 mAh = 4 A × 1 h = 4 A × 3600 s. Using Q = I × t, Q = 4 × 3600 = 14 400 C.

**(b)** Using W = V × Q, W = 3.85 × 14 400 = 55 440 J, or about 55 kJ. (Battery makers often quote this as 15.4 Wh, watt-hours, which is the same energy in different units: 55 440 ÷ 3600 = 15.4.)

**(c)** Using t = Q / I with I = 0.25 A: t = 14 400 ÷ 0.25 = 57 600 s. Dividing by 3600 gives 16 hours.

**Answer:** about 14 400 C, about 55 kJ, and roughly 16 hours of use. In reality the figure will be a little lower, because batteries cannot deliver every last coulomb and phones do not draw a perfectly steady current, but the estimate is a useful guide.
:::

## 1.8 Measuring Current and Voltage

The instrument you will use most often is the **digital multimeter**, which can be switched to measure voltage, current or resistance. How you connect it depends entirely on what you want to measure, and getting this right is both a matter of accuracy and of safety.

**To measure current**, you need the current to flow *through* the meter, so it must become part of the path. You break the circuit at the point of interest and connect the meter into the gap. This is called connecting it **in series**. A meter used this way is an **ammeter**.

**To measure voltage**, you want to compare the potential at two points, so you touch the meter's probes onto those two points without breaking anything. This is called connecting it **in parallel** (or "across" the component). A meter used this way is a **voltmeter**.

![Figure 1.5 — An ammeter (A) is connected in series, so that the circuit current flows through it. A voltmeter (V) is connected in parallel with the component whose voltage it measures.](fig/ch01_meters.svg){width=72%}

The reason for the difference lies in the meters themselves. A good ammeter has a very *low* internal resistance, so that putting it in the path barely changes the current. A good voltmeter has a very *high* internal resistance, so that it draws almost no current from the points it is comparing. In Chapter 8 we will see exactly how much error a real meter introduces, and why it matters.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfall: The Ammeter Across a Supply**

Never connect a multimeter that is set to measure current directly across a battery or power supply. Because the ammeter has almost no resistance, it acts as a short circuit. A very large current flows, which will at best blow the meter's internal fuse, and at worst damage the meter, the supply or you. Before you touch the probes to anything, glance at the dial and the socket the red lead is plugged into. It takes two seconds and it is a habit every working engineer has.
:::

## 1.9 A Problem-Solving Method You Will Use Throughout

You will have noticed that the worked examples follow a pattern. That is deliberate. Good problem-solvers in engineering are rarely the people who know the most formulae; they are the people who approach problems in an orderly way. Here is the method we will use and refine throughout this book.

1. **Read and picture.** Read the question twice. Sketch the circuit if one is not given. Mark everything you know on the sketch.
2. **List knowns and unknowns.** Write each known quantity with its unit, converted to base SI units. Write clearly what you are asked to find.
3. **Choose the tool.** Decide which law, equation or method connects the knowns to the unknowns. In this chapter there are only two choices; by the end of the book you will have a whole toolkit.
4. **Solve on paper, symbols first.** Rearrange the equation in symbols before you substitute numbers. It is quicker, it is easier to check, and it shows your reasoning.
5. **Substitute and calculate.** Keep at least three or four significant figures in intermediate steps.
6. **Sense-check.** Is the answer the right order of magnitude? Has it got the right unit? Does it agree with common sense? A torch bulb drawing 4000 A is clearly wrong.
7. **State the answer clearly**, with a sensible prefix and number of significant figures.

It may feel slow at first. Use it anyway. On harder problems, especially in mesh and nodal analysis, this structure is what keeps you from getting lost.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Forgetting to convert time to seconds.** Minutes and hours must become seconds before using I = Q / t.
- **Confusing m and M.** A 2 mA current is two thousandths of an amp; a 2 MΩ resistor is two million ohms.
- **Talking about voltage "at" a single point** without a reference. Voltage is always between two points.
- **Believing current is used up** in a lamp or resistor. The same current flows out as flows in; it is energy that is converted.
- **Connecting an ammeter in parallel.** Ammeters go in series; voltmeters go in parallel.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Charge** (Q) is a basic property of matter, measured in coulombs (C). One electron carries 1.602 × 10^−19^ C, so one coulomb is about 6.24 × 10^18^ electrons.
- **Conductors** contain free electrons that can move; **insulators** do not.
- **Current** (I) is the rate of flow of charge: I = Q / t. One ampere is one coulomb per second.
- By convention, current flows from the positive terminal of a source, round the circuit, to the negative terminal. This **conventional current** is used throughout engineering.
- Current only flows round a **complete, closed path**.
- **Voltage** (V), or potential difference, is the energy per unit charge: V = W / Q. One volt is one joule per coulomb.
- Voltage is always measured **between two points**. It appears *across* a component; current flows *through* it.
- **SI prefixes** in steps of a thousand keep values manageable. Convert to base units before calculating.
- **Ammeters** connect in series; **voltmeters** connect in parallel.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

I = {{frac|Q|t}}          Q = I × t          V = {{frac|W|Q}}          W = V × Q
:::

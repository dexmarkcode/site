# The Electric Vehicle as an Engineering System

::: chapter-intro
An electric car has far fewer moving parts than a petrol car, and yet it is harder to engineer well. This chapter explains why. We look at where the technology came from, the family of electrified architectures, the components of a battery electric powertrain and the path energy takes from the socket to the tyre. We close with a method for attacking problems that the rest of the book will use again and again.
:::

The first time I drove a properly engineered electric vehicle, the thing that struck me was not the acceleration, impressive as it was. It was the silence at a standstill. There was no idle, no tremor through the steering wheel, nothing to tell you the machine was ready except a word on the screen. You pressed the pedal and the car simply moved, as though somebody had given it a push from behind.

That smoothness hides a great deal of work. Behind the pedal sits a chain of systems that must cooperate within milliseconds: a battery holding as much energy as a small house uses in a week, a stack of semiconductor switches turning several hundred amps on and off twenty thousand times a second, a motor spinning at speeds that would wreck most petrol engines, and software watching thousands of signals for the first sign of trouble. When any one of those links is weak, the driver feels it as lost range, a slow charge, a warning light or, in the worst cases, a fire.

This book is about the engineering of that chain. It is written for students meeting the subject for the first time, for engineers moving across from combustion powertrains, and for technicians and managers who want to understand what their colleagues are talking about. My aim throughout is not just to describe how things work, but to show you how to reason about them when they do not.

## A Short History, and Why It Matters

People are often surprised to hear that electric cars are older than petrol ones in commercial terms. By the 1890s, electric carriages were on sale in Britain, France and the United States. London had a fleet of battery-electric cabs in 1897, nicknamed "Hummingbirds" for the noise they made. In New York at the turn of the century, electric vehicles outsold petrol cars for a period. They were quiet, clean and needed no hand-cranking to start.

Then three things happened in fairly quick succession. Cheap oil was found in large quantities. Henry Ford's moving assembly line brought the price of a petrol car down to a level no hand-built electric could match. And in 1912 the electric self-starter removed the petrol car's most hated chore. Lead-acid batteries could not compete on range, and the electric car became a curiosity: milk floats, golf buggies and factory trucks.

The story matters for an engineer because it shows that the technology lost on *energy storage and cost*, not on the motor. Electric motors were already efficient and reliable a century ago. What was missing was a battery that could hold enough energy in a sensible mass and volume, at a price people would pay.

That is still the central problem. The modern revival began in the 1990s, when California's zero-emission vehicle rules pushed manufacturers to build cars such as the General Motors EV1, first with lead-acid and then nickel-metal hydride batteries. These cars proved the concept and were then withdrawn. The breakthrough came from lithium-ion cells developed for laptops and phones. A small Californian company showed in 2008 that thousands of commodity laptop cells, carefully managed, could power a sports car over a useful distance. Nissan launched the Leaf in 2010 as a mass-market hatchback, and from that point the question was no longer whether electric cars would work, but how fast they would get cheap.

The answer turned out to be: faster than almost anyone forecast. Industry surveys show average lithium-ion pack prices falling from more than a thousand US dollars per kilowatt-hour around 2010 to somewhere between one hundred and one hundred and fifty dollars by the mid-2020s, roughly a tenfold reduction in about fifteen years. Energy density at the cell level more than doubled over a similar period. Those two curves explain most of what has happened to the car industry since.

::: note
**Engineer's Note**

When someone tells you a new technology will "change everything", ask which curve it bends. Electric vehicles did not take off because motors improved; they took off because cost per kilowatt-hour fell and energy per kilogram rose. Keep that question in your back pocket when you read Chapter 15 on emerging technologies.
:::

## The Family of Electrified Vehicles

"Electric vehicle" is a loose term. In practice engineers talk about a spectrum of architectures, sorted by how much of the driving work comes from electricity and where that electricity comes from. Figure 1.1 shows the main members of the family.

![**Figure 1.1** The main electrified powertrain architectures. Arrows show the direction of mechanical (grey) and electrical (teal) power flow. As we move from left to right, the battery grows and the engine shrinks or disappears.](figures/fig1_1.png)

### Mild hybrids

A mild hybrid adds a small electric machine, usually a belt-driven starter-generator on a 48 V system, to an otherwise conventional engine. It can restart the engine smoothly, recover a little braking energy and give a brief torque boost. It cannot drive the car on electricity alone in any meaningful way. Fuel savings are modest, often in the region of five to ten per cent, but the cost is low and the engineering changes are contained. You will meet 48 V systems again in Chapter 12.

### Full hybrids

A full hybrid has a larger battery, typically around one to two kilowatt-hours, and a traction motor strong enough to move the car at low speed without the engine. There are three classic arrangements:

- **Series hybrid.** The engine never drives the wheels. It turns a generator, and the electricity either charges the battery or feeds the traction motor. This lets the engine run at its most efficient point, but every joule passes through two conversions, generator and motor, which costs efficiency on the motorway.
- **Parallel hybrid.** The engine and motor are both mechanically connected to the wheels, usually through the gearbox. Either or both can drive. It is efficient at steady speed but harder to control smoothly.
- **Power-split (series-parallel) hybrid.** A planetary gear set divides engine power between a mechanical path and an electrical path. This is the arrangement made famous by Toyota's hybrid system. It behaves like a continuously variable transmission and is very efficient in town.

### Plug-in hybrids and range extenders

A plug-in hybrid electric vehicle (PHEV) has a battery large enough, often ten to twenty-five kilowatt-hours, to cover typical daily journeys on electricity, and a charging inlet so it can be filled from the grid. The engine takes over for longer trips. A range-extended electric vehicle (EREV) is a series hybrid with a big battery: the wheels are always driven electrically, and a small engine acts purely as a generator when the battery runs low.

PHEVs are a compromise, and like all compromises they are only as good as the way they are used. A plug-in hybrid that is charged every night can do most of its mileage electrically. One that is never plugged in carries a heavy battery around for nothing and can use more fuel than the conventional car it replaced.

### Battery electric vehicles

A battery electric vehicle (BEV) has no engine at all. All of its energy comes from the grid and is stored in a battery of, typically, forty to one hundred kilowatt-hours for a passenger car, and several hundred kilowatt-hours for a heavy truck. It is mechanically the simplest of the family and electrically the most demanding. It is the main subject of this book.

### Fuel cell electric vehicles

A fuel cell electric vehicle (FCEV) is electrically driven like a BEV, but it makes its electricity on board from hydrogen in a proton exchange membrane fuel cell. A modest buffer battery handles transients and regenerative braking. Fuel cells have found a niche in some heavy-duty and fleet applications, but the cost of hydrogen and its infrastructure has kept passenger-car volumes small. Almost everything we say about motors, inverters and DC-DC converters applies to FCEVs as well.

## Anatomy of a Battery Electric Powertrain

Let us now open the bonnet, or more accurately lift the floor, of a typical battery electric car. Figure 1.2 shows the main blocks and the links between them. It is worth learning this picture well. Almost every chapter that follows zooms in on one of its boxes.

![**Figure 1.2** The battery electric powertrain. Heavy lines carry high-voltage DC or AC power; dashed lines carry control and communication signals. The vehicle control unit coordinates the whole system.](figures/fig1_2.png)

### The traction battery

The battery is a large assembly of individual cells, connected in series to reach the required voltage and in parallel to reach the required capacity. A typical 400 V pack might use 96 cells in series; an 800 V pack, around 192 to 200. The cells sit inside modules or, increasingly, directly inside the pack structure, together with cooling plates, busbars, sensing wires, fuses and a steel or aluminium enclosure strong enough to survive a crash and keep water out for the life of the car.

The battery is usually the single most expensive component. It also sets most of the vehicle's limits: how far it can go, how fast it can charge, how much power is available on a cold morning and how the car will have aged after eight years. Chapters 4 to 6 deal with it in detail.

### The battery management system

The battery management system (BMS) is the electronics and software that look after the cells. It measures every cell voltage, a sample of temperatures and the pack current. From those measurements it estimates the state of charge, the state of health and the power the battery can safely deliver or accept in the next few seconds. It balances cells that drift apart, controls the contactors that connect the pack to the rest of the car and shuts everything down if something goes wrong. A good BMS is the difference between a battery that lasts fifteen years and one that loses a fifth of its capacity in three.

### Contactors, pre-charge and the high-voltage junction

Between the battery and the rest of the high-voltage system sit two main contactors, one on each pole, plus a smaller pre-charge contactor in series with a resistor. When the car wakes up, the pre-charge path slowly charges the large capacitors in the inverter and other loads. Only when their voltage is close to the battery voltage does the BMS close the main contactors. Without pre-charge, the inrush current into those capacitors could weld the contacts shut. We work through the sizing in Chapter 3.

A high-voltage junction box, sometimes called the power distribution unit, then splits the DC supply to the inverter, the on-board charger, the DC-DC converter, the electric heater and the air-conditioning compressor, each with its own fuse.

### The traction inverter

The motor needs alternating current whose frequency and amplitude change with speed and torque. The battery supplies direct current. The inverter sits between them. It contains six power switches, either silicon insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) or, increasingly, silicon carbide MOSFETs, arranged in three half-bridges. By switching them rapidly in carefully timed patterns, the inverter synthesises three sinusoidal currents. It also runs in reverse during regenerative braking, turning the motor's AC output back into DC to charge the battery.

The inverter is the heart of the drive's control system. Its microcontroller runs the motor control algorithm, typically at ten to twenty thousand updates per second, and it is responsible for delivering exactly the torque the vehicle control unit asks for. Chapters 10 and 11 cover it.

### The traction motor

Most passenger BEVs use a permanent-magnet synchronous motor (PMSM), because it offers the best efficiency and power density. Some use induction motors, which need no magnets and are cheap and robust, and a few use wound-rotor (electrically excited) synchronous motors, which avoid rare-earth magnets while retaining good efficiency. Motor speeds of 15,000 to 20,000 revolutions per minute are now normal, and some designs exceed 25,000. Chapters 7 to 9 explain how these machines work, how they are designed and how they are controlled.

### The reduction gear and driveline

Because electric motors produce strong torque from standstill and keep working over a very wide speed range, most BEVs need only a single fixed reduction gear, typically with a ratio of about 8:1 to 12:1, followed by a differential. Removing the multi-speed gearbox is one of the main sources of mechanical simplicity in an EV. A few high-performance cars use two-speed gearboxes on one axle, to get better efficiency at very high speed, but that is the exception.

The motor, inverter and gearbox are often built into a single unit, called an electric drive unit or e-axle. Integration saves cables, connectors, mass and cost, and it shortens the high-current AC connections between inverter and motor.

### The on-board charger

To charge from an ordinary AC supply, the car needs a rectifier and a controlled converter on board. This is the on-board charger (OBC). Typical ratings are 7.4 kW single-phase or 11 kW three-phase in Europe, with 22 kW available on some models. For fast DC charging, the conversion happens in the charging station and DC flows directly into the battery through the charge inlet, bypassing the OBC. Chapter 12 covers the on-board charger and Chapter 13 the charging infrastructure.

### The DC-DC converter and the low-voltage network

Despite its large traction battery, an EV still has a 12 V (or, increasingly, 48 V) network for lights, infotainment, control units, wipers and safety systems. There is no alternator, so a DC-DC converter takes power from the high-voltage battery and steps it down. A small low-voltage battery is still fitted, mainly to wake the car and to keep safety-critical systems alive if the high-voltage system is shut down.

### Thermal management

Every component in the chain generates heat, and almost every one has a narrow comfortable temperature range. Lithium-ion cells prefer roughly 15 to 35 °C. Power semiconductors must keep their junctions below about 150 to 175 °C. Motor windings are limited by their insulation class and magnets by the risk of demagnetisation. Meanwhile the passengers want to be warm in winter and cool in summer. The thermal management system ties all of this together with pumps, valves, radiators, chillers and, in most modern designs, a heat pump. In cold climates it can make a larger difference to real-world range than any other subsystem. Chapter 14 covers it.

### The vehicle control unit

Finally, something has to decide what all these parts should do. The vehicle control unit (VCU), sometimes merged into a larger domain or zonal controller, reads the accelerator and brake pedals, the gear selector and the state of every subsystem, and then decides how much torque to request from each motor, how to blend regenerative and friction braking, when to cool the battery and when to stop charging. It communicates over CAN, CAN FD or automotive Ethernet. In a modern car, much of what the driver experiences as "how the car feels" lives in this software.

## Following the Energy

A useful habit when you meet any powertrain is to follow a single kilowatt-hour on its journey and count the losses. For a battery electric vehicle, the journey starts at the wall socket and ends at the contact patch of the tyre.

![**Figure 1.3** Where the energy goes: an indicative grid-to-wheel efficiency chain for a battery electric car in mixed driving. Each bar shows energy remaining after the stage; the final figure is the share delivered as mechanical work at the wheels.](figures/fig1_3.png)

Figure 1.3 shows a representative chain. The exact numbers depend heavily on the vehicle and the duty cycle, but the shape is consistent. Charging from AC loses something in the region of five to twelve per cent in the on-board charger, cabling and battery. The battery loses a few per cent more on discharge. The inverter typically converts at 95 to 98 per cent efficiency averaged over a drive cycle, and the motor at 88 to 95 per cent. The single-speed gearbox loses another two or three per cent. Multiply these together and you find that something like 75 to 85 per cent of the energy drawn from the grid reaches the wheels as useful work.

Compare that with a petrol engine, whose tank-to-wheel efficiency in real driving is typically between 20 and 30 per cent, with the rest leaving as heat through the radiator and exhaust. That gap, roughly a factor of three, is the fundamental reason electric vehicles use so little energy per kilometre. It is also why an EV has so little waste heat available for warming the cabin, which becomes a real problem in winter.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.1 — The efficiency chain**

A car is charged at home from a 7.4 kW wallbox. Assume the following average efficiencies: on-board charger 93 %, battery charge 97 %, battery discharge 97 %, inverter 96 %, motor 92 %, reduction gear 97 %. What fraction of the energy taken from the grid reaches the wheels, and how much grid energy is needed to deliver 15 kWh of work at the wheels?

*Solution.* The efficiencies of stages in series multiply:

η = 0.93 × 0.97 × 0.97 × 0.96 × 0.92 × 0.97 = 0.774

So about 77 % of the grid energy arrives at the wheels. To deliver 15 kWh at the wheels:

E~grid~ = 15 / 0.774 ≈ 19.4 kWh

Notice that no single stage looks bad. Every one is above 90 %. Yet together they lose almost a quarter of the energy. When you are hunting for range, it is often more productive to gain a point or two across several stages than to chase a big improvement in one.
:::

Regenerative braking adds a twist. When the driver lifts off or presses the brake, the motor runs as a generator and pushes energy back along the chain into the battery. That energy suffers the same losses in reverse, so if you recover 10 kWh at the wheels you might get 8 kWh into the battery and 6.5 kWh back out to the wheels later. Even so, regeneration is the main reason EVs perform so well in stop-start town driving, where a combustion car simply throws its braking energy away as heat in the discs.

### Well-to-wheel

The grid-to-wheel picture leaves out how the electricity was generated. A fair comparison with petrol or diesel must also count the losses and emissions in producing the fuel, which engineers call well-to-tank, and the emissions from manufacturing the vehicle and especially the battery. Life-cycle studies consistently find that a BEV starts life with a larger carbon "debt" from battery production and pays it back over the first one to three years of use, depending on the electricity mix. On a grid with a high share of renewables and nuclear, the lifetime advantage is large. On a coal-heavy grid it is smaller but, in most published analyses, still positive. You do not need to become a life-cycle analyst to design an inverter, but you should know the shape of the argument, because customers and regulators will ask.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.2 — Running cost per kilometre**

A small family BEV uses 16 kWh per 100 km at the wheels-plus-losses level measured from the battery, and charging losses add a further 10 %. Electricity at home costs 25p per kWh. A comparable petrol car uses 6.0 litres per 100 km and petrol costs £1.40 per litre. Compare the energy cost of driving 100 km.

*Solution.* Energy drawn from the grid per 100 km is 16 × 1.10 = 17.6 kWh. At 25p per kWh, the cost is 17.6 × 0.25 = £4.40.

The petrol car uses 6.0 litres at £1.40, which costs £8.40.

The EV costs a little over half as much per kilometre in energy. If the owner charges overnight on a time-of-use tariff at, say, 8p per kWh, the cost falls to about £1.41 per 100 km. If they rely on public rapid chargers at 75p per kWh, it rises to £13.20, more than the petrol car. The engineering is identical in all three cases; the economics depend almost entirely on where the car is charged.
:::

## Voltage Levels and the High-Voltage Boundary

Engineers working on electric vehicles talk constantly about "HV" and "LV", and the boundary between them is not arbitrary. International vehicle regulations, such as UN Regulation No. 100, define high voltage in the vehicle context as any working voltage above 60 V DC or 30 V AC (rms). Above these levels, direct contact can drive a dangerous current through the human body, and the system must be designed with protection against electric shock: insulation, enclosures, interlocks, isolation monitoring and clear orange cabling.

Below the boundary sit the 12 V and 48 V systems. Forty-eight volts was chosen deliberately: allowing for charging voltages and transients, it stays under 60 V, so it can deliver four times the power of a 12 V system at the same current without triggering the full set of high-voltage safety requirements.

Above the boundary, traction systems cluster around two levels:

- **400 V class.** Nominal pack voltages of about 350 to 400 V, ranging from roughly 270 V when nearly empty to 420 V when full. This has been the mainstream for most of the past decade.
- **800 V class.** Nominal voltages of about 700 to 800 V, ranging up to around 900 V. Doubling the voltage halves the current for the same power, which reduces cable mass and resistive losses and, most importantly, makes very fast DC charging practical.

::: example
**Worked Example 1.3 — Why 800 volts?**

A car is charged at 250 kW. Compare the current and the resistive loss in a charging cable and inlet assembly with a total round-trip resistance of 4 mΩ, for a 400 V and an 800 V battery.

*Solution.* Current is I = P / V.

At 400 V: I = 250 000 / 400 = 625 A. Loss = I^2^R = 625^2^ × 0.004 = 1 563 W.

At 800 V: I = 250 000 / 800 = 312.5 A. Loss = 312.5^2^ × 0.004 = 391 W.

Halving the current cuts the resistive loss to a quarter. At 400 V, over 1.5 kW of heat must be removed from the cable and connector, which is why very high-power chargers on 400 V cars need liquid-cooled cables and still struggle to sustain peak power. At 800 V the same power is far easier to deliver. The price you pay is in the semiconductors, which must now block around 1 200 V instead of 650 V, and in the insulation and creepage distances throughout the system. We return to this trade in Chapters 10 and 13.
:::

## Requirements: From the Customer to the Component

Every engineering decision in an EV ultimately traces back to a requirement, and every requirement traces back to something a customer, a regulator or the business needs. Learning to walk that chain in both directions is one of the most valuable skills you can develop.

Take a deceptively simple customer statement: "The car should feel quick." Marketing turns this into a target, say 0–100 km/h in 6.5 seconds. The vehicle dynamics engineer turns that into a wheel torque and power profile. The drive-unit engineer turns it into a motor peak torque, a peak power, a gear ratio and a maximum speed. The inverter engineer turns it into a peak phase current. The battery engineer turns it into a peak discharge power at a given state of charge and temperature, and the cell engineer turns that into a maximum discharge current per cell and an acceptable voltage drop. At each step there are choices, and each choice pushes on other requirements. A higher gear ratio helps acceleration but raises motor speed on the motorway. A bigger battery helps range and power but adds cost and mass, which hurts acceleration and range.

The industry formalises this cascade in the V-model, shown in Figure 1.4. Requirements flow down the left side, from vehicle to system to component. Design and build happen at the bottom. Verification and validation flow up the right side, testing each level against the requirements written for it.

![**Figure 1.4** The V-model of automotive development. Each requirement on the left has a matching test on the right. Problems found high on the right-hand side, in vehicle testing, are the most expensive to fix.](figures/fig1_4.png)

The V-model is sometimes criticised as slow and document-heavy, and modern teams wrap it in agile practices, especially for software. But its central insight is sound: a requirement you cannot test is not a requirement, and a test with no requirement behind it is a guess.

### Attributes and their tensions

It helps to group vehicle requirements into a handful of attributes. For an EV the important ones are:

- **Range and efficiency.** How far the car goes on a charge, in standard tests and in real use, including cold weather.
- **Performance.** Acceleration, top speed, sustained power on long climbs or at high speed, and towing.
- **Charging.** Peak and average DC charging power, time from 10 to 80 per cent state of charge, AC charging rate, and behaviour at low temperature.
- **Durability.** Capacity retention after a given number of years and kilometres, typically guaranteed by a battery warranty of eight years.
- **Safety.** Electrical, functional and crash safety, including thermal runaway behaviour.
- **Cost.** Bill of materials, manufacturing cost and warranty cost.
- **Refinement.** Noise, vibration and harshness (NVH), smoothness of torque, and the absence of the whines and whistles electric drives are prone to.

These attributes pull against each other. Fast charging stresses cells and threatens durability. High performance needs bigger semiconductors and more cooling, which costs money. Cheaper cell chemistries may reduce energy density and therefore range. Much of the art of EV engineering is in finding the balance that suits a particular product, and being honest about what you gave up.

## A Method for Solving Problems

I want to finish this chapter by setting out the approach to problem-solving used throughout this book. It is not original. Good engineers have worked this way for a very long time. But it is worth stating plainly, because under deadline pressure it is the first thing people forget.

1. **State the problem in numbers.** "Range is poor" is a complaint. "Range at −5 °C on our commuting route is 210 km against a target of 260 km" is a problem. Until you can write the gap down with units, you do not understand it well enough to fix it.
2. **Draw the system boundary.** Decide which parts of the chain could plausibly be involved, using a diagram like Figure 1.2. Be generous at first; you can narrow it later.
3. **Build the simplest model that could explain the gap.** Often this is a single equation or a short energy balance. A model that is roughly right and quick to evaluate beats an elaborate one that takes a week to set up.
4. **Estimate, then measure.** Use the model to predict what you should see. Then collect data, from logs, bench tests or the vehicle, and compare. The size and shape of the disagreement is usually more informative than the agreement.
5. **Find the dominant term.** In almost every real problem, one or two effects account for most of the gap. Fix those first. Resist the temptation to polish the small terms because they are easier.
6. **Check the fix against the other attributes.** Every change pushes on something else. A fix that restores range by cutting cabin heating will generate a different complaint.
7. **Write it down.** Record what you found and why. The next person to meet the problem, quite possibly you in two years' time, will thank you.

The rest of the book will give you the physics and the models for step 3 and the measurement knowledge for step 4. The judgement in steps 5 and 6 comes with practice. Every chapter therefore contains at least one *Case File*: a realistic problem, worked through in this order.

::: casefile
**Case File 1 — The van that lost its range in winter**

*The complaint.* A delivery company running a fleet of electric vans reports that in January the vans struggle to complete their routes. In summer, the same routes leave 35 % charge in reserve. In January, several vans return with less than 5 %.

*State it in numbers.* The route is 140 km with 120 stops. Summer consumption from the logs: 24 kWh per 100 km. January consumption: 36 kWh per 100 km, a 50 % increase. Usable battery energy: 52 kWh when warm. The vans are parked outside overnight at around −2 °C.

*Draw the boundary.* Candidates include the battery (reduced capacity and higher resistance in the cold), the cabin heater, battery heating, tyre and air resistance (cold, dense air and winter tyres), and driver behaviour (doors open at every stop).

*Simplest model.* Extra energy per route = 140 × (0.36 − 0.24) = 16.8 kWh. Where could 16.8 kWh go? A resistive (PTC) cabin heater rated at 5 kW, run continuously over an eight-hour shift at an average of 2 kW because the door is constantly open, uses 16 kWh. That alone is almost the whole gap.

*Measure.* The fleet's telematics confirm heater power averaging 1.9 kW across the shift. Battery heating at start-up adds about 1 kWh. Cold-weather rolling and aerodynamic losses account for most of the remainder.

*Dominant term.* Cabin heating. Battery capacity loss in the cold is real but smaller than the drivers believed.

*Fix and cross-check.* The company introduces pre-conditioning: the vans heat their cabins and batteries while still plugged in at the depot, drawing energy from the grid rather than the battery. They also fit heated seats and steering wheels for the drivers, which provide comfort at a few hundred watts rather than several kilowatts. Consumption falls to about 30 kWh per 100 km. On the next vehicle order, they specify a heat pump. Nobody had to change the battery.

*Lesson.* The instinct was to blame the battery because it is the component everyone has read about. The energy balance pointed at the heater within ten minutes.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Electric cars predate petrol cars commercially; they lost a century ago because of energy storage and cost, and they returned when lithium-ion cells made storage cheap and dense.
- Electrified vehicles form a spectrum: mild hybrid, full hybrid, plug-in hybrid, range extender, battery electric and fuel cell. They differ mainly in battery size, voltage and where the energy comes from.
- A battery electric powertrain consists of the battery and its management system, contactors and distribution, the traction inverter, the motor, a single-speed reduction gear, the on-board charger, the DC-DC converter, a thermal system and a vehicle control unit.
- Roughly three-quarters or more of grid energy reaches the wheels in a BEV, compared with about a quarter of fuel energy in a petrol car. Many small losses in series add up.
- High voltage in vehicles means above 60 V DC or 30 V AC. Traction systems cluster at 400 V and 800 V; higher voltage reduces current and resistive losses, especially during fast charging.
- Requirements cascade from customer to component, and verification climbs back up. Vehicle attributes such as range, performance, charging, durability, safety and cost pull against each other.
- Solve problems by stating the gap in numbers, drawing the boundary, building a simple model, measuring, finding the dominant term, cross-checking and writing it down.
:::

### Problems

1. List the main differences between a series hybrid and a parallel hybrid. Explain why a series arrangement is attractive for a city bus but less attractive for a car used mainly on motorways.
2. A BEV has the following average efficiencies: charger 94 %, battery round trip 95 %, inverter 97 %, motor 93 %, gear 98 %. Calculate the grid-to-wheel efficiency. If the car needs 13 kWh at the wheels per 100 km, how much grid energy does it use per 100 km?
3. A car accepts 150 kW at a pack voltage of 380 V. Find the charging current. If the battery were redesigned with twice as many cells in series (and half as many in parallel) at the same total energy, what would the current be at the same power, and by what factor would resistive losses in a fixed cable fall?
4. Explain why a 48 V mild hybrid system does not need the full set of high-voltage protective measures, while a 120 V system would.
5. A plug-in hybrid has a 14 kWh usable battery and an electric consumption of 20 kWh per 100 km. Its owner commutes 30 km each way, five days a week, and charges only at home. Estimate the share of weekday driving done on electricity. What changes if the owner can also charge at work?

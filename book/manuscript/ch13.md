# Charging Infrastructure and Standards

::: chapter-intro
An electric vehicle is only as useful as the network that refuels it. This chapter explains how vehicles are charged: the modes and power levels, the connectors used around the world, the simple analogue signalling that governs AC charging, the digital conversation behind DC fast charging, and the architecture of the charging stations themselves. It then looks at the grid connection, at depot and fleet charging, and at wireless charging, battery swapping and megawatt charging for heavy trucks.
:::

For many drivers, the first real encounter with electric vehicle engineering is not the motor or the battery but a charging cable that will not start a session. Charging failures are one of the most common sources of complaint from EV owners, and they are often blamed on the car, the charger or "the network" without anyone quite knowing which. In truth, a charging session is a conversation between at least two complex systems built by different companies, governed by standards that have evolved quickly and been interpreted in slightly different ways. When the conversation works, it is effortless. When it does not, finding out why requires understanding every layer.

This chapter gives you those layers. I have tried to keep it practical: the details of signal voltages and message sequences matter, because they are what you see on an oscilloscope or in a log file when you are trying to work out why a car and a charger will not talk to each other.

## Charging Modes and Power Levels

The international standard IEC 61851 defines four *charging modes*, which describe how the vehicle connects to the supply and what protection is provided:

- **Mode 1:** connection to an ordinary socket with no special protection or communication. Not permitted for cars in many countries and rarely used.
- **Mode 2:** connection to an ordinary domestic or industrial socket through a cable with an *in-cable control and protection device* (IC-CPD), the box on the "granny cable" supplied with many cars. Typically 2.3 kW in the UK (10 A), up to around 3.7 kW.
- **Mode 3:** connection to dedicated AC charging equipment, a wallbox or public post, with control pilot communication and protection built in. This is the normal way to charge on AC, at 3.7 to 22 kW.
- **Mode 4:** DC charging, where the conversion from AC to DC happens in the charging station and DC flows directly to the battery.

In everyday language, people talk instead about power levels. Table 13.1 summarises them.

Table: **Table 13.1** Typical charging power levels and their uses.

| Category | Typical power | Supply | Where used | Approx. range added per hour* |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Domestic socket (Mode 2) | 2.3 kW | 1-phase AC, 10 A | Occasional, overnight | 13 km |
| Home / workplace wallbox | 7.4 kW | 1-phase AC, 32 A | Overnight, workplace | 40 km |
| Three-phase AC | 11–22 kW | 3-phase AC | Workplace, destination, depots | 60–120 km |
| DC rapid | 50 kW | DC | Older public sites | 280 km |
| DC high-power | 150–400 kW | DC | Motorway hubs | 800–2 000 km (limited by the car) |
| Megawatt (MCS) | up to 3.75 MW | DC | Heavy trucks | – |

*Assuming 18 kWh per 100 km and the car accepting the full power, which at high power it rarely does for long.

Two points are worth stressing. First, with AC charging the power conversion happens in the car's on-board charger, so the charging rate is the *lower* of what the wallbox offers and what the OBC can accept. A car with a 7.4 kW single-phase OBC charges at 7.4 kW even on a 22 kW three-phase post. Second, with DC charging the car's battery management system sets the current, so the rate is the lower of what the charger can deliver and what the battery can accept at its present state of charge and temperature, as Chapter 6 explained. A 350 kW charger does not make a car charge at 350 kW.

## Connectors Around the World

Charging connectors grew up regionally, and the result is a small zoo. Table 13.2 lists the main types.

Table: **Table 13.2** Principal charging connector standards.

| Connector | Standard | Main regions | AC / DC | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Type 1 | SAE J1772, IEC 62196-2 | North America (legacy), Japan | AC, single-phase | Five pins |
| Type 2 | IEC 62196-2 | Europe, UK, Australia, many others | AC, single- and three-phase | Seven pins; mandated in EU |
| CCS1 / CCS2 (Combo) | IEC 62196-3, SAE J1772 | North America / Europe | AC + DC | Type 1 or Type 2 plus two DC pins |
| CHAdeMO | IEC 62196-3, JEVS G105 | Japan; declining elsewhere | DC | CAN-based communication |
| GB/T | GB/T 20234 | China | Separate AC and DC connectors | CAN-based for DC |
| NACS / SAE J3400 | SAE J3400 | North America | AC + DC through same pins | Compact; adopted widely in North America from the mid-2020s |
| ChaoJi | CHAdeMO 3.0 / GB/T | China, Japan (emerging) | DC | Designed for very high power |
| MCS | SAE J3271, IEC (in development) | Global, heavy vehicles | DC | Up to 1 250 V and 3 000 A |

In Europe and the UK, the combination of Type 2 for AC and CCS2 for DC is effectively universal for new cars. In North America, the market has moved from CCS1 towards the SAE J3400 connector, originally developed by one manufacturer and later standardised, with adapters bridging the transition. China uses GB/T, with ChaoJi developed as its high-power successor.

For the engineer, the connector matters less than the communication protocol behind it. CCS and J3400 both use the same underlying signalling for AC and the same digital protocols for DC, so the vehicle's charging controller is largely the same whichever connector is fitted.

## AC Charging: The Control Pilot

Mode 3 AC charging, used with Type 1, Type 2 and J3400 connectors, relies on a beautifully simple analogue signalling scheme defined in IEC 61851-1 and SAE J1772. Two signal pins do the work.

The **proximity pilot** (PP) tells the vehicle that a connector is inserted and, on Type 2, encodes the current rating of the cable through a resistor between PP and protective earth: for example, 680 Ω for a 20 A cable, 220 Ω for 32 A and 100 Ω for 63 A. The vehicle uses this to avoid drawing more current than the cable can carry, and to lock the connector.

The **control pilot** (CP) carries a signal from the charging station. The station drives CP through a 1 kΩ resistor with ±12 V, and the vehicle connects resistors between CP and earth through a diode, pulling the positive voltage down to signal its state. Figure 13.2 shows the arrangement.

![**Figure 13.2** Control pilot signalling. (a) Simplified circuit: the station sources ±12 V through 1 kΩ; the vehicle presents 2.74 kΩ when connected and switches in an additional 1.3 kΩ when ready to charge. (b) The positive level of the CP signal identifies the state; the PWM duty cycle tells the vehicle how much current it may draw.](figures/fig13_2.png)

The positive level of the CP voltage defines the *state*:

- **State A, +12 V:** no vehicle connected.
- **State B, +9 V:** vehicle connected, not ready to charge.
- **State C, +6 V:** vehicle connected and requesting charge.
- **State D, +3 V:** vehicle requesting charge with ventilation required (historic, for lead-acid batteries that emitted hydrogen).
- **State E or F, 0 V or −12 V:** error or station unavailable.

When the station is ready to supply power, it switches the CP signal from steady +12 V to a 1 kHz square wave. The *duty cycle* of that square wave tells the vehicle the maximum current it may draw. Between 10 % and 85 % duty, the relationship is:

$$ I_{max} = 0.6 \times D \quad (\mathrm{A, with}\ D\ \mathrm{in}\ \%) $$ {13.1}

and between 85 % and 96 %, *I*~max~ = 2.5 × (*D* − 64). A duty cycle of 5 % has a special meaning: it tells the vehicle to start digital communication, which is used for DC charging and for advanced AC features such as Plug & Charge.

::: example
**Worked Example 13.1 — Reading the control pilot**

A technician measures the control pilot of a wallbox with an oscilloscope while a car is charging. The signal is a 1 kHz square wave swinging between +6 V and −12 V, with a duty cycle of 50 %. What state is the session in, and what current is the car permitted to draw? What would duty cycles of 26.7 % and 90 % mean?

*Solution.* The positive level of +6 V indicates state C: vehicle connected and charging.

Permitted current: *I*~max~ = 0.6 × 50 = 30 A.

At 26.7 %: 0.6 × 26.7 = 16 A, typical of a three-phase 11 kW wallbox (16 A per phase).

At 90 %: 2.5 × (90 − 64) = 65 A, used for higher-current single-phase supplies in North America.

This is why the oscilloscope is the first tool to reach for when an AC charging session fails. If the positive level never drops from +9 V to +6 V, the car is not requesting charge. If the square wave never appears, the station is not offering it. If the duty cycle is lower than expected, the station or its load management is limiting the current.
:::

The wallbox itself provides protection: an overcurrent device, and residual-current protection, which, as Case File 12 showed, must detect smooth DC residual current of 6 mA or more because a fault inside the vehicle's charger can produce it. In the UK and elsewhere, wallboxes must also protect against the loss of the supply's neutral or earth connection on certain types of supply, usually by monitoring the supply voltage and disconnecting if it goes outside safe limits.

## DC Charging: The Digital Conversation

DC charging is far more demanding. The station delivers hundreds of amps directly to the battery, and the vehicle must control that current precisely according to its state. The two sides therefore hold a continuous digital conversation.

For CCS and J3400, that conversation runs over the same control pilot wire, using *power-line communication* (PLC): a HomePlug Green PHY signal at 2 to 30 MHz superimposed on the 1 kHz pilot. Before they can talk, the two sides must find each other, a process called *SLAC* (Signal Level Attenuation Characterisation), in which the vehicle measures signal strength to make sure it is talking to the charger it is plugged into and not a neighbour on the next bay. Then they exchange messages defined by DIN SPEC 70121 or, for newer systems, ISO 15118-2 and ISO 15118-20. CHAdeMO and GB/T use CAN bus over dedicated pins instead.

### A typical DC session

A DC charging session proceeds through a series of steps, shown in Figure 13.3:

1. **Plug-in and wake-up.** The vehicle detects the connector through the proximity pin, locks it and wakes its charging controller.
2. **Communication set-up.** The station sets the pilot to 5 % duty; the two sides perform SLAC, obtain network addresses and agree a protocol version.
3. **Authorisation.** Either the driver presents a card or app, or, with *Plug & Charge* under ISO 15118, the vehicle presents a digital certificate and the station checks it against the payment provider automatically.
4. **Parameter exchange.** The vehicle reports its maximum voltage, maximum current, and battery details; the station reports its voltage and current capabilities.
5. **Cable check.** The station applies voltage to the cable and connector, with the vehicle's contactors still open, and checks insulation resistance. This protects against a damaged cable before power flows.
6. **Pre-charge.** The station raises its output voltage to match the battery voltage reported by the vehicle, so that when the vehicle closes its DC contactors no large current surge flows.
7. **Energy transfer.** The vehicle closes its contactors and then sends a *current request* several times a second. The station follows that request within its limits. As the battery fills or warms, the BMS lowers its request, which is how the charging curve of Figure 6.5 comes about.
8. **Shutdown.** When the target SoC is reached or the driver stops the session, the vehicle reduces its current request to zero, the station ramps down, the vehicle opens its contactors, the station verifies the output voltage has fallen below a safe level, and the connector unlocks.

![**Figure 13.3** A DC fast-charging session, showing the sequence of phases and the station output current and voltage during the session. The vehicle controls the current throughout; the station follows its requests within its own limits.](figures/fig13_3.png)

Every one of these steps has timeouts and error conditions, and different manufacturers' interpretations of them are the main cause of interoperability problems. The industry runs regular interoperability test events, at which vehicle and charger makers connect their products and resolve mismatches, and conformance test systems now check each side against the standard.

### Plug & Charge and the back end

*Plug & Charge*, defined in ISO 15118, lets the driver simply plug in: identification, authorisation and billing all happen automatically through a public-key infrastructure of digital certificates installed in the car. It removes the need for apps and cards and is far more convenient, but it requires a secure certificate ecosystem shared by vehicle makers, charge-point operators and payment providers.

Behind the station, the *Open Charge Point Protocol* (OCPP) connects it to the operator's back-office system for monitoring, remote control, pricing, firmware updates and smart charging commands. ISO 15118-20 also defines the messages needed for bidirectional power transfer, which is the foundation for DC vehicle-to-grid.

## Inside a DC Charging Station

A high-power DC station is a sizeable piece of power electronics, often split into a *power cabinet* and one or more *dispensers* at the parking bays.

The power cabinet takes a three-phase supply, typically at 400 V for smaller units or from a dedicated transformer at medium voltage for larger sites. Inside are stacks of *power modules*, typically 20 to 50 kW each, each containing an active PFC rectifier and an isolated DC-DC converter, much like a large OBC. The modules can be combined in parallel to deliver high current, and their outputs can be switched between dispensers, so that a site with 600 kW of modules can give one car 350 kW while another receives 250 kW, or share it among four cars. This *dynamic power sharing* lets operators match installed capacity to real demand.

The output must cover a wide voltage range, typically 200 to 1 000 V, to serve both 400 V and 800 V cars. The current is usually limited by the cable and connector, commonly 200 A for uncooled cables and 400 to 600 A for *liquid-cooled cables*, which have coolant flowing through them and temperature sensors in the connector pins.

That current limit has an important consequence. A 400 V car cannot draw 350 kW from a "350 kW" charger, because at 400 V that would require 875 A. The charger's power rating is usually only achievable at the upper end of its voltage range.

::: example
**Worked Example 13.2 — The 400 V car on the 350 kW charger**

A station is rated at 350 kW and 500 A, with an output range of 200 to 1 000 V. What maximum power can it deliver to (a) a 400 V-class car whose battery is at 380 V during the early part of the charge, and (b) an 800 V-class car at 750 V?

*Solution.* (a) Current limit 500 A × 380 V = 190 kW. The car may request less if its own limits are lower.

(b) At 750 V, the current needed for 350 kW is 467 A, within the 500 A limit, so the full 350 kW is available.

This is the main practical reason why 800 V architectures charge faster on today's infrastructure. It is also why the battery voltage rising during the charge can help: a 400 V-class car whose battery rises to 420 V near 80 % SoC could draw 210 kW on the current limit, although by then its BMS will usually be requesting much less.
:::

::: problem
**Field Problem — "The charger says 350 kW but I only got 120 kW"**

*Symptom.* A driver complains that a high-power charger delivered far less than its advertised power.

*Likely causes.* (1) The car's own maximum DC power is lower than the charger's rating. (2) A 400 V-class car limited by the charger's current rating. (3) The battery was cold, or already at a high SoC, so the BMS requested less. (4) The station shares power between dispensers, and another car was charging. (5) The station had derated because of high temperature in its cabinet or cable. (6) The site's grid connection or load management limited output.

*How to tell them apart.* Check the car's displayed battery temperature and SoC at the start. Look at the charging curve: a car-limited curve follows the shape of Figure 6.5, while a station-limited curve is often flat at a level below the car's capability. Station logs, if available, record their own limits. Precondition the battery and retry at a different dispenser, or at a quiet time, to separate the causes.
:::

## The Grid Connection

Every charger connects to the electricity network, and at scale that connection becomes a major part of the engineering and cost.

A single 7.4 kW home charger draws as much current as an electric shower, but for hours rather than minutes. Across a street of houses, if every car charged at 6 pm, the local transformer could be overloaded. *Smart charging*, which shifts charging to off-peak times or modulates it in response to signals from the network, is therefore increasingly expected. In Great Britain, regulations require new home chargers to be capable of smart operation and to include a randomised delay at the start of scheduled charging, so that thousands of cars do not all start at exactly midnight.

For rapid charging hubs and fleet depots, the grid connection is often the critical path and the largest single cost. A motorway hub with ten 350 kW chargers would need 3.5 MW if all ran at full power simultaneously, a connection more typical of a factory than a service station. Operators use *diversity*, the statistical fact that not all chargers run at full power at the same time, together with dynamic power sharing, on-site *battery storage* that charges slowly from the grid and discharges quickly into cars, and sometimes local solar generation.

::: example
**Worked Example 13.3 — Sizing a depot connection**

A delivery company plans to charge 40 electric vans overnight. Each returns needing about 60 kWh, and they are parked from 8 pm to 6 am. The chargers are 92 % efficient overall. What average power does the depot need, and what would the peak be if every van started charging at full rate on 22 kW chargers as it arrived?

*Solution.* Energy from the grid = 40 × 60 / 0.92 = 2 609 kWh.

Spread over 10 hours: an average of about 261 kW.

If every van plugged into a 22 kW charger at once: 40 × 22 = 880 kW.

A smart-charging system that schedules and throttles charging so the total stays near 280 to 300 kW meets the fleet's needs with a connection about a third of the uncontrolled peak, saving a great deal in connection cost and, often, in network capacity charges. The key requirement is reliable information about each van's state of charge and departure time.
:::

## Other Ways to Charge

### Wireless charging

*Inductive* or *wireless* charging transfers power across an air gap between a coil in the ground and a coil on the underside of the car, using magnetic resonance at around 85 kHz. SAE J2954 defines power classes up to 11 kW for passenger cars, with efficiencies from grid to battery around 90 % when well aligned. The appeal is convenience: no cable to plug in, which suits automated parking, taxis and, eventually, autonomous vehicles. The challenges are cost, alignment tolerance, foreign-object detection (a coin or a can between the pads can overheat), protection of people and animals from stray magnetic fields, and electromagnetic compatibility.

*Dynamic* wireless charging, with coils embedded in the road to charge vehicles on the move, has been demonstrated on test tracks and short public road sections. Its economics depend on very heavy usage, such as bus routes or freight corridors.

### Battery swapping

Battery swapping replaces a depleted pack with a charged one at a station in a few minutes. It has been commercialised at scale in China for some car brands and for two-wheelers and taxis in several countries. It requires standardised or brand-specific pack designs and a significant stock of spare batteries, and it moves battery ownership from the driver to the operator, which can be attractive for leasing and second-life management.

### Megawatt charging for heavy vehicles

Heavy trucks need far more energy: a long-haul tractor unit might carry 500 to 1 000 kWh. To recharge a meaningful amount during a driver's mandatory rest break, typically 45 minutes in Europe, chargers must deliver around a megawatt. The *Megawatt Charging System* (MCS) defines a connector and protocol for up to 1 250 V and 3 000 A, a theoretical maximum of 3.75 MW, using ISO 15118-20 communication over Ethernet rather than PLC. Megawatt charging pushes cable cooling, connector design and grid connections into new territory, and early deployments are concentrated along major freight corridors.

::: casefile
**Case File 13 — Sessions that would not start**

*The complaint.* Owners of a newly launched car report that DC charging sessions frequently fail to start at chargers from one particular manufacturer, while working normally at others. The failure rate on the affected chargers is about 30 %; on others, under 2 %.

*State it in numbers.* Station logs from the operator show the failures occur during communication set-up, specifically a timeout waiting for the vehicle's response during SLAC, about 3 to 5 seconds after the connector is inserted.

*Draw the boundary.* The vehicle's charging communication controller (its wake-up time, its PLC modem and its SLAC implementation), the station's PLC modem and timing, and the cable and pilot circuit, which carry the PLC signal.

*Simplest model.* A timeout at SLAC means one side is not answering in time. If the vehicle's charging controller wakes from sleep slowly, it may not be ready when the station begins. Stations from different manufacturers apply the timing limits in the standards with different margins, which would explain why only one brand is affected.

*Measure.* Bench tests with a charging simulator show that the vehicle's controller takes 2.8 to 3.6 seconds from plug-in to being ready for SLAC, because it waits for the gateway to authorise network wake-up before starting its modem. The affected station brand begins SLAC 2.5 seconds after plug-in and allows only a short window for the first response before declaring failure. Other brands start later or retry.

*Dominant term.* Vehicle wake-up latency combined with the station's tight timing.

*Fix and cross-check.* The vehicle maker changes the wake-up sequence so that the charging controller starts its PLC modem immediately on detecting the proximity signal, cutting its readiness time to under 1.5 seconds. The station maker, after discussions at an interoperability event, adds a retry of the SLAC process before declaring failure. Failure rates fall below 1 % at all stations. The vehicle team checks that early modem start-up does not raise the car's quiescent current when a cable is left plugged into an inactive post for long periods.

*Lesson.* Charging is a two-party conversation, and timing margins on both sides matter. "It works on every other charger" does not prove the charger is at fault, nor that the car is.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- IEC 61851 defines charging modes 1 to 4; practical power levels range from 2.3 kW domestic sockets to 400 kW public DC and multi-megawatt truck charging.
- AC charging rate is limited by the on-board charger; DC charging rate by the battery's requests and the station's voltage and current limits.
- Connector standards vary by region (Type 1/2, CCS1/2, CHAdeMO, GB/T, SAE J3400, ChaoJi, MCS), but the underlying protocols are largely shared.
- AC charging uses the proximity pilot to encode cable rating and the control pilot's voltage levels and PWM duty cycle to signal state and permitted current.
- DC charging uses digital communication (PLC with DIN 70121 or ISO 15118 for CCS and J3400; CAN for CHAdeMO and GB/T) through a sequence of set-up, cable check, pre-charge, energy transfer and shutdown, with the vehicle controlling current.
- Station power modules share power between dispensers; current limits mean 400 V cars cannot use the full power of high-power chargers.
- Grid connections drive cost at scale; smart charging, diversity and on-site storage reduce the required capacity.
- Wireless charging, battery swapping and megawatt charging address specific use cases.
:::

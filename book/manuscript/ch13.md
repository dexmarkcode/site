Sit in a quiet room and listen to two people talking at once. Your ear receives a single, complicated pressure wave, yet your brain can usually pick out each voice. It can do this because, for sound at ordinary levels, air is a *linear* medium: the wave produced by two voices together is simply the sum of the waves each would produce alone. Neither voice distorts the other; they just add.

Circuits made of resistors and sources behave in exactly the same way. If a circuit contains several sources, the current in any resistor is the sum of the currents that each source would produce if it were acting alone. This is the **superposition theorem**, and it rests on a property called **linearity** that we have been quietly relying on since Chapter 3.

Superposition is not always the quickest way to solve a circuit – nodal and mesh analysis usually win when there are many sources. But it is a superb way to *understand* a circuit, because it shows how much each source contributes to each result. It also explains how audio mixers combine signals, how amplifiers handle a steady bias and a varying signal at the same time, and why some quantities, notably power, refuse to add up in the way you might expect.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- define linearity and explain the properties of homogeneity and additivity;
- use proportionality to scale the results of a linear circuit;
- state the superposition theorem and the conditions under which it applies;
- "switch off" independent voltage and current sources correctly;
- use superposition to find voltages and currents in circuits with several sources;
- explain why power cannot be found by superposition;
- apply superposition correctly when dependent sources are present.
:::

## 13.1 What Makes a Circuit Linear?

A component is **linear** if its voltage and current are related by a straight line through the origin. A resistor obeying Ohm's law is linear: V = IR. A filament lamp or a diode is not (Chapter 3).

A **linear circuit** is one built entirely from linear components and independent sources (and, as we shall see, linear dependent sources). Such a circuit has two important properties.

**Homogeneity (proportionality).** If every independent source in the circuit is multiplied by the same factor k, every voltage and current in the circuit is multiplied by k. Double the battery voltage and every current doubles.

**Additivity.** The response to several sources acting together is the sum of the responses to each source acting separately.

Together these two properties define linearity. Additivity is the superposition theorem. Homogeneity we have already used, in Chapter 7, when we found the output of a ladder by assuming a 1 V output and scaling.

![Figure 13.1 — In a linear circuit, an output is directly proportional to the source: doubling the input doubles the output. Points on the line show the ladder of Chapter 7, where V~B~ = V~S~/2.](fig/ch13_linear.svg){width=68%}

::: example
**Worked Example 13.1 — Scaling a known result**

In Worked Example 7.4, a 30 V source applied to a ladder gave V~B~ = 15 V and a source current of 1 A. Without re-solving the circuit, find V~B~ and the source current when the source is (a) 12 V, (b) −9 V.

**(a)** The source is multiplied by 12/30 = 0.4. Every response scales by 0.4: V~B~ = 6 V, I = 0.4 A.

**(b)** The factor is −9/30 = −0.3: V~B~ = −4.5 V, I = −0.3 A. The negative signs simply mean that the voltage and current have reversed.
:::

That is linearity at its most useful. Once you have solved a linear circuit for one source value, you have solved it for every source value.

## 13.2 The Superposition Theorem

::: keyeq
**The superposition theorem**

In a linear circuit containing several independent sources, the voltage across (or current through) any element equals the algebraic sum of the voltages (or currents) produced by each independent source acting alone, with all other independent sources switched off.
:::

### Switching sources off

"Switching off" a source means setting its value to zero, and it is important to be precise about what that means for each type.

- A **voltage source set to zero** keeps the voltage between its terminals at 0 V, whatever current flows. That is exactly what a plain wire does. So a voltage source is switched off by replacing it with a **short circuit**.
- A **current source set to zero** passes 0 A, whatever voltage appears across it. That is exactly what a gap does. So a current source is switched off by replacing it with an **open circuit**.

![Figure 13.2 — Switching off sources. An ideal voltage source becomes a short circuit; an ideal current source becomes an open circuit.](fig/ch13_switchoff.svg){width=78%}

Two further points complete the rules. If a real source has **internal resistance**, only the ideal source is switched off; its internal resistance stays in the circuit. And **dependent sources are never switched off** – a point we return to in Section 13.5.

### The procedure

1. Choose one independent source to keep. Switch off all the others.
2. Solve the resulting single-source circuit for the quantity you need, using any method (often simple series–parallel reduction and the divider rules).
3. Repeat for each independent source in turn.
4. Add the individual results algebraically, paying careful attention to direction or polarity.

Step 4 is where errors creep in. Choose a reference direction for your answer at the start, and for each partial result decide whether it is in that direction (positive) or the opposite (negative).

## 13.3 Superposition in Action

Let us return, one last time, to our two-battery circuit. We have solved it with branch currents, nodal analysis and mesh analysis. Superposition gives us a fourth view, and this time it tells us something new: how much of the voltage at A comes from each battery.

![Figure 13.3 — Superposition applied to the two-battery circuit. (a) The original. (b) The 5 V source replaced by a short. (c) The 10 V source replaced by a short.](fig/ch13_superpos.svg){width=100%}

::: example
**Worked Example 13.2 — Each battery's share**

Use superposition to find V~A~ in Figure 13.3(a).

**Step 1: 10 V source alone** (Figure 13.3(b)). With the 5 V source shorted, the 1 Ω resistor runs from A to the bottom rail, in parallel with the 4 Ω resistor: 1 ∥ 4 = 0.8 Ω. This forms a divider with the 2 Ω:

V~A~′ = 10 × {{frac|0.8|2 + 0.8}} = 2.857 V

**Step 2: 5 V source alone** (Figure 13.3(c)). With the 10 V source shorted, the 2 Ω resistor runs from A to the bottom rail, in parallel with the 4 Ω: 2 ∥ 4 = 1.333 Ω. This forms a divider with the 1 Ω:

V~A~″ = 5 × {{frac|1.333|1 + 1.333}} = 2.857 V

**Step 3: Add.** V~A~ = 2.857 + 2.857 = **5.714 V**, agreeing with all our earlier results.

**Insight.** The two batteries contribute *exactly equally* to the voltage at A, even though one is twice the voltage of the other. The 5 V battery is connected through a smaller resistance (1 Ω rather than 2 Ω), which exactly compensates. No other method makes this so clear.
:::

### A voltage source and a current source together

![Figure 13.4 — (a) A circuit with a voltage source and a current source. (b) The current source opened. (c) The voltage source shorted.](fig/ch13_mixed.svg){width=100%}

::: example
**Worked Example 13.3 — Mixed sources**

In Figure 13.4(a), find V~A~ and the current in each resistor.

**Step 1: 24 V source alone** (Figure 13.4(b)). The current source is replaced by an open circuit, so it simply disappears. The 6 Ω and 3 Ω form a divider:

V~A~′ = 24 × {{frac|3|6 + 3}} = 8 V

**Step 2: 3 A source alone** (Figure 13.4(c)). The voltage source is replaced by a short, so the 6 Ω now runs from A to the bottom rail, in parallel with the 3 Ω: 6 ∥ 3 = 2 Ω. All 3 A flows into this combination:

V~A~″ = 3 × 2 = 6 V

**Step 3: Add.** V~A~ = 8 + 6 = **14 V**.

**Step 4: Currents.** Through the 3 Ω (downwards): 14/3 = 4.667 A. Through the 6 Ω (from the source towards A): (24 − 14)/6 = 1.667 A. Check KCL at A: 1.667 + 3 = 4.667 ✓.

**Alternatively** we could find each current by superposition directly. Through the 3 Ω: 8/3 + 6/3 = 2.667 + 2 = 4.667 A ✓. Through the 6 Ω, towards A: from the 24 V source alone, 24/9 = 2.667 A towards A; from the 3 A source alone, 6/6 = 1 A *away* from A (towards the shorted source). Net: 2.667 − 1 = 1.667 A towards A ✓. Notice the sign: the two contributions flow in opposite directions and partly cancel.
:::

## 13.4 Power Does Not Superpose

Here is a trap that catches many students, and some engineers. Superposition works for voltages and currents. It does **not** work for power.

The reason is mathematical. Power in a resistor is P = I²R, which is not a linear function of current. If a current is the sum of two parts, I = I′ + I″, then

::: eq
P = (I′ + I″)²R = I′²R + I″²R + 2I′I″R
:::

The first two terms are the powers that each source would produce alone. The third term, 2I′I″R, is a "cross term" that appears only when both sources act together. Adding the separate powers misses it entirely.

::: example
**Worked Example 13.4 — The wrong way to find power**

In Worked Example 13.3, find the power in the 3 Ω resistor (a) correctly and (b) by wrongly adding the powers from each source alone.

**(a) Correctly.** V = 14 V, so P = 14²/3 = **65.3 W**.

**(b) Wrongly.** With the 24 V source alone, V′ = 8 V and P′ = 64/3 = 21.3 W. With the 3 A source alone, V″ = 6 V and P″ = 36/3 = 12 W. Sum: 33.3 W.

The "superposed" answer is barely half the true power. The missing 32 W is the cross term: 2 × (8/3) × 2 × 3 = 32 W.
:::

The rule is simple: **use superposition to find voltages and currents; then calculate power from the total voltage or current.**

## 13.5 Superposition With Dependent Sources

When a circuit contains dependent sources, superposition still works, provided you remember one rule: **dependent sources stay in the circuit for every partial calculation.** Only the *independent* sources are switched on and off one at a time.

Why? A dependent source is part of the model of a device – a transistor, say. Switching it off would be like removing the transistor from the circuit, which would change the circuit itself, not just the excitation. Its value will be different in each partial circuit, because its controlling variable will be different, and that is exactly as it should be.

::: example
**Worked Example 13.5 — Superposition with a dependent source**

A node A is fed from a 10 V source through 2 Ω and receives 2 A from a current source. It is connected to the reference through 4 Ω, and a dependent current source draws 0.5V~A~ amps from A to the reference. Find V~A~ by superposition.

**Step 1: 10 V source alone** (current source opened; dependent source kept). KCL at A:

{{frac|V~A~′ − 10|2}} + {{frac|V~A~′|4}} + 0.5V~A~′ = 0

Multiply by 4: 2V~A~′ − 20 + V~A~′ + 2V~A~′ = 0, so V~A~′ = 4 V.

**Step 2: 2 A source alone** (voltage source shorted; dependent source kept). KCL at A:

{{frac|V~A~″|2}} + {{frac|V~A~″|4}} + 0.5V~A~″ − 2 = 0

So 1.25V~A~″ = 2 and V~A~″ = 1.6 V.

**Step 3: Add.** V~A~ = 4 + 1.6 = **5.6 V**.

**Check by direct nodal analysis**: (V~A~ − 10)/2 + V~A~/4 + 0.5V~A~ − 2 = 0 gives 1.25V~A~ = 7, so V~A~ = 5.6 V ✓.
:::

## 13.6 Where Superposition Earns Its Keep

If nodal and mesh analysis can solve any linear circuit, why bother with superposition? There are several situations where it is genuinely the best tool.

### Sources of different kinds

Many electronic circuits contain a steady DC supply and a varying signal at the same time. An audio amplifier, for example, has its transistors held at a steady operating point by DC **bias** voltages, while the music signal rides on top. Superposition lets engineers analyse the two separately: one calculation for the DC bias conditions, another for the signal, then add them. Figure 13.5 shows the idea. This separation is used in every electronics course you will ever take, and it works precisely because the circuit (or a small-signal model of it) is linear.

![Figure 13.5 — A steady DC bias plus a varying signal. In a linear circuit, the effect of each can be calculated separately and the results added.](fig/ch13_bias.svg){width=100%}

When you later study AC circuits, you will also meet circuits driven by sources at different frequencies at once. Superposition is then not just convenient but essential, because the circuit responds differently at each frequency.

### Seeing each source's contribution

Superposition tells you not only the answer but *where it comes from*. In Example 13.2 it showed that two batteries of different voltages contributed equally. In a sensor circuit, it can show how much of an output is caused by the quantity being measured and how much by a drifting supply voltage. This kind of insight is invaluable in design.

### Summing signals

The **passive summing network** of Figure 13.6 is a direct application. Several input voltages are fed through equal resistors to a common node.

![Figure 13.6 — A passive summing (mixing) network. Each input contributes to the output in proportion to its own value.](fig/ch13_mixer.svg){width=52%}

::: example
**Worked Example 13.6 — A three-input mixer**

In Figure 13.6, all four resistors are 10 kΩ. Find V~out~ in terms of V~1~, V~2~ and V~3~, and evaluate it for V~1~ = 1 V, V~2~ = 2 V and V~3~ = −0.5 V. Assume the sources have negligible internal resistance.

**Step 1: V~1~ alone.** With V~2~ and V~3~ shorted to the reference, their 10 kΩ resistors join the output node to the reference, in parallel with the bottom 10 kΩ. Three 10 kΩ resistors in parallel make 3.33 kΩ. So V~1~ sees a divider of 10 kΩ and 3.33 kΩ:

V~out~′ = V~1~ × {{frac|3.33|10 + 3.33}} = {{frac|V~1~|4}}

**Step 2: By symmetry**, V~2~ and V~3~ each contribute a quarter of their own value.

**Step 3: Add.**

V~out~ = {{frac|V~1~ + V~2~ + V~3~|4}}

For the values given: V~out~ = (1 + 2 − 0.5)/4 = **0.625 V**.

**Comment.** The output is proportional to the *sum* of the inputs, which is exactly what a sound engineer's mixing desk needs. In practice an amplifier would follow to restore the signal level and to stop the inputs from interacting. Weighted versions of this network, with resistors in the ratio 1 : 2 : 4 : 8, form the heart of simple digital-to-analogue converters.
:::

### When not to use superposition

For a circuit with many sources, superposition means solving many circuits, and nodal or mesh analysis is usually faster. For power, as we have seen, it gives wrong answers unless used carefully. And for non-linear circuits – anything with diodes or transistors operating over a wide range – it does not apply at all.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Switching sources off the wrong way.** Voltage sources become shorts; current sources become opens.
- **Removing internal resistance** along with the source. Only the ideal source is switched off.
- **Switching off dependent sources.** They always stay.
- **Losing track of directions** when adding partial results. Fix a reference direction first.
- **Superposing power.** Find the total current or voltage first, then calculate power.
- **Applying superposition to non-linear circuits.**
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A **linear circuit** obeys **homogeneity** (scaling all sources scales every response) and **additivity** (responses to several sources add).
- The **superposition theorem**: any voltage or current is the sum of the contributions from each independent source acting alone.
- Switch off a **voltage source** by replacing it with a **short**; switch off a **current source** by replacing it with an **open**. Keep internal resistances. **Never switch off dependent sources.**
- Add partial results **algebraically**, respecting direction.
- **Power does not superpose**, because P = I²R is not linear.
- Superposition is ideal for **DC bias plus signal** problems, for seeing **each source's contribution**, and for **summing networks**.

::: keyeq
**Key Ideas**

Response = Σ (response to each independent source alone)     Voltage source off → short     Current source off → open     P ≠ P′ + P″
:::

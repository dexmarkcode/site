Every year, in the first weeks of a new term, I watch the same thing happen. A room full of bright, capable students meets circuit analysis for the first time, and a good number of them decide, quietly and far too early, that it is not for them. They have seen a page of simultaneous equations, or a diagram with a dozen resistors and three batteries, and concluded that the subject belongs to people with a special gift.

It does not. Circuit analysis is one of the most learnable subjects in engineering. It rests on a handful of physical ideas – charge, current, voltage, resistance, energy – and two simple laws that you can state in a sentence each. Everything else is method: careful, logical, step-by-step method that anyone can master with good explanations and enough practice. The students who struggle are rarely short of ability. Far more often, they were given formulae before they were given understanding, and shortcuts before they were given a map.

This book is my attempt to provide the understanding and the map.

### Who this book is for

I have written it for anyone meeting circuit analysis for the first time: students starting a degree, apprenticeship, college course or A-level in electrical or electronic engineering, physics or a related subject; technicians and makers who build circuits and want to understand them properly; and professionals in neighbouring fields who need a solid grounding in the subject. No previous knowledge of electricity is assumed. The mathematics needed is no more than confident algebra – rearranging equations and solving simultaneous equations – and Appendix B provides a refresher on the latter.

### What makes this book different

**It is built around problem-solving.** Each chapter introduces a small number of tools, explains *why* they work, and then shows, through worked examples set out step by step, *how* to use them. More than a hundred worked examples run through the book. They are not decoration; they are where the learning happens.

**It explains before it calculates.** Every law and theorem is introduced with a physical picture or a practical story, so that you know what it means before you start using it. When something is counter-intuitive – that adding resistors in parallel *reduces* resistance, that maximum power means only 50 % efficiency, that a meter can change the voltage it measures – I stop and explain why.

**It builds steadily.** The book is divided into six parts of three chapters each. Part One establishes the physical foundations. Part Two introduces the language of circuits and Kirchhoff's laws. Part Three develops techniques for simplifying networks. Part Four presents the systematic methods of nodal and mesh analysis. Part Five covers the great network theorems: superposition, Thévenin and Norton. Part Six brings everything together with maximum power transfer, a strategy for choosing methods, and the realities of measurement and fault-finding.

**It keeps returning to the same circuits.** One small circuit with two batteries and three resistors appears again and again, solved by the branch-current method, by nodal analysis, by mesh analysis, by superposition, by Thévenin's theorem and by source transformation. Seeing six methods give the same answer is the best possible demonstration that they are all simply different ways of applying the same physics – and the best possible training in choosing between them.

**It checks its answers.** Throughout, I model the habits of a working engineer: estimating before calculating, checking with a power balance or a spare Kirchhoff equation, and asking whether an answer makes physical sense.

**It connects to the real world.** You will meet kettles and electric showers, phone batteries and car starter motors, fairy lights and fuses, thermistors and strain gauges, star–delta motor starters and solar panels. Circuit analysis is not an abstract exercise; it is how the electrical world around us is designed and repaired.

### Conventions

This book follows British spelling and usage, and uses the IEC circuit symbols standard in the UK and Europe, including the rectangular resistor symbol. Conventional current is used throughout. Units follow the SI system, and values are written in engineering notation with prefixes in steps of a thousand.

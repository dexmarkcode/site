# Field-Oriented Control and Drive Strategies

::: chapter-intro
A traction motor is only as good as its controller. This chapter explains how a drive turns a torque request into precisely shaped phase currents. We build up the reference-frame transformations that make AC machines behave like simple DC machines, assemble them into field-oriented control, tune the current loops, choose currents for maximum torque per amp and for field weakening, and look at position sensing. We then step up to the vehicle and see how torque is shaped for smoothness, traction and regenerative braking.
:::

When an engineer first meets motor control, the mathematics can look forbidding: rotating reference frames, transformations with square roots of three, inductance matrices and coupled differential equations. I want to show you that behind it lies a single, rather beautiful idea. If you could stand on the rotor and spin with it, the three alternating currents in the stator would look like two steady, unchanging currents. And steady currents are easy to control.

Everything in field-oriented control follows from that idea. Once you see it, the transformations become bookkeeping and the control structure becomes almost obvious. So let us start by climbing onto the rotor.

## What the Controller Must Do

The vehicle control unit asks the motor drive for a torque, typically updated every few milliseconds. The drive must deliver that torque accurately, quickly and smoothly, across the whole speed range, in both directions, motoring and generating, while keeping the currents within the inverter's limits and the voltages within what the battery can supply. It must do this efficiently, choosing the currents that produce the requested torque with the least loss. And it must do so robustly, despite motor parameters that change with temperature and saturation, a battery voltage that sags under load, and sensors with errors.

The inner workings of the controller run fast. A typical traction drive samples its phase currents and updates its switching commands at 10 to 20 kHz, once every 50 to 100 microseconds. The current loops have bandwidths of one to a few kilohertz. That means a step in requested current is followed within a millisecond or less. By comparison, the vehicle's torque requests change over tens of milliseconds. This separation of time scales is what makes the problem tractable: the inner loops are fast enough that, from the vehicle's point of view, the motor simply produces whatever torque it is asked for.

## Space Vectors and the Clarke Transformation

At any instant, the three phase currents *i*~a~, *i*~b~ and *i*~c~ of a star-connected motor add up to zero. So there are really only two independent quantities, not three. We can capture them as a single *space vector*: a two-dimensional arrow that represents the combined effect of the three currents, pointing in the direction of the magnetic field they create together.

The *Clarke transformation* converts the three phase quantities into two components, *α* and *β*, on a pair of fixed, perpendicular axes, with *α* aligned to phase a:

$$ i_\alpha = \frac{2}{3}\left(i_a - \frac{1}{2}\, i_b - \frac{1}{2}\, i_c\right), \qquad i_\beta = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,(i_b - i_c) $$ {9.1}

The factor 2/3 makes the transformation *amplitude-invariant*: a balanced set of sinusoidal phase currents with peak value *I* produces a space vector of length *I*. That is why the torque equation in Chapter 7 contains a factor of 3/2; it converts back from this two-axis representation to the power of all three phases.

For balanced sinusoidal currents at angular frequency *ω*~e~, the α and β components are themselves sinusoids, 90° apart, and the space vector rotates at *ω*~e~ with constant length. That rotating arrow is the rotating magnetic field of Chapter 7, described mathematically.

## The Park Transformation: Riding on the Rotor

The Clarke transformation has simplified three quantities to two, but they are still alternating. The decisive step is the *Park transformation*, which views the space vector from a reference frame that rotates with the rotor. If the rotor's electrical angle is *θ*~e~, measured from the α-axis to the rotor's north-pole axis, then:

$$ i_d = i_\alpha \cos\theta_e + i_\beta \sin\theta_e, \qquad i_q = -i_\alpha \sin\theta_e + i_\beta \cos\theta_e $$ {9.2}

The *d-axis* (direct axis) points along the rotor's magnetic field; the *q-axis* (quadrature axis) is 90 electrical degrees ahead. Because the stator field rotates in step with a synchronous rotor, in steady operation *i*~d~ and *i*~q~ are *constant*. Figure 9.1 shows the progression.

![**Figure 9.1** From three phases to two steady currents. (a) Three sinusoidal phase currents. (b) The Clarke transformation gives two sinusoidal components on fixed α–β axes. (c) The Park transformation, using the rotor angle, gives constant d and q currents in the rotating frame.](figures/fig9_1.png)

In the rotor frame, the motor's voltage equations become:

$$ v_d = R\, i_d + L_d \frac{di_d}{dt} - \omega_e L_q\, i_q $$ {9.3}

$$ v_q = R\, i_q + L_q \frac{di_q}{dt} + \omega_e L_d\, i_d + \omega_e\, \psi_m $$ {9.4}

Each equation looks like a simple resistor-inductor circuit, plus two extra terms. The terms −*ω*~e~*L*~q~*i*~q~ and *ω*~e~*L*~d~*i*~d~ are *cross-coupling* terms: current on one axis induces voltage on the other, in proportion to speed. The term *ω*~e~*ψ*~m~ is the back-EMF of the magnets, which appears only on the q-axis. The torque, from Chapter 7, is:

$$ T = \frac{3}{2}\, p \left[\, \psi_m\, i_q + (L_d - L_q)\, i_d\, i_q \right] $$ {9.5}

In this frame, a permanent-magnet AC motor looks very much like a DC motor: *i*~q~ is the torque-producing current, like the armature current of a DC machine, and *i*~d~ adjusts the effective field. Control becomes a matter of regulating two DC currents.

## The Field-Oriented Control Structure

Figure 9.2 shows the complete control structure, which is used, with variations, in almost every traction drive.

![**Figure 9.2** Field-oriented control of a permanent-magnet traction motor. A torque request is converted into d and q current references. PI controllers in the rotating frame, with decoupling feedforward, compute voltage references, which are transformed back to the stationary frame and applied by the space-vector modulator. Measured phase currents and rotor position close the loop.](figures/fig9_2.png)

Working from left to right:

1. **Torque to current references.** The requested torque, together with the measured speed and DC-link voltage, is converted into references *i*~d~^\*^ and *i*~q~^\*^. In production drives this is done with look-up tables generated from motor measurements, because the inductances change with current through saturation and the relationship between current and torque is therefore not quite as clean as Equation 9.5 suggests.
2. **Current measurement and transformation.** Phase currents are sampled, usually at the centre of the PWM period where switching noise is lowest, and transformed into *i*~d~ and *i*~q~ using the measured rotor angle.
3. **Current controllers.** Two proportional-integral (PI) controllers, one per axis, compare the reference and measured currents and compute the required voltages *v*~d~^\*^ and *v*~q~^\*^.
4. **Decoupling feedforward.** The cross-coupling and back-EMF terms from Equations 9.3 and 9.4 are calculated and added to the PI outputs, so the PI controllers need only deal with the simple R-L dynamics.
5. **Inverse transformation and modulation.** The voltage references are transformed back to the stationary frame and passed to the *space-vector PWM* modulator (Chapter 11), which calculates the switching times for the six power switches.

The whole loop runs once every switching period or every half period. It is remarkable that a microcontroller can do all this, along with protection, diagnostics and communication, in a few tens of microseconds, but modern automotive processors with hardware trigonometry and dedicated PWM peripherals do it comfortably.

## Tuning the Current Loops

With decoupling in place, each axis is a simple first-order plant: a voltage applied across an inductance *L* and resistance *R*, with transfer function 1/(*Ls* + *R*). A PI controller with proportional gain *K*~p~ and integral gain *K*~i~ can be tuned by a neat method called *pole-zero cancellation*: choose the controller's zero to cancel the plant's pole, leaving a closed loop that behaves like a first-order filter with a chosen bandwidth *ω*~c~:

$$ K_p = \omega_c\, L, \qquad K_i = \omega_c\, R $$ {9.6}

The bandwidth cannot be chosen freely. The controller samples and updates once per switching period, and the PWM process itself introduces delay. A common rule is to keep the current-loop bandwidth below about one-tenth of the sampling frequency. Higher bandwidth gives faster torque response and better rejection of disturbances, but less margin against instability.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.1 — Tuning a q-axis current loop**

A motor has *L*~q~ = 0.30 mH and *R* = 15 mΩ. The inverter samples at 16 kHz. Choose PI gains for a q-axis current-loop bandwidth of 1 500 Hz, and check the bandwidth against the sampling rate.

*Solution.* *ω*~c~ = 2π × 1 500 = 9 425 rad/s.

*K*~p~ = 9 425 × 0.30 × 10^−3^ = 2.83 V/A.

*K*~i~ = 9 425 × 0.015 = 141 V/(A·s).

Check: 1 500 Hz is just under one-tenth of 16 kHz, so the design is reasonable. The closed loop responds to a step in current reference with a time constant of 1/*ω*~c~ ≈ 0.11 ms.

In practice, *L*~q~ falls significantly at high current because of saturation, perhaps by 30 to 50 %. A fixed *K*~p~ would then give a higher effective bandwidth and less stability margin. Production controllers therefore schedule their gains with current, or use the inductance tables from the motor characterisation.
:::

## Choosing the Currents: MTPA and Field Weakening

Equation 9.5 shows that, in an IPM machine, many different combinations of *i*~d~ and *i*~q~ give the same torque. Which should the controller choose?

### Maximum torque per ampere

Below base speed, voltage is plentiful and the controller is free to choose. The obvious goal is to produce the requested torque with the smallest current, which minimises copper loss and inverter loss. This is *maximum torque per ampere* (MTPA) operation. For a given current magnitude, there is an optimum angle between the current vector and the q-axis, at which the combined magnet and reluctance torque is greatest. For an ideal IPM machine with constant inductances, the optimum d-axis current for a given q-axis current is:

$$ i_d = \frac{\psi_m}{2(L_q - L_d)} - \sqrt{\frac{\psi_m^2}{4(L_q - L_d)^2} + i_q^2} $$ {9.7}

Plotting the MTPA points for every torque level gives the *MTPA trajectory* shown in Figure 9.3, curving from the origin into the second quadrant of the d–q plane.

![**Figure 9.3** The d–q current plane for an IPM motor, showing constant-torque curves (hyperbolas), the current-limit circle, the MTPA trajectory and the voltage-limit ellipse at one speed above base speed. At low speed the operating point follows MTPA; when the voltage ellipse cuts in, it moves along the ellipse towards larger negative *i*~d~ (field weakening).](figures/fig9_3.png)

::: example
**Worked Example 9.2 — The value of MTPA**

For the motor of Worked Example 7.4 (*p* = 4, *ψ*~m~ = 0.08 Wb, *L*~d~ = 0.15 mH, *L*~q~ = 0.45 mH), compare the torque produced by a current vector of magnitude 447 A (peak) when (a) all of it is placed on the q-axis, (b) it is split as in Example 7.4 (*i*~d~ = −200 A, *i*~q~ = 400 A), and (c) it is placed at the MTPA angle.

*Solution.* (a) *i*~d~ = 0, *i*~q~ = 447 A: *T* = 6 × 0.08 × 447 = 215 N·m.

(b) From Example 7.4: 336 N·m.

(c) Searching numerically for the angle that maximises Equation 9.5 at |*I*| = 447 A gives a current angle of 35° from the q-axis: *i*~d~ = −257 A, *i*~q~ = 366 A. Then

*T* = 6 × [0.08 × 366 + (−0.30 × 10^−3^)(−257)(366)] = 6 × [29.3 + 28.2] = 345 N·m.

MTPA gives 60 % more torque than putting all the current on the q-axis, for the same copper loss. Equivalently, for a required 215 N·m, MTPA would need roughly 30 % less current and so about half the copper loss. This is why IPM machines must not be controlled as though they were simple surface-magnet machines.
:::

### Field weakening

Above base speed, the voltage-limit ellipse of Chapter 8 shrinks into the region the MTPA trajectory passes through, and the controller must leave MTPA. It moves the operating point along the edge of the voltage ellipse, towards more negative *i*~d~. The negative d-axis current produces a voltage *ω*~e~*L*~d~*i*~d~ that opposes the magnets' back-EMF in Equation 9.4, freeing voltage to push q-axis current into the machine.

In practice, field-weakening controllers combine pre-calculated look-up tables, indexed by torque, speed and DC-link voltage, with a feedback loop that watches how close the voltage command is to the modulator's limit and adds extra negative *i*~d~ if it gets too close. That feedback is important because the battery voltage varies widely with state of charge and load. A drive that works perfectly at 400 V may lose control at 320 V on a nearly empty battery if the tables are not right.

At very high speeds, some machines reach the region where the operating point should follow a *maximum torque per volt* (MTPV) trajectory, which extracts the most torque from the limited voltage. Few drivers will ever reach that region, but the controller must handle it gracefully.

## Knowing Where the Rotor Is

All of the transformations above depend on the rotor angle *θ*~e~. An error in that angle means the controller's d and q axes are not the motor's true d and q axes, and current intended to produce torque ends up partly on the wrong axis.

### Resolvers

Most traction motors use a *resolver*: a small, rugged rotary transformer mounted on the motor shaft. An excitation coil is fed with a sinusoid, typically around 10 kHz, and two output coils, mounted 90° apart, pick up versions of it whose amplitudes vary as the sine and cosine of the shaft angle. A *resolver-to-digital converter* demodulates them and computes the angle, often with a tracking loop that also gives speed. Resolvers are favoured because they contain no electronics on the motor, tolerate high temperature, vibration and oil, and give absolute position from the instant of power-up.

Other sensors are also used, including inductive position sensors built on printed circuit boards, which are cheaper and lighter, and magnetic sensors.

### Calibration and offset

The resolver's zero position must be aligned with the rotor's magnetic d-axis, and the offset between them must be measured at the end of the production line, typically by spinning the motor and observing the back-EMF waveform. If a resolver is replaced in service, or the calibration value is lost, the offset may be wrong.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.3 — The cost of a 10° position error**

The motor from Example 9.2 is commanded to produce torque with *i*~d~^\*^ = 0 and *i*~q~^\*^ = 400 A, which should give 192 N·m. The resolver offset is wrong by 10 electrical degrees. What torque results if the error places the actual current vector 10° towards positive *i*~d~? And towards negative *i*~d~?

*Solution.* The controller regulates the currents correctly in its own frame, but the motor's true frame is rotated. The true currents are:

Case 1 (towards positive *i*~d~): *i*~d~ = 400 sin 10° = +69.5 A, *i*~q~ = 400 cos 10° = 393.9 A.
*T* = 6 × [0.08 × 393.9 + (−0.30 × 10^−3^)(69.5)(393.9)] = 6 × [31.5 − 8.2] = 140 N·m.

Case 2 (towards negative *i*~d~): *i*~d~ = −69.5 A, *i*~q~ = 393.9 A.
*T* = 6 × [31.5 + 8.2] = 238 N·m.

A 10° error changes torque by −27 % or +24 % depending on its direction. Worse, because the sign of *i*~q~ changes between motoring and regenerating, an offset that adds torque in one direction tends to remove it in the other. The driver may feel the car pull more strongly when accelerating than it brakes regeneratively, and at high speed the error can also upset field weakening and risk loss of current control. Correct calibration is not optional.
:::

### Sensorless control

It is possible to estimate rotor position without a sensor, using the motor's own electrical behaviour. At medium and high speeds, *back-EMF observers* estimate the angle from the voltage the magnets induce. At low speed and standstill, where the back-EMF is too small, *high-frequency injection* methods superimpose a small high-frequency voltage and detect the rotor's saliency, the difference between *L*~d~ and *L*~q~, from the resulting current. Sensorless control is common in industrial drives and pumps and is used in some automotive auxiliaries. For traction drives, it is increasingly used as a *backup* that keeps the vehicle driving if the resolver fails, rather than as the primary method, because the safety and accuracy demands at standstill and launch are severe.

## Controlling Induction Motors

Induction motors use the same field-oriented principle, but with a twist. Their rotor field is not fixed by magnets; it is created by the d-axis current itself, building up with the *rotor time constant* *τ*~r~ = *L*~r~/*R*~r~, typically a few hundred milliseconds. And there is no physical rotor feature to align with: the reference frame must be aligned with the *rotor flux*, which slips relative to the rotor.

In *indirect rotor-flux-oriented control*, the controller computes the slip frequency needed for the requested torque,

$$ \omega_{slip} = \frac{1}{\tau_r} \cdot \frac{i_q}{i_d} $$ {9.8}

and adds it to the measured rotor speed to obtain the angle of the flux frame. The d-axis current sets the flux level and the q-axis current sets the torque. Because the rotor resistance changes significantly with temperature, the estimate of *τ*~r~ must be corrected online, or the controller's frame drifts away from the true flux and both torque accuracy and efficiency suffer.

Induction motor drives also gain efficiency by *reducing flux at light load*: there is no point magnetising the machine fully to produce a few newton-metres. Flux can be built up again quickly when the driver demands torque, though not instantly, which is one reason the response of induction drives is tuned with particular care.

## From Motor Torque to Vehicle Behaviour

The motor controller delivers the torque it is asked for. Deciding what to ask for is the job of the vehicle control unit, and that decision determines much of how an EV feels to drive.

### Pedal maps

The accelerator pedal does not request a fixed torque. A *pedal map* converts pedal position and vehicle speed into a torque request, shaped to give a progressive, predictable response. Many EVs offer several maps, from gentle to aggressive, and the same hardware can be made to feel very different through these maps alone.

### One-pedal driving and regeneration

When the driver lifts off the accelerator, the map can request negative torque, so the car slows as if braking. With strong enough lift-off regeneration, the driver can bring the car almost to a stop without touching the brake pedal: *one-pedal driving*. When the brake pedal is pressed, a *brake blending* function decides how much deceleration to produce by regeneration and how much with the friction brakes. Regeneration is limited by the battery's charge-power limit (Chapter 6), by motor capability, and by vehicle stability: too much regenerative torque on a single driven axle on a slippery road can lock the wheels or upset the car. The blending must be seamless; the driver should feel a consistent relationship between pedal force and deceleration however the work is divided.

### Driveline oscillation and active damping

The driveline between motor and wheels, consisting of gears, driveshafts and tyres, is a torsional spring with very little damping. An electric motor can change its torque in a millisecond, far faster than an engine. A sudden step in motor torque therefore excites the driveline's natural frequency, typically 5 to 15 Hz, and the car shuffles back and forth. Drivers feel this as judder or *shunt*.

The cure is *active damping*. The controller watches for the oscillation, by comparing motor speed with a model of where it should be or with wheel speeds, and modulates the torque request to cancel it, much as a skilled driver eases on the throttle. Figure 9.4 shows the effect. Equally important is shaping torque as it passes through zero, because gear lash, the small play between gear teeth, makes the driveline momentarily slack as torque reverses. Approaching zero slowly and accelerating away from it prevents the clunk of teeth meeting.

![**Figure 9.4** Vehicle response to a step torque request. Without damping, the driveline rings at its natural frequency for several cycles; with active damping, the response is smooth and almost as fast.](figures/fig9_4.png)

### Traction control

An electric motor's fast torque response makes it superb for traction control. When a wheel begins to spin, the controller can cut torque within a few milliseconds, compared with the hundreds of milliseconds an engine needs. Electric vehicle traction control systems therefore often run inside or alongside the motor controller, using motor speed directly, and can hold the tyre very close to its peak friction. With one motor per wheel or per side, the same capability becomes *torque vectoring*, distributing torque to help the car turn.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Car pulls harder accelerating than it slows regenerating, after a motor repair**

*Symptom.* After replacement of a resolver, a car feels normal when accelerating but has noticeably weaker lift-off regeneration, and at high speed occasionally logs a current-control fault.

*Likely causes.* (1) Resolver offset not calibrated after replacement (see Worked Example 9.3). (2) Resolver mounted with wrong polarity or wiring swap, reversing direction sensing. (3) Damaged resolver connector causing noisy angle signals. (4) Genuine regen limitation from a cold or full battery, unrelated to the repair.

*How to tell them apart.* Check the battery's charge power limit first. Then compare logged d and q currents with the torque estimate: an offset produces a characteristic asymmetry between motoring and generating. Run the manufacturer's offset learning procedure, which typically spins the motor unloaded and measures the back-EMF angle. Inspect the connector and harness for damage. A large offset or reversed wiring is usually obvious from the first test drive data.
:::

::: casefile
**Case File 9 — The clunk at parking speed**

*The complaint.* Customers of a new rear-drive saloon report a "clunk" and slight jolt when they gently press the accelerator after rolling to a near stop, for example in slow traffic or car parks. The issue is reported as poor quality, and dealer visits are increasing.

*State it in numbers.* Accelerometer measurements in the cabin show a peak fore-aft jerk of 18 m/s^3^ at the moment of tip-in from light regeneration, against an internal refinement target of 6 m/s^3^. The event happens when motor torque crosses from about −40 N·m to positive.

*Draw the boundary.* Pedal map and torque request shaping in the vehicle control unit, the motor controller's response, driveline lash and stiffness, and motor and gearbox mounts.

*Simplest model.* When torque reverses, the gear teeth must cross the lash in the reduction gear and differential, about 0.4° at the motor shaft once multiplied through. During the crossing, the motor is effectively unloaded and accelerates freely; when the teeth meet again, the impact excites the driveline at around 9 Hz. A torque ramp of 2 000 N·m/s through zero gives the motor time to build up a relative speed of several radians per second before contact, which is consistent with the measured jerk.

*Measure.* High-speed logs of motor speed and wheel speed show the motor speeding up by 12 rpm during the lash crossing, followed by a decaying 9 Hz oscillation. The existing active damping function is disabled below 5 km/h because its wheel-speed signals become unreliable at very low speed.

*Dominant term.* Lash crossing at too high a torque rate, with no damping at low speed.

*Fix and cross-check.* The vehicle control unit's torque shaping is changed to slow the torque rate to 300 N·m/s in a window around zero torque, then accelerate it again once the lash is taken up, detected by the change in motor acceleration. The motor controller adds a low-speed damping mode that uses motor speed alone. Peak jerk falls to 5 m/s^3^. The team checks that the extra 50 ms of response delay at tip-in is not noticeable in subjective drives, and that the change does not upset hill-hold behaviour on steep car-park ramps.

*Lesson.* Electric drives are so fast that they expose every compliance and every bit of play in the mechanical system. Smoothness is a control problem as much as a mechanical one.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Field-oriented control transforms the three phase currents into two currents in a frame rotating with the rotor, where they become steady and can be regulated like DC currents.
- The Clarke transformation maps three phases to two stationary axes; the Park transformation, using rotor angle, maps them to the rotating d and q axes.
- In the d–q frame, each axis is an R–L circuit with cross-coupling and back-EMF terms; PI current controllers with decoupling feedforward regulate *i*~d~ and *i*~q~. Pole-zero cancellation gives simple tuning rules.
- Below base speed, MTPA chooses the current angle that minimises current for each torque; above base speed, field weakening moves along the voltage limit.
- Accurate rotor position, usually from a resolver, is essential; offset errors cause large, asymmetric torque errors. Sensorless methods provide backup.
- Induction motor control orients to rotor flux using slip calculation and depends on an accurate rotor time constant.
- At vehicle level, pedal maps, regenerative braking blending, active damping, lash management and fast traction control shape how the car feels.
:::

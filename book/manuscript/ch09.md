Walk into almost any factory in Britain and you will find, mounted on a wall near a large electric motor, a grey steel box labelled "star–delta starter". When the start button is pressed, there is a deep hum as the motor begins to turn, then a loud clunk a few seconds later as contactors inside the box change over. That clunk is the motor's windings being reconnected from one shape to another: from a **star** to a **delta**. The trick reduces the huge surge of current a large motor draws when it starts, protecting both the motor and the supply.

The star and the delta are the two ways of connecting three components between three terminals. They turn up in three-phase power systems, in motor windings and, as we saw at the end of Chapter 7, inside the humble bridge network that defeats series–parallel reduction. In 1899 the American engineer Arthur Kennelly showed how to convert one into the other, so that any delta of resistors can be replaced by an equivalent star, and vice versa, without changing anything outside them.

This chapter completes Part Three. With the **delta–star transformation** added to series, parallel and the divider rules, you will be able to reduce a far wider range of networks, including unbalanced bridges, to a single equivalent resistance.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- recognise delta (Δ, or π) and star (Y, or T) connections in a circuit;
- convert a delta of resistors into an equivalent star;
- convert a star of resistors into an equivalent delta;
- explain what "equivalent" means for a three-terminal network;
- use the transformations to analyse unbalanced bridge networks;
- apply the special case of equal resistors quickly;
- relate the transformations to three-phase systems and star–delta motor starting.
:::

## 9.1 Two Shapes With Three Terminals

Figure 9.1 shows the two connections. In the **delta**, named after the Greek letter Δ, three resistors form a closed triangle, and each terminal sits at a corner. In the **star**, sometimes called a **wye** after the letter Y, three resistors meet at a central point, and each terminal is at the end of an arm.

![Figure 9.1 — (a) A delta connection: each resistor connects between a pair of terminals. (b) A star connection: each resistor connects one terminal to a common central point.](fig/ch09_shapes.svg){width=88%}

Notice the naming convention, which I will follow throughout. In the delta, each resistor is named after the **two terminals it connects**: R~ab~ joins a and b. In the star, each resistor is named after the **one terminal it reaches**: R~a~ runs from the centre to a. Keeping this straight makes the formulas easy to remember.

The same two networks are often drawn in a different shape, especially in electronics, as in Figure 9.2. Redrawn this way, the delta looks like the Greek letter π and the star like the letter T. Do not be fooled by the new outlines: the π network *is* a delta and the T network *is* a star. Check the connections, as we learned to in Chapter 4, and you will see they are identical.

![Figure 9.2 — The same networks drawn as (a) a π network and (b) a T network. The terminal c appears twice because the bottom wire is a single node.](fig/ch09_pi_t.svg){width=96%}

## 9.2 What Does "Equivalent" Mean Here?

In Chapter 6 we replaced two series resistors by one equivalent resistor. Equivalent meant that the source could not tell the difference: same voltage, same current at the two terminals.

For three-terminal networks the idea is the same, but there are more terminals to satisfy. A delta and a star are **equivalent** if, whatever is connected to terminals a, b and c, the currents flowing into those terminals and the voltages between them are exactly the same for both. The circuit outside cannot tell which one is inside the box.

A convenient way to guarantee this is to demand that the resistance measured between **every pair of terminals**, with the third terminal left unconnected, is the same for both networks. For linear resistive networks this condition turns out to be sufficient, and it gives us the formulas we need.

### Deriving the conversion

Take the pair a–b, with c left open.

In the **star**, current entering at a passes through R~a~ to the centre and out through R~b~ to terminal b. R~c~ leads to the open terminal c, so it carries no current. The resistance between a and b is simply

::: eq
R~a~ + R~b~
:::

In the **delta**, current entering at a can go directly through R~ab~ to b, or round the other way through R~ca~ and R~bc~ in series. These two paths are in parallel:

::: eq
R~ab~ ∥ (R~bc~ + R~ca~) = {{frac|R~ab~ (R~bc~ + R~ca~)|R~ab~ + R~bc~ + R~ca~}}
:::

For equivalence these must be equal. Writing Σ = R~ab~ + R~bc~ + R~ca~ for the sum of the delta resistors, we get three equations, one for each pair of terminals:

::: eq
R~a~ + R~b~ = {{frac|R~ab~R~bc~ + R~ab~R~ca~|Σ}}    (1)

R~b~ + R~c~ = {{frac|R~bc~R~ab~ + R~bc~R~ca~|Σ}}    (2)

R~c~ + R~a~ = {{frac|R~ca~R~ab~ + R~ca~R~bc~|Σ}}    (3)
:::

To find R~a~ on its own, add (1) and (3) and subtract (2). On the left, R~b~ and R~c~ cancel, leaving 2R~a~. On the right, most of the products cancel too, leaving 2R~ab~R~ca~ / Σ. So R~a~ = R~ab~R~ca~ / Σ, and the other two follow in exactly the same way.

## 9.3 Delta to Star

::: keyeq
**Delta-to-star transformation**

R~a~ = {{frac|R~ab~ R~ca~|Σ}}     R~b~ = {{frac|R~ab~ R~bc~|Σ}}     R~c~ = {{frac|R~bc~ R~ca~|Σ}}

where Σ = R~ab~ + R~bc~ + R~ca~.
:::

There is a simple pattern to remember: **each star resistor equals the product of the two delta resistors that touch the same terminal, divided by the sum of all three**. For R~a~, the two delta resistors that meet at terminal a are R~ab~ and R~ca~.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.1 — A first delta-to-star conversion**

A delta has R~ab~ = 6 Ω, R~bc~ = 18 Ω and R~ca~ = 12 Ω. Find the equivalent star.

**Step 1: Sum.** Σ = 6 + 18 + 12 = 36 Ω.

**Step 2: Each arm.**

R~a~ = {{frac|6 × 12|36}} = 2 Ω     R~b~ = {{frac|6 × 18|36}} = 3 Ω     R~c~ = {{frac|18 × 12|36}} = 6 Ω

**Step 3: Check one pair.** Between a and b in the star: 2 + 3 = 5 Ω. In the delta: 6 ∥ (18 + 12) = 6 × 30 / 36 = 5 Ω. ✓
:::

The star resistors are always smaller than the delta resistors that produced them. That makes sense: a delta offers two parallel paths between every pair of terminals, while a star offers only one, so the star's individual resistors must be smaller to give the same terminal-to-terminal resistance.

## 9.4 Star to Delta

Sometimes we need to go the other way. Solving equations (1) to (3) for the delta resistors in terms of the star resistors gives the following result.

::: keyeq
**Star-to-delta transformation**

R~ab~ = {{frac|R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~|R~c~}}     R~bc~ = {{frac|R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~|R~a~}}     R~ca~ = {{frac|R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~|R~b~}}
:::

The pattern this time: **each delta resistor equals the sum of the pairwise products of the star resistors, divided by the star resistor on the opposite side**. The resistor R~ab~ sits between terminals a and b; the star arm *not* touching either of them is R~c~, so that is the one we divide by.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.2 — Star back to delta**

Convert the star R~a~ = 2 Ω, R~b~ = 3 Ω, R~c~ = 6 Ω into a delta.

**Step 1: Sum of pairwise products.** R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~ = 6 + 18 + 12 = 36 Ω².

**Step 2: Each side.**

R~ab~ = 36 / R~c~ = 36 / 6 = 6 Ω     R~bc~ = 36 / R~a~ = 36 / 2 = 18 Ω     R~ca~ = 36 / R~b~ = 36 / 3 = 12 Ω

**Comment.** We have recovered exactly the delta we started with in Worked Example 9.1, as we should.
:::

::: tip
**Two Ways to Remember the Formulas**

Delta to star: "**product of adjacent over sum**". Star to delta: "**sum of products over opposite**". If you think of it in conductances instead, star-to-delta looks exactly like delta-to-star with resistances swapped for conductances – another example of duality.
:::

### The equal-resistor case

When all three resistors are equal, the formulas collapse to something you can do in your head. For a delta of three resistors R~Δ~, each star arm is R~Δ~ × R~Δ~ / (3R~Δ~) = R~Δ~ / 3. Going the other way, each delta side is 3R~Y~.

::: keyeq
**Equal resistors**

R~Y~ = {{frac|R~Δ~|3}}     R~Δ~ = 3R~Y~
:::

This case is extremely common in three-phase power systems, where loads are usually balanced across the three phases.

## 9.5 Solving the Unbalanced Bridge

Now we can deal with the network that defeated us in Chapter 7. Figure 9.3 shows an unbalanced bridge driven by a 20 V source. Check: R~1~R~4~ = 6 × 6.5 = 39, but R~2~R~3~ = 3 × 5 = 15, so it is not balanced, and R~5~ carries current.

![Figure 9.3 — An unbalanced bridge. R~1~, R~2~ and R~5~ form a delta between nodes T, C and D.](fig/ch09_bridge.svg){width=72%}

The key is to spot a delta. Look at the top half of the bridge: R~1~ connects T to C, R~2~ connects T to D, and R~5~ connects C to D. Those three resistors form a closed triangle between three nodes – a delta. Replace it with a star, and the bridge becomes an ordinary series–parallel network.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.3 — Solving an unbalanced bridge**

For Figure 9.3, find the equivalent resistance seen by the source, the source current and the current in every resistor, including R~5~.

**Step 1: Identify the delta.** Between T, C and D: R~TC~ = R~1~ = 6 Ω, R~TD~ = R~2~ = 3 Ω, R~CD~ = R~5~ = 9 Ω. Sum Σ = 18 Ω.

**Step 2: Convert to a star**, with a new central node N. Each arm is the product of the two delta resistors touching that terminal, over the sum:

- R~T~ (touching T: R~1~ and R~2~) = 6 × 3 / 18 = 1 Ω
- R~C~ (touching C: R~1~ and R~5~) = 6 × 9 / 18 = 3 Ω
- R~D~ (touching D: R~2~ and R~5~) = 3 × 9 / 18 = 1.5 Ω

The circuit now looks like Figure 9.4.

![Figure 9.4 — The bridge after converting the top delta into a star with centre N. It is now a simple series–parallel network.](fig/ch09_bridge_star.svg){width=72%}

**Step 3: Reduce.** Left leg: R~C~ + R~3~ = 3 + 5 = 8 Ω. Right leg: R~D~ + R~4~ = 1.5 + 6.5 = 8 Ω. These are in parallel between N and B: 8 ∥ 8 = 4 Ω. Add R~T~: R~eq~ = 1 + 4 = **5 Ω**.

**Step 4: Source current.** I = 20 / 5 = **4 A**.

**Step 5: Node voltages** (taking B as 0 V). The 4 A flows through R~T~, so V~N~ = 20 − 4 × 1 = 16 V. The two legs are equal, so each carries 2 A. Then V~C~ = 16 − 2 × 3 = 10 V and V~D~ = 16 − 2 × 1.5 = 13 V.

**Step 6: Return to the original circuit.** Here is the crucial point. The star is equivalent to the delta *only at the terminals T, C and D*. The node N does not exist in the real circuit, and the star currents are not the real currents in R~1~, R~2~ and R~5~. But the voltages at T, C and D *are* correct, because those terminals are shared. So we use them in the original circuit:

- I~R1~ = (V~T~ − V~C~) / 6 = (20 − 10) / 6 = 1.667 A
- I~R2~ = (V~T~ − V~D~) / 3 = (20 − 13) / 3 = 2.333 A
- I~R5~ = (V~D~ − V~C~) / 9 = (13 − 10) / 9 = 0.333 A, flowing from D to C
- I~R3~ = V~C~ / 5 = 10 / 5 = 2 A
- I~R4~ = V~D~ / 6.5 = 13 / 6.5 = 2 A

**Step 7: Check KCL.** At T: 1.667 + 2.333 = 4 A ✓. At C: in 1.667 (from R~1~) + 0.333 (from R~5~) = 2 A, out 2 A through R~3~ ✓. At D: in 2.333, out 2 + 0.333 = 2.333 ✓.
:::

Step 6 is where most students go wrong, so let me emphasise it. **The transformation preserves what happens at the three terminals, and nothing else.** Use the transformed circuit to find terminal voltages (or terminal currents), then return to the original circuit to find the currents in the resistors that were transformed.

### There is always more than one route

In the bridge, we could equally well have converted the *bottom* delta (R~3~, R~4~ and R~5~, between C, D and B) into a star, or converted one of the stars (for example the three resistors meeting at C) into a delta. Every route leads to the same answer. Choosing the route that gives the neatest numbers is a matter of experience.

::: example
**Worked Example 9.4 — Checking by a different route**

Find the equivalent resistance of Figure 9.3 by converting the bottom delta (C, D, B) instead.

**Step 1: The delta.** R~CD~ = 9 Ω, R~CB~ = R~3~ = 5 Ω, R~DB~ = R~4~ = 6.5 Ω. Σ = 20.5 Ω.

**Step 2: The star.** R~C~ = 9 × 5 / 20.5 = 2.195 Ω; R~D~ = 9 × 6.5 / 20.5 = 2.854 Ω; R~B~ = 5 × 6.5 / 20.5 = 1.585 Ω.

**Step 3: Reduce.** Left path from T: R~1~ + R~C~ = 8.195 Ω. Right path: R~2~ + R~D~ = 5.854 Ω. In parallel: 8.195 × 5.854 / 14.049 = 3.415 Ω. Add R~B~: 3.415 + 1.585 = **5.000 Ω** ✓.

The numbers were less tidy, but the answer is identical.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 9.5 — Five equal resistors**

Five 3 Ω resistors are connected as a bridge. Find the resistance between the top and bottom nodes, (a) using a delta–star transformation and (b) by spotting a shortcut.

**(a)** The top delta of three 3 Ω resistors becomes a star of 1 Ω arms. Each leg is then 1 + 3 = 4 Ω; two in parallel give 2 Ω; adding the top arm gives 1 + 2 = **3 Ω**.

**(b)** The bridge is balanced (3 × 3 = 3 × 3), so the middle resistor carries no current and can be removed: (3 + 3) ∥ (3 + 3) = 3 Ω. ✓

**Lesson.** Always check for balance first. If it is balanced, you can skip the transformation altogether.
:::

## 9.6 Star and Delta in Three-Phase Systems

Although this book is about DC circuits, it would be a shame not to mention where star and delta matter most in practice. Britain's electricity is generated, transmitted and distributed as **three-phase** AC: three live conductors whose voltages are equal in size but staggered in time. Large loads, such as industrial heaters and motors, are connected across the three phases in either star or delta.

In a UK low-voltage system, the voltage between any two lines is 400 V, and the voltage between any line and the star point (neutral) is 230 V. In a **star** connection, each element is connected between a line and the star point and so receives 230 V. In a **delta** connection, each element is connected between two lines and so receives 400 V.

![Figure 9.5 — Motor windings connected in star and in delta. In star each winding receives the lower phase voltage; in delta it receives the full line voltage.](fig/ch09_motor.svg){width=88%}

The transformations of this chapter apply to balanced three-phase loads directly. A delta-connected heater with 30 Ω per element behaves, from its three terminals, exactly like a star-connected heater with 10 Ω per element.

### Star–delta starting, explained with resistors

Now we can explain the clunk in the factory. When a large motor starts from rest, its windings behave, for a moment, rather like plain resistances, and they draw a very large current. Let us compare the resistance between two supply lines for the two connections, using a resistance R for each winding.

- **Delta:** between two lines there is one winding directly, in parallel with the other two in series: R ∥ 2R = 2R/3.
- **Star:** between two lines there are two windings in series: 2R.

The star presents **three times** the resistance of the delta between the same pair of lines, so it draws one third of the line current. The starter therefore connects the windings in star while the motor accelerates, keeping the inrush current down to a third. Once the motor is up to speed and its current has fallen, the contactors reconnect the windings in delta, and the motor delivers its full power. Real motors involve inductance and AC theory, but the factor of three is exactly the one our DC resistances predict.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Mixing up the formulas.** Delta-to-star: product of *adjacent* over *sum*. Star-to-delta: sum of products over *opposite*.
- **Converting resistors that do not form a true delta or star.** A delta must be a closed triangle between exactly three nodes; a star must have a centre node with nothing else connected to it.
- **Using star currents as if they were real.** The internal currents of a transformed network are not the real currents. Find terminal voltages, then go back to the original.
- **Forgetting to check for balance.** A balanced bridge needs no transformation at all.
- **Losing track of node names.** Label the three terminals clearly before and after the conversion.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A **delta** (Δ, π) connects three resistors in a triangle; a **star** (Y, T) connects them to a common centre.
- The two are **equivalent** when the resistance between every pair of terminals is the same.
- **Delta to star**: R~a~ = R~ab~R~ca~ / Σ, and similarly for R~b~ and R~c~.
- **Star to delta**: R~ab~ = (R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~) / R~c~, and similarly for the others.
- For **equal resistors**, R~Y~ = R~Δ~ / 3.
- An **unbalanced bridge** can be solved by transforming one of its deltas or stars into the other form.
- The transformation is valid **only at the terminals**; return to the original circuit for internal currents.
- In three-phase systems, a star-connected load sees 230 V per element and a delta-connected load 400 V. **Star–delta starting** reduces a motor's inrush current to one third.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

R~a~ = R~ab~R~ca~ / Σ     R~ab~ = (R~a~R~b~ + R~b~R~c~ + R~c~R~a~) / R~c~     R~Y~ = R~Δ~ / 3
:::

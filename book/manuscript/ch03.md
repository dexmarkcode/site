In 1827 a schoolteacher in Cologne published a short book setting out the results of years of careful experiments. Georg Simon Ohm had found that, for a metal wire kept at a steady temperature, the current through it was exactly proportional to the voltage across it. Double the voltage, double the current. It is hard to imagine a simpler or more useful result.

His reward was ridicule. A senior official at the Prussian education ministry called his work "a web of naked fancies", and Ohm resigned his teaching post. It took more than a decade for the scientific world to recognise what he had done. In 1841 the Royal Society in London awarded him its Copley Medal, and today his name is attached to the unit of resistance and to the most widely used law in electrical engineering.

You will use Ohm's law thousands of times in your career, often without noticing. This chapter makes sure you use it correctly: in all its forms, with the right units, and with a clear understanding of when it applies and when it does not. We will also put it to work straight away on some genuinely practical problems – choosing a resistor for an LED, working out the voltage lost in a long cable and understanding why your car's headlights dim when you start the engine.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- state Ohm's law and use it in all three of its forms;
- interpret voltage–current graphs and distinguish ohmic from non-ohmic components;
- calculate the power in a resistor using P = I²R and P = V²/R;
- choose a resistor's value and power rating for a practical job;
- calculate the voltage drop and power loss in a cable;
- explain short circuits, open circuits and the job of a fuse;
- model a real battery with an EMF and internal resistance.
:::

## 3.1 Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that **the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, provided its temperature and other physical conditions stay the same**. The constant of proportionality is the resistance.

::: keyeq
**Ohm's law**

V = I × R

where V is the voltage across the component in volts (V), I is the current through it in amperes (A) and R is its resistance in ohms (Ω).
:::

This also gives us the proper definition of the ohm that I promised in Chapter 2: **a component has a resistance of one ohm if a voltage of one volt across it drives a current of one ampere through it.**

The law can be rearranged to find whichever quantity you need:

::: eq
V = I × R          I = {{frac|V|R}}          R = {{frac|V|I}}
:::

Many students learn these using the triangle in Figure 3.1. Cover the quantity you want with your finger, and the triangle shows you how to calculate it from the other two: cover V and you see I × R; cover I and you see V over R; cover R and you see V over I.

![Figure 3.1 — The Ohm's law triangle. Cover the quantity you want to find; what remains shows how to calculate it.](fig/ch03_triangle.svg){width=34%}

I have mixed feelings about the triangle. It is a fine memory aid, but it can become a crutch that hides the meaning. Try to understand each form in words as well:

- **V = IR**: the voltage needed to push a current through a resistance grows with both the current and the resistance.
- **I = V/R**: for a given push, more resistance means less current.
- **R = V/I**: resistance is how many volts it takes to drive each amp.

If you understand those three sentences, you will never need the triangle.

### Applying Ohm's law correctly

Ohm's law relates three quantities that must all belong to the **same component** (or the same group of components). The V must be the voltage *across* that resistor, the I must be the current *through* that same resistor, and the R must be its resistance. This sounds obvious, but in larger circuits the most common Ohm's law error is to divide the voltage across one thing by the resistance of another. When we reach series and parallel circuits I shall keep reminding you to label every voltage and current with the component it belongs to.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.1 — Finding current**

A 470 Ω resistor is connected across a 12 V supply. What current flows?

**Solution.** I = V / R = 12 / 470 = 0.0255 A = 25.5 mA.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.2 — Finding voltage**

A current of 20 mA flows through a 2.2 kΩ resistor. What is the voltage across it?

**Solution.** Convert to base units: I = 0.020 A, R = 2200 Ω.

V = I × R = 0.020 × 2200 = 44 V.

**A useful shortcut.** Milliamps multiplied by kilohms give volts directly, because the 10^−3^ and 10^3^ cancel: 20 mA × 2.2 kΩ = 44 V. Electronics engineers use this constantly. But use it knowingly, not by accident.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.3 — Finding resistance**

A sensor connected to a 9 V supply draws 15 mA. What is its effective resistance?

**Solution.** R = V / I = 9 / 0.015 = 600 Ω.
:::

## 3.2 Voltage–Current Graphs

A good way to see what Ohm's law says is to plot it. If we measure the current through a component at several different voltages and plot current against voltage, a component that obeys Ohm's law gives a **straight line through the origin**, as in the left-hand graph of Figure 3.2. The steeper the line, the smaller the resistance (more current for each volt). Such components are called **ohmic** or **linear**.

![Figure 3.2 — Voltage–current graphs. Left: an ohmic resistor gives a straight line through the origin. Centre: a filament lamp's line curves over, because its resistance rises as it heats up. Right: a diode passes almost no current until about 0.6 V, then a great deal.](fig/ch03_vi.svg){width=100%}

Not everything behaves like that. The centre graph shows a **filament lamp**. At low voltages the filament is cool and its resistance is low; as the voltage rises the filament heats up to white heat and its resistance increases several times over. The current still rises, but less and less steeply. The lamp does not obey Ohm's law over its full range, because the condition "temperature stays the same" is badly broken. (The dashed line shows what the current *would* be if the resistance stayed at its cold value; this is why filament lamps tended to fail at the moment of switch-on, when the surge of current was greatest.)

The right-hand graph shows a **diode**, a semiconductor component that lets current through in only one direction. Almost nothing flows until the voltage reaches about 0.6 V for a silicon diode, after which the current rises extremely steeply. A diode is very definitely not ohmic.

Does this mean Ohm's law is wrong? No. It means Ohm's law is a statement about a particular kind of component – a linear resistor – and not a universal law of nature in the way that conservation of energy is. However, the relationship R = V / I can still be used *at any single operating point* to describe a non-linear component's behaviour at that moment. And many important components, including the resistors that make up most of the circuits in this book, are linear to a very good approximation.

::: tip
**Why Linearity Matters So Much**

Almost every method in Parts Four and Five of this book – nodal analysis, mesh analysis, superposition, Thévenin and Norton – relies on the circuit being **linear**: built from components whose voltage is proportional to their current. That is why we spend so long on resistors. Once you understand linear circuits thoroughly, you will learn special techniques for diodes and transistors that are built on exactly the same foundations.
:::

## 3.3 Power in a Resistor

In Chapter 2 we found that the power delivered to any component is P = V × I. For a resistor, we can combine this with Ohm's law to get two more very useful forms.

Substitute V = IR into P = VI:

::: eq
P = (I R) × I = I²R
:::

Or substitute I = V/R:

::: eq
P = V × {{frac|V|R}} = {{frac|V²|R}}
:::

::: keyeq
**Power in a resistor**

P = V I = I²R = {{frac|V²|R}}

Use whichever form contains the two quantities you already know.
:::

All three give the same answer; the choice depends on what you know. If you know the current, I²R is usually quickest. If you know the voltage, V²/R saves a step.

The I²R form deserves special attention, because it carries a big message: **power loss rises with the square of the current**. Double the current through a cable and the heat produced in it goes up four times. This single fact explains why the National Grid transmits power at 400 000 volts: for the same power, a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the I²R losses in the lines fall dramatically.

The same is true from the other direction. P = V²/R tells us that for a fixed resistance, power rises with the square of the voltage. A small change in supply voltage makes a bigger change in power, as the next example shows.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.4 — Will the resistor survive?**

A 470 Ω, 0.25 W resistor is connected directly across a 12 V supply. Is it safe?

**Step 1: Calculate the power.** We know V and R, so use P = V² / R.

P = {{frac|12²|470}} = {{frac|144|470}} = 0.306 W

**Step 2: Compare with the rating.** 0.306 W is greater than 0.25 W. The resistor would run too hot and would probably fail, possibly after discolouring the board around it.

**Step 3: Choose a better part.** A 0.5 W resistor would be within its rating. Experienced designers usually go further and keep a resistor below about half of its rating, especially inside a warm enclosure, so a 1 W resistor would be a comfortable choice here.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.5 — The kettle element**

What is the resistance of the element of a 3 kW, 230 V kettle, and what current does it draw?

**Solution.** We know P and V, so rearrange P = V² / R to make R the subject: R = V² / P.

R = {{frac|230²|3000}} = {{frac|52 900|3000}} = 17.6 Ω

I = V / R = 230 / 17.6 = 13.0 A, which agrees with Worked Example 2.6.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.6 — Sensitivity to supply voltage**

A 1 kW heater designed for 230 V is used where the supply has sagged by 10 % to 207 V. Assuming its resistance stays the same, what power does it now produce?

**Solution.** Because P = V² / R and R is constant, power is proportional to V². The voltage ratio is 207 / 230 = 0.9, so the power ratio is 0.9² = 0.81.

P = 0.81 × 1000 = 810 W.

**Comment.** A 10 % fall in voltage has produced a 19 % fall in power. Squared relationships always magnify changes in this way.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.7 — Working back from power**

A 10 Ω resistor is dissipating 2.5 W. What current flows through it, and what voltage is across it?

**Solution.** From P = I²R, I² = P / R = 2.5 / 10 = 0.25, so I = √0.25 = 0.5 A. Then V = IR = 0.5 × 10 = 5 V.

**Check.** P = VI = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 W. ✓
:::

The tick at the end of Example 3.7 is a habit I would like you to adopt. Whenever you have found several quantities, check them against a relationship you *did not* use to find them. It takes seconds, and it catches a surprising number of slips.

## 3.4 Putting Ohm's Law to Work

Formulae are only useful if you can apply them to real problems. The rest of this chapter takes three everyday engineering situations and works through them in detail.

### Choosing a resistor for an LED

Light-emitting diodes are now everywhere: indicator lights, torches, displays, car lamps, room lighting. Like all diodes they are non-linear, and they have an awkward property: once the voltage across an LED reaches its operating value, a tiny further increase causes a very large increase in current. Connect an LED straight across a battery and it will usually destroy itself in a flash.

The standard solution is to put a resistor in series with the LED, as in Figure 3.3. The LED holds its own voltage roughly constant at its **forward voltage** – about 2 V for a red LED, rather more for blue or white – and the resistor takes up the rest of the supply voltage. Since the resistor *is* ohmic, it sets the current.

![Figure 3.3 — An LED with a current-limiting resistor. The LED holds about 2 V across itself; the resistor takes the remaining voltage and, through Ohm's law, fixes the current.](fig/ch03_led.svg){width=62%}

::: example
**Worked Example 3.8 — Designing an LED indicator**

A red LED has a forward voltage of 2.0 V and should be run at 20 mA. It is powered from a 9 V battery. Choose a suitable series resistor from the E12 series and specify its power rating.

**Step 1: Voltage across the resistor.** The supply is 9 V and the LED takes 2 V, so the resistor must take the remainder (we will justify this formally with Kirchhoff's voltage law in Chapter 5):

V~R~ = 9 − 2 = 7 V

**Step 2: Resistance needed.** The same 20 mA flows through the resistor as through the LED.

R = {{frac|V~R~|I}} = {{frac|7|0.020}} = 350 Ω

**Step 3: Choose a preferred value.** 350 Ω is not an E12 value. The neighbours are 330 Ω and 390 Ω. A 330 Ω resistor would give 7 / 330 = 21.2 mA, slightly over the target; 390 Ω gives 7 / 390 = 17.9 mA, slightly under. Running an LED slightly below its rated current costs a barely visible amount of brightness and extends its life, so choose **390 Ω**.

**Step 4: Power rating.** P = V² / R = 7² / 390 = 0.126 W. A standard 0.25 W resistor is fine, at about half its rating.

**Answer:** a 390 Ω, 0.25 W resistor, giving about 18 mA.
:::

This little problem contains the whole of practical design in miniature: an ideal calculation, a real-world compromise, a check of the consequences and a safety margin. You will do the same thing again and again.

### Voltage drop in a cable

In Worked Example 2.1 we found that 50 m of 1.5 mm² twin cable has a loop resistance of about 1.15 Ω. That sounds tiny. But suppose someone plugs a 3 kW heater into a socket at the end of it, as shown in Figure 3.4.

![Figure 3.4 — A long cable has resistance in both the outgoing and the return conductor. These resistances are in series with the load, so some of the supply voltage is lost along the way.](fig/ch03_cable.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 3.9 — How much voltage reaches the heater?**

Using the heater resistance from Worked Example 3.5 (17.6 Ω) and a cable loop resistance of 1.15 Ω, find (a) the current, (b) the voltage at the heater, (c) the voltage lost in the cable and (d) the power wasted in the cable.

**Step 1: Total resistance.** The cable and the heater are in one loop, so the same current flows through both. Their resistances add (we will prove this in Chapter 6):

R~total~ = 17.63 + 1.15 = 18.78 Ω

**Step 2: Current.** I = 230 / 18.78 = 12.25 A.

**Step 3: Voltage at the heater.** V~heater~ = I × R~heater~ = 12.25 × 17.63 = 216 V.

**Step 4: Voltage lost in the cable.** V~cable~ = I × R~cable~ = 12.25 × 1.15 = 14.0 V. As a check, 216 + 14 = 230 V. ✓

**Step 5: Power wasted.** P = I²R = 12.25² × 1.15 = 172 W.

**Comment.** The cable loses about 6 % of the supply voltage and wastes 172 W as heat along its length. UK wiring practice generally limits the voltage drop in final circuits to a few per cent, so this cable is too thin for the job. A 4 mm² cable would cut the loss by more than half. This is exactly the kind of calculation an electrician makes when choosing a cable size.
:::

## 3.5 Short Circuits, Open Circuits and Fuses

Ohm's law also explains two extreme conditions that every engineer must recognise.

An **open circuit** is a break in the path – a disconnected wire, a blown fuse, an open switch, a cracked solder joint. Its resistance is effectively infinite. From I = V / R, an infinite resistance gives zero current, *whatever the voltage*. Notice the last phrase. An open circuit can have a large voltage across it while carrying no current at all. That is why the contacts of an open switch on a mains circuit are still dangerous to touch.

A **short circuit** is an unintended path of very low resistance – two bare wires touching, a screwdriver slipping across terminals, a solder bridge on a board. From I = V / R, a resistance close to zero gives a very large current. The current is limited only by the small resistance of the wiring and the source itself, and it can easily reach hundreds or thousands of amps. Wires overheat, insulation melts, and fires start.

This is why circuits are protected by **fuses** and **circuit breakers**. A fuse contains a thin wire designed to melt when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit before the wiring can overheat. In the UK, plug-top fuses are made in 3 A, 5 A and 13 A ratings. Choosing one is a direct application of P = VI.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.10 — Choosing a plug fuse**

Choose the correct plug fuse for (a) a 500 W table lamp and (b) a 2.2 kW iron, both on a 230 V supply.

**(a)** I = P / V = 500 / 230 = 2.2 A. The smallest standard fuse larger than this is **3 A**.

**(b)** I = 2200 / 230 = 9.6 A. The 3 A and 5 A fuses are too small, so use **13 A**.

**Comment.** The fuse should be rated a little above the normal operating current, but no higher than necessary. A 13 A fuse in a table lamp's plug would allow a fault current large enough to overheat the lamp's thin flex before the fuse blew.
:::

## 3.6 Real Sources: EMF and Internal Resistance

Here is an experiment you can do with an old torch battery and a multimeter. Measure the voltage across the battery on its own: perhaps 1.55 V. Now connect a small lamp or resistor across it and measure again: perhaps 1.40 V. The voltage has dropped as soon as the battery has been asked to supply current. The more current you draw, the more it drops.

The explanation is that a real battery is not a perfect source of voltage. The chemicals and connections inside it have some resistance of their own, called **internal resistance**, symbol **r**. We model a real battery as a perfect voltage source in series with that small resistance, as shown inside the dashed box in Figure 3.5.

![Figure 3.5 — A model of a real battery: an ideal source of EMF E in series with an internal resistance r. When a load draws current, some of the EMF is lost across r, so the terminal voltage V is less than E.](fig/ch03_internal.svg){width=62%}

The voltage of the perfect source is called the **electromotive force**, or **EMF**, symbol **E**. It is the voltage you measure with no load connected, when no current flows and so nothing is lost across r. When a current I flows, a voltage Ir is lost inside the battery, and the voltage at the terminals is

::: keyeq
**Terminal voltage of a real source**

V = E − I r

where E is the EMF, I is the current drawn and r is the internal resistance.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.11 — A torch cell under load**

An AA cell has an EMF of 1.5 V and an internal resistance of 0.2 Ω. It is connected to a 2.8 Ω load. Find the current and the terminal voltage.

**Step 1: Total resistance.** The internal resistance and the load are in one loop: R~total~ = 0.2 + 2.8 = 3.0 Ω.

**Step 2: Current.** I = E / R~total~ = 1.5 / 3.0 = 0.5 A.

**Step 3: Terminal voltage.** V = E − Ir = 1.5 − 0.5 × 0.2 = 1.4 V.

**Check.** The voltage across the load should be I × R~L~ = 0.5 × 2.8 = 1.4 V. ✓
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 3.12 — Why the lights dim when you start the car**

A car battery has an EMF of 12.6 V and an internal resistance of 0.02 Ω. The starter motor draws 150 A. What is the terminal voltage while cranking?

**Solution.** V = E − Ir = 12.6 − 150 × 0.02 = 12.6 − 3.0 = 9.6 V.

**Comment.** Every other circuit in the car – headlights, dashboard, radio – shares that terminal voltage, so they all see a sudden drop from 12.6 V to 9.6 V. That is the dimming you notice. In cold weather the battery's internal resistance rises and the starter draws more current, which is why flat batteries reveal themselves on winter mornings.
:::

You can also turn the idea round and *measure* internal resistance. Measure the open-circuit voltage to get E. Then connect a known load, measure the terminal voltage and calculate the current. The difference between the two voltages, divided by the current, is r.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.13 — Measuring internal resistance**

A 9 V battery reads 9.2 V with nothing connected. With a 100 Ω resistor across it, it reads 8.9 V. Find its internal resistance.

**Step 1: EMF.** E = 9.2 V (no current, so no internal drop).

**Step 2: Current with the load.** I = V / R~L~ = 8.9 / 100 = 0.089 A.

**Step 3: Internal resistance.** The voltage lost inside is 9.2 − 8.9 = 0.3 V, so r = 0.3 / 0.089 = 3.4 Ω.
:::

Hold on to this model. It is the first appearance of an idea that will grow into Thévenin's theorem in Chapter 14: that *any* linear circuit, however complicated, looks from the outside exactly like a single voltage source in series with a single resistance. The battery is simply the most familiar example.

## 3.7 Measuring Resistance

Most multimeters have a resistance range, usually marked with Ω. In this mode the meter passes a small known current through the component and measures the voltage across it, then uses R = V / I to display the resistance. Three rules make the reading trustworthy:

1. **The circuit must be switched off**, and any large capacitors discharged. The meter supplies its own small current; any other voltage in the circuit will upset the reading and may damage the meter.
2. **The component should be isolated.** If a resistor is still soldered into a board, other components connected to it provide extra paths for the meter's current, and the reading will be too low. Lift one end of the component before measuring if you need an accurate value.
3. **Do not hold both ends with your fingers.** Your body has a resistance of perhaps 100 kΩ or more between your hands, and it appears in parallel with the component. For a 100 Ω resistor that makes no difference; for a 1 MΩ resistor it makes a big one.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Mixing up components.** V, I and R in Ohm's law must all refer to the same component.
- **Forgetting prefixes.** 2.2 kΩ is 2200 Ω, and 20 mA is 0.020 A.
- **Using Ohm's law on non-ohmic components** as if R were constant. Lamps, diodes and LEDs need different treatment.
- **Ignoring the power rating.** Every time you calculate a resistor value, calculate its power as well.
- **Assuming a battery's voltage is fixed.** Real sources lose voltage across their internal resistance when they deliver current.
- **Measuring resistance in a live circuit.** Always switch off and isolate.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Ohm's law**: V = IR, so I = V/R and R = V/I. One ohm is one volt per amp.
- An **ohmic** (linear) component gives a straight-line V–I graph through the origin. Lamps and diodes are non-ohmic.
- **Power in a resistor**: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. Power rises with the square of current or voltage.
- Always check a resistor's **power rating**, ideally keeping below about half of it.
- An **LED** needs a series resistor: R = (V~supply~ − V~LED~) / I.
- **Cable resistance** causes voltage drop and power loss, which grow with current.
- An **open circuit** has infinite resistance and zero current; a **short circuit** has near-zero resistance and a dangerously large current. **Fuses** protect against excess current.
- A **real source** behaves like an EMF E in series with internal resistance r: V = E − Ir.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

V = IR     I = V / R     R = V / I     P = VI = I²R = V² / R     V = E − Ir
:::

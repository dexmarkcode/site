# Electrical Fundamentals for High-Voltage Vehicles

::: chapter-intro
This chapter gathers the electrical ideas that the rest of the book takes for granted: energy and charge, resistance and heating, capacitors and inductors, alternating current and three-phase power, magnetism, and the principle behind every switching converter. It finishes with the subject that matters most to anyone who touches an electric vehicle, which is how high-voltage systems are made safe and how you keep yourself safe around them.
:::

Engineers who come to electric vehicles from mechanical backgrounds sometimes tell me that the electrical side feels like a different language. In a sense it is. But it is a language with a small vocabulary and a very consistent grammar, and most of the confusion I see comes from a handful of ideas that were never quite nailed down: the difference between amp-hours and watt-hours, why current matters so much more than voltage for heating, what a capacitor is actually doing in an inverter, and what "three-phase" means in practice.

If you studied electrical engineering, much of this chapter will be revision, and you may want to move quickly to Sections 3.6 and 3.7 on switching and safety. If you did not, take your time. Every later chapter is built on these foundations.

## Charge, Energy and Power

Electric current is the flow of charge. One ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point each second. Voltage is the energy each coulomb carries: one volt means one joule per coulomb. Multiply them and the coulombs cancel, leaving joules per second, or watts:

$$ P = V I $$ {3.1}

This is the most useful equation in the book. It tells you, for instance, that a 400 V battery delivering 150 kW is supplying 375 A, and that the same power at 800 V needs only 187.5 A.

### Amp-hours and watt-hours

Battery capacity is quoted in two ways, and mixing them up is the commonest mistake I see in student work. The *charge capacity* of a cell, in ampere-hours (Ah), says how much charge it can deliver: a 50 Ah cell can supply 50 A for one hour, or 25 A for two hours, in principle. The *energy* stored, in watt-hours (Wh), is the charge multiplied by the average voltage at which it is delivered:

$$ E = Q\, V_{nom} $$ {3.2}

A 50 Ah cell with a nominal voltage of 3.65 V stores about 182.5 Wh. Connecting cells in series adds their voltages but not their amp-hours; connecting them in parallel adds amp-hours but not voltage. Energy adds in both cases.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.1 — Sizing a pack from cells**

An engineer needs a battery of roughly 70 kWh with a nominal voltage near 350 V, using prismatic cells rated at 3.65 V and 100 Ah. How should the cells be arranged?

*Solution.* Cells in series: 350 / 3.65 = 95.9, so choose 96 in series (written 96s). Nominal pack voltage = 96 × 3.65 = 350.4 V.

Energy of one series string: 350.4 V × 100 Ah = 35.0 kWh. Two strings in parallel (2p) give 70.1 kWh.

The pack is 96s2p: 192 cells, 350 V nominal, 200 Ah, 70 kWh. Alternatively, a 200 Ah cell in a 96s1p arrangement would achieve the same result with half as many cells and fewer connections, which is why large-format cells have become popular. A 192s1p arrangement of the original 100 Ah cell would give the same energy at 700 V.
:::

The *C-rate* is a convenient way to express current relative to capacity. A current of 1C fully charges or discharges the cell in about one hour; for a 100 Ah cell, 1C is 100 A, 2C is 200 A and C/3 is 33 A. Because it is normalised, the C-rate allows you to compare cells of different sizes. For a whole pack, the C-rate in terms of power is often more useful: a 70 kWh pack delivering 210 kW is running at 3C.

## Resistance and Heating

Every conductor resists current to some degree. Ohm's law relates voltage, current and resistance:

$$ V = I R $$ {3.3}

Combining Equations 3.1 and 3.3 gives the power dissipated as heat in a resistance:

$$ P_{loss} = I^2 R $$ {3.4}

The square on the current is the reason high-voltage architectures exist. Doubling the current for a given conductor quadruples the heat. Everything in an EV that carries current, from the cells themselves to busbars, connectors, fuses, contactors, cables, semiconductor switches and motor windings, obeys Equation 3.4, and managing that heat is a large part of the engineering.

### Conductors and busbars

The resistance of a conductor of length *l*, cross-sectional area *A* and resistivity *ρ* is:

$$ R = \frac{\rho\, l}{A} $$ {3.5}

Copper has a resistivity of about 1.72 × 10^−8^ Ω·m at 20 °C, and aluminium about 2.82 × 10^−8^ Ω·m. Aluminium has roughly 60 % of copper's conductivity but only 30 % of its density, so for the same resistance an aluminium conductor is about half the mass, though larger. Many battery packs use aluminium busbars for that reason, and cell terminals are frequently aluminium on one side and copper on the other, which creates the jointing challenges we meet in Chapter 5.

Resistivity rises with temperature. For copper, the temperature coefficient *α* is about 0.0039 per kelvin:

$$ R_T = R_{20}\,[1 + \alpha (T - 20)] $$ {3.6}

A copper busbar at 90 °C has about 27 % more resistance than at 20 °C. This creates a positive feedback: more current, more heat, higher resistance, more heat still. Thermal design must make sure the loop settles at a safe temperature.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.2 — A busbar under load**

A copper busbar inside a battery pack is 0.8 m long with a cross-section of 30 mm × 5 mm. It carries 500 A during hard acceleration. Find its resistance and power loss at 20 °C and at 90 °C.

*Solution.* Area *A* = 30 × 5 = 150 mm^2^ = 150 × 10^−6^ m^2^.

*R*~20~ = 1.72 × 10^−8^ × 0.8 / 150 × 10^−6^ = 91.7 μΩ.

*R*~90~ = 91.7 × [1 + 0.0039 × 70] = 116.7 μΩ.

Losses at 500 A: *P* = 500^2^ × 91.7 × 10^−6^ = 22.9 W at 20 °C, and 29.2 W at 90 °C.

Thirty watts sounds modest, but it is concentrated in a small bar inside a sealed box, and there may be dozens of such bars and several hundred joints, each adding contact resistance. A badly made bolted joint with 100 μΩ of extra resistance would dissipate 25 W at the same current in a few square centimetres. Hot joints are one of the classic failure modes of battery packs.
:::

### Skin effect

At DC, current spreads evenly across a conductor's cross-section. With alternating current, the changing magnetic field inside the conductor pushes current towards the surface, an effect called *skin effect*. The *skin depth*, within which most of the current flows, is about 9 mm in copper at 50 Hz but only about 0.5 mm at 20 kHz. This matters in two places in an EV: in motor windings at high electrical frequency, which we discuss in Chapter 8, and in the busbars and capacitors of an inverter, where switching harmonics at tens or hundreds of kilohertz flow.

## Capacitors and Inductors

Resistors turn electrical energy into heat. Capacitors and inductors store it, and they are the components that make power conversion possible.

### Capacitors

A capacitor stores energy in an electric field between two conductors separated by an insulator. The charge it holds is proportional to the voltage across it, *Q* = *CV*, and the energy it stores is:

$$ E_C = \frac{1}{2}\, C V^2 $$ {3.7}

The current into a capacitor is proportional to the *rate of change* of its voltage:

$$ i = C\, \frac{dv}{dt} $$ {3.8}

So a capacitor resists sudden changes of voltage. Connect a discharged capacitor directly to a battery and Equation 3.8 demands an enormous current, limited only by the resistance of the wires.

Every inverter, DC-DC converter and on-board charger has a large capacitor across its DC input, called the *DC-link capacitor*. Its job is to supply the rapidly pulsing current drawn by the switches, so that the battery and the cables see a smoother current. In a traction inverter this capacitor is typically several hundred microfarads to a couple of millifarads, usually made from metallised polypropylene film because of its low losses and good reliability.

### Pre-charge

That capacitor creates a problem when the car wakes up. If the main contactors closed directly onto an empty DC-link capacitor, the inrush current could reach thousands of amps for a few microseconds, enough to weld the contacts or damage the capacitor. The solution is the pre-charge circuit shown in Figure 3.1.

![**Figure 3.1** Pre-charge circuit and waveform. (a) The pre-charge contactor and resistor charge the DC-link capacitor before the main positive contactor closes. (b) The capacitor voltage rises exponentially with time constant τ = RC; the main contactor closes once the voltage reaches about 95 % of the battery voltage.](figures/fig3_1.png)

When the pre-charge contactor closes, the capacitor charges through the resistor *R*, and its voltage rises exponentially:

$$ v_C(t) = V_{batt}\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) $$ {3.9}

After one time constant *τ* = *RC*, the capacitor reaches 63 % of the battery voltage; after three, 95 %; after five, over 99 %. The BMS monitors the voltage on the inverter side and closes the main contactor only when the difference is small enough, typically within 5 to 10 % or a few tens of volts. If the voltage fails to rise within the expected time, something is wrong: a short circuit, a missing capacitor, a failed resistor or an extra load. The BMS then aborts and reports a pre-charge fault. You will meet this fault in Case File 3.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.3 — Designing a pre-charge resistor**

A 400 V system has a total DC-link capacitance of 1 000 μF, spread across the inverter, DC-DC converter and on-board charger. The vehicle must be ready to drive within 300 ms of the pre-charge starting, and the capacitor must reach 95 % of battery voltage. Choose *R*, and find the peak current and the energy dissipated in the resistor.

*Solution.* Reaching 95 % takes three time constants, so 3*τ* = 0.3 s and *τ* = 0.1 s. Then *R* = *τ* / *C* = 0.1 / 0.001 = 100 Ω.

The peak current occurs at the instant of closing, when the capacitor is empty: *I*~pk~ = 400 / 100 = 4 A. The peak power in the resistor is 400 × 4 = 1 600 W, but only for an instant.

A neat result from circuit theory says that charging a capacitor through a resistor from a fixed voltage always dissipates in the resistor exactly the energy finally stored in the capacitor, regardless of the value of *R*:

*E*~R~ = ½ *CV*^2^ = 0.5 × 0.001 × 400^2^ = 80 J.

The resistor must absorb 80 J in a fraction of a second without overheating, and it must survive repeated attempts if pre-charge is retried. Wire-wound or thick-film resistors with high pulse ratings are used. For comparison, without pre-charge, if the circuit resistance were 20 mΩ, the initial current would be 400 / 0.02 = 20 000 A.
:::

### Inductors

An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field, usually in a coil of wire wound on a magnetic core. The voltage across it is proportional to the rate of change of current through it:

$$ v = L\, \frac{di}{dt} $$ {3.10}

and the energy stored is:

$$ E_L = \frac{1}{2}\, L I^2 $$ {3.11}

An inductor is the mirror image of a capacitor: it resists sudden changes of *current*. Try to interrupt the current in an inductor quickly and Equation 3.10 produces a large voltage spike. That is how an ignition coil works, and it is also why every switching circuit must give inductor current somewhere to go when a switch opens. Motor windings are large inductors, and so are the stray inductances of busbars and cables. Much of power electronics, as we shall see, is about managing those spikes.

## Alternating Current and Three-Phase Power

The battery supplies direct current. The motor needs alternating current, and the grid supplies it. So an EV engineer must be fluent in AC.

### Sinusoids, RMS and phase

A sinusoidal voltage can be written *v*(*t*) = *V*~pk~ sin(ω*t*), where *V*~pk~ is the peak value and ω = 2π*f* is the angular frequency. Because the voltage is constantly changing, we describe its heating effect with the *root-mean-square* (RMS) value, which for a sine wave is:

$$ V_{rms} = \frac{V_{pk}}{\sqrt{2}} $$ {3.12}

The UK mains supply is nominally 230 V RMS, which means its peak is about 325 V. This matters when you design a charger: the rectified mains peaks at 325 V on a single-phase supply even though the "voltage" is 230 V.

When a load contains inductance or capacitance, the current is shifted in time relative to the voltage by a phase angle φ. Only the component of current in phase with the voltage transfers real power. The *power factor* cos φ expresses this:

$$ P = V_{rms}\, I_{rms} \cos\varphi $$ {3.13}

### Three-phase systems

Almost every traction motor, and most grid connections for fast charging, use three phases. A three-phase system is three sinusoidal voltages of equal amplitude, displaced from each other by 120°, as shown in Figure 3.2.

![**Figure 3.2** Three-phase voltages. (a) The three waveforms are displaced by 120°; at every instant they sum to zero. (b) The same voltages drawn as rotating phasors. The line-to-line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage.](figures/fig3_2.png)

Three-phase power has three great virtues. First, for a balanced load the total instantaneous power is constant, not pulsating, so a three-phase motor produces smooth torque. Second, the three currents sum to zero, so in principle no return conductor is needed, saving copper. Third, the three phases together create a magnetic field that rotates in space, which is exactly what an AC motor needs. We return to that rotating field in Chapter 7.

The voltage between any two lines is √3 times the voltage of each phase measured to the star point. For a balanced load the total power is:

$$ P = \sqrt{3}\, V_{LL}\, I_L \cos\varphi $$ {3.14}

where *V*~LL~ is the line-to-line RMS voltage and *I*~L~ the line current.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.4 — Motor phase current**

A traction motor delivers 100 kW of mechanical power at 94 % efficiency. The inverter supplies it with a line-to-line voltage of 260 V RMS at a power factor of 0.88. What is the phase current?

*Solution.* Electrical input power = 100 / 0.94 = 106.4 kW.

From Equation 3.14: *I*~L~ = 106 400 / (√3 × 260 × 0.88) = 106 400 / 396.3 = 268 A RMS.

The peak phase current is √2 × 268 = 379 A. The inverter's switches must carry this peak, plus margin for transients, which is why inverter ratings are usually quoted in peak or RMS amps rather than kilowatts.
:::

### Star and delta

The three windings of a motor or transformer can be connected in *star* (also called wye), with one end of each winding joined at a common point, or in *delta*, with the windings connected end to end in a triangle. Most traction motors are star-connected because it avoids circulating currents and simplifies control. Delta connection appears in some industrial machines and in transformers, and a few vehicle drives switch between the two to extend the speed range.

## Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

Motors, inductors and transformers all depend on two facts of nature discovered in the nineteenth century.

The first is that a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force. For a straight conductor of length *l* carrying current *I* at right angles to a field of flux density *B*:

$$ F = B I l $$ {3.15}

This is the *Lorentz force*, and it is how a motor makes torque.

The second is Faraday's law: a changing magnetic flux through a coil induces a voltage in it. For a coil of *N* turns linking flux *Φ*:

$$ e = -N\, \frac{d\Phi}{dt} $$ {3.16}

This is how a generator works, and it is also why a spinning motor produces a *back-EMF* that opposes the supply voltage. The faster the motor spins, the larger the back-EMF, which is ultimately why every electric drive runs out of voltage at high speed and must enter the constant-power region we met in Chapter 2.

### Magnetic materials and losses

Motors and inductors use iron-based cores to concentrate magnetic flux. Silicon steel, rolled into thin laminations typically 0.2 to 0.35 mm thick and coated with insulation, is the standard material for motor stators and rotors. The laminations exist to fight *eddy currents*: Faraday's law induces circulating currents in any conductor exposed to a changing field, and the core itself is a conductor. Thin, insulated laminations break up those current paths.

Even so, cores lose energy in two ways each time the field changes. *Hysteresis loss* comes from the energy needed to realign the magnetic domains in the material, and it is proportional to frequency. *Eddy-current loss* is proportional roughly to the square of frequency and the square of lamination thickness. At the high electrical frequencies of modern traction motors, often over 1 kHz at top speed, core losses become a significant part of the total, and lamination thickness becomes an important design choice. Chapter 8 explores this trade.

## The Principle of Switching Power Conversion

How do you turn 400 V DC into 12 V DC, or into a three-phase AC wave of variable frequency, without wasting most of the power as heat? The answer, which underlies every converter in the vehicle, is to use switches and energy-storage components rather than resistors.

Imagine you want to supply a load with half of the battery voltage. You could put a resistor in series to drop the other half, but that resistor would dissipate as much power as the load. Instead, connect the load through a switch that turns on and off very rapidly, spending half its time on. The *average* voltage seen by the load is then half the battery voltage, and an ideal switch dissipates no power: when it is on, there is current but no voltage across it; when it is off, there is voltage but no current.

The pulsed voltage is useless to most loads, so we smooth it with an inductor and a capacitor. Figure 3.3 shows the result, a *buck converter*. The fraction of time the switch is on is the *duty cycle*, *D*, and in steady state the output voltage is:

$$ V_{out} = D\, V_{in} $$ {3.17}

![**Figure 3.3** The buck converter, the simplest switching converter. (a) Circuit: when the switch is on, current flows from the source through the inductor; when it is off, the diode carries the inductor current. (b) The switch-node voltage is a pulse train whose average equals *D*V~in~; the inductor current ripples around the load current.](figures/fig3_3.png)

This is pulse-width modulation (PWM), and it is the foundation of power electronics. A traction inverter is, in essence, three of these switching legs whose duty cycles vary sinusoidally, so that the average voltage on each motor terminal traces out a sine wave. A DC-DC converter for the 12 V system is a more sophisticated version of the buck converter, usually with a transformer for isolation. An on-board charger uses switching stages to draw a sinusoidal current from the mains and to regulate the battery charging current.

Real switches are not ideal. They have a small resistance when on, and they take a finite time to turn on and off, during which both voltage and current are present and power is lost. Those *conduction losses* and *switching losses* are the main inefficiencies of power electronics, and Chapter 10 examines them in detail. Choosing the switching frequency is always a compromise: higher frequency allows smaller inductors and capacitors but increases switching losses.

## High-Voltage Safety

Now to the most important section of this chapter. A traction battery is lethal. It cannot be switched off in the way a mains circuit can, because the cells always hold their voltage. It can deliver thousands of amps into a short circuit. And it is often physically close to people: under the floor, behind the seats, next to the crash structure.

The design of the vehicle must therefore make the high-voltage system safe under normal use, under single faults, in a crash and during servicing. Regulations such as UN Regulation No. 100 for the electrical safety of electric vehicles and international standards such as ISO 6469 set out the requirements. You do not need to memorise them, but you should understand the principles, because they shape every high-voltage component.

### The floating (IT) system

The high-voltage system in a vehicle is not connected to the vehicle chassis. Both the positive and negative poles are insulated from it. Electrical engineers call this an *IT system*, borrowing the terminology of industrial power distribution. Its great advantage is that a single insulation fault, such as one pole touching the chassis, does not create a dangerous current path. To get a shock, a person would have to touch both poles at once, or touch one pole while the other has also failed to the chassis.

![**Figure 3.4** The floating high-voltage system. Both battery poles are isolated from the chassis; the isolation resistances *R*~iso+~ and *R*~iso−~ represent the insulation. An isolation monitoring device measures them continuously. All exposed conductive parts are bonded to the chassis.](figures/fig3_4.png)

That advantage only holds while the insulation remains healthy, which is why every EV has an *isolation monitoring device*, usually built into the BMS. It measures the resistance between each pole and the chassis, typically by switching a known resistance between them and observing how the voltages shift. UN Regulation No. 100 requires the isolation resistance of DC circuits to be at least 100 Ω per volt of working voltage, and of AC circuits at least 500 Ω per volt, unless additional protection is provided. If the measured value falls towards that limit, the vehicle warns the driver and may refuse to close its contactors.

::: example
**Worked Example 3.5 — What the isolation limit means**

An 800 V system has degraded insulation between its negative pole and the chassis, measured at the regulatory minimum of 100 Ω/V. A mechanic standing on the ground touches the positive pole. Assuming the human body and contact resistance is 1 kΩ and the mechanic is effectively connected to the chassis potential, estimate the current through the body.

*Solution.* Minimum isolation resistance = 100 × 800 = 80 kΩ. The current path runs from the positive pole, through the body to chassis, then through the 80 kΩ fault back to the negative pole:

*I* = 800 / (80 000 + 1 000) ≈ 9.9 mA.

The limit is chosen so that even with one insulation fault at the minimum acceptable value, a single contact cannot drive more than about 10 mA through a person. That is unpleasant but well below the currents associated with heart fibrillation for direct current. With a dead short in place of the 80 kΩ fault, the current would be 800 mA, very probably fatal. This is why an isolation warning must never be ignored.
:::

### Protective measures

Several further layers of protection surround the floating system:

- **Insulation and enclosures.** All live parts are inside enclosures that require tools to open, with insulation rated for the working voltage plus transients.
- **Equipotential bonding.** Every metal enclosure of a high-voltage component is connected to the chassis by a low-resistance bond, so that a fault inside it cannot make the case live relative to the body of the car.
- **Orange cables.** High-voltage cables are coloured orange so technicians can identify them.
- **High-voltage interlock loop (HVIL).** A low-voltage signal loop runs through every high-voltage connector and service cover. Unplug any of them and the loop breaks; the BMS opens the contactors within milliseconds.
- **Active and passive discharge.** When the contactors open, the DC-link capacitors still hold their charge. An active discharge circuit in the inverter drains them quickly, typically to below 60 V within a few seconds, and a passive bleed resistor backs it up more slowly in case the active circuit fails.
- **Crash disconnection.** The airbag controller signals the BMS to open the contactors on impact, and some designs add a pyrotechnic fuse that physically cuts the battery circuit.
- **Fuses.** A main fuse inside the pack, and smaller fuses for each auxiliary load, interrupt short-circuit currents.
- **Service disconnect.** A manually removable plug or switch splits the battery into two halves of lower voltage for servicing.

### Working safely

No book can make you competent to work on high-voltage systems; that requires formal training and supervised practice. But the principles of safe isolation are worth stating because they explain why technicians do what they do.

Before work begins, the vehicle is made safe: switched off, key removed and kept away, low-voltage battery disconnected, service disconnect removed and secured, and a waiting time observed for capacitors to discharge. Then the absence of voltage is *proved* with an approved voltage tester that has itself been checked on a known live source before and after the test. Only then is the system treated as dead. Throughout, the technician uses insulated tools and appropriate gloves and works with a trained colleague nearby. Rated test equipment matters too: multimeters and probes used on traction systems must carry an appropriate measurement category rating, typically CAT III 1000 V or better.

::: note
**Engineer's Note**

If you design high-voltage components, design for the technician who will one day work on them tired, in a cold workshop, with incomplete information. Put the service disconnect where it can be reached. Make the discharge time short. Label everything. The people who will use your product are not reading your design documents.
:::

## Measuring Electrical Quantities

Much of EV engineering is measurement, and a few practical points save a great deal of grief.

*Current* in high-voltage systems is measured with shunt resistors, Hall-effect sensors or fluxgate sensors. A shunt is a precision low-value resistor, often 25 to 100 μΩ, across which the voltage drop is measured; it is accurate and cheap but sits at high voltage and dissipates power. Hall-effect sensors measure the magnetic field around a conductor, so they are isolated, but they drift with temperature and have an offset error. Fluxgate sensors are highly accurate and are used where precise charge counting matters. The choice affects the accuracy of state-of-charge estimation, as we shall see in Chapter 6: a 0.5 % offset error in a current sensor can accumulate into a large charge error over a day.

*Voltage* across individual cells is measured by dedicated cell-monitoring chips, accurate to a millivolt or two. Pack voltage is measured across high-value divider resistors on the isolated side of the BMS.

*Waveforms* are captured with oscilloscopes, but a standard scope probe's ground lead is connected to the scope's earth. Connecting it to a floating high-voltage node can short that node to earth through the scope, which is dangerous to you and to the equipment. Differential probes rated for the voltage are essential, and for fast switching measurements in inverters, specialised isolated probes are needed.

::: casefile
**Case File 3 — The intermittent pre-charge fault**

*The complaint.* A fleet of light commercial EVs starts reporting intermittent "unable to start" events with a stored pre-charge timeout fault. The events cluster on cold, damp mornings and the vehicles usually start normally on a second attempt.

*State it in numbers.* Over three weeks, 41 events across 300 vehicles, all at ambient temperatures between −3 and +6 °C. Logged data show that during failed pre-charges, the DC-link voltage rises to only 280–320 V against a pack voltage of 370 V before the 500 ms timeout expires. In normal starts it reaches 352 V (95 %) in about 300 ms.

*Draw the boundary.* The pre-charge circuit, the DC-link capacitors and anything connected to the DC link: the inverter, the DC-DC converter, the on-board charger, the cabin heater, the air-conditioning compressor, and the insulation of all of them.

*Simplest model.* If the voltage plateaus below the battery voltage, something is drawing current from the DC link during pre-charge. With a 100 Ω pre-charge resistor, a plateau at 300 V means a leakage of (370 − 300) / 100 = 0.7 A, equivalent to a load of about 430 Ω on the DC link. That is far too high a current for normal capacitor leakage, and it suggests either a load switching on too early or a significant insulation breakdown.

*Measure.* Logs show that on failed starts, the DC-DC converter has already been commanded on by the low-voltage system, which is trying to charge a weak 12 V battery after a cold night. The converter's control software starts drawing current as soon as it sees DC-link voltage above its minimum threshold of 250 V, before pre-charge has finished.

*Dominant term.* A sequencing error, made worse in cold weather because the 12 V battery is weaker and the converter starts more readily. No hardware is faulty.

*Fix and cross-check.* A software update makes the DC-DC converter wait for a "pre-charge complete" message from the BMS before drawing power. The team also reviews every other DC-link load for the same behaviour and finds the heater could do the same thing. Both are fixed. As a cross-check, they confirm that the delay of roughly 300 ms in DC-DC start-up does not cause the 12 V voltage to dip below the level needed by the control units at wake-up.

*Lesson.* Pre-charge faults are often reported as hardware problems, but the pre-charge circuit is also a measurement of everything else on the DC link. A plateau tells you something is drawing current, and the shape of the curve tells you how much.
:::

::: problem
**Field Problem — Isolation fault warning**

*Symptom.* The vehicle displays an isolation fault warning, sometimes only after rain or washing, and may refuse to start.

*Likely causes.* (1) Water ingress into a high-voltage connector or component, often through a damaged seal. (2) Coolant leaking inside a component such as the heater, charger or battery, as glycol-water mixtures conduct. (3) Chafed cable insulation touching the body. (4) A faulty isolation monitor giving a false reading.

*How to tell them apart.* Read the logged isolation values and note whether the fault is on the positive or negative side, as the ratio of the two values can indicate roughly where along the battery string the fault lies. Correlate with weather and washing. A trained technician then isolates components one at a time, measuring insulation resistance with an approved insulation tester, until the reading recovers. Coolant loss combined with an isolation fault points strongly to an internal leak.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Power is voltage times current. Charge capacity (Ah) and energy (Wh) are different; energy equals charge times average voltage.
- Resistive loss scales with the square of current, which is why higher voltages and careful conductor design matter. Resistance rises with temperature.
- Capacitors resist sudden voltage changes and inductors resist sudden current changes. DC-link capacitors smooth switching currents but must be pre-charged through a resistor to avoid destructive inrush.
- Three-phase systems deliver constant power, need no return conductor and create a rotating magnetic field. Power equals √3 *V*~LL~*I*~L~ cos φ.
- The Lorentz force makes motors produce torque; Faraday's law produces back-EMF. Laminated cores limit eddy currents, and core losses rise steeply with frequency.
- Switching converters regulate power by rapidly switching and filtering, with duty cycle setting the average voltage. Real switches have conduction and switching losses.
- The high-voltage system floats relative to the chassis and is protected by isolation monitoring, insulation, bonding, interlocks, discharge circuits, crash disconnection and fuses. Never ignore an isolation warning, and never treat a system as dead until absence of voltage has been proved.
:::

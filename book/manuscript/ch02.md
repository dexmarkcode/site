# Vehicle Dynamics and the Energy Budget

::: chapter-intro
Before you can size a battery, choose a motor or argue about range, you have to know how much force and energy it takes to move the vehicle. This chapter builds the road-load equation term by term, shows how drive cycles turn it into energy per kilometre, and uses it to size a powertrain for acceleration, top speed and gradient. It ends with the range equation and the reasons real-world range so often disappoints.
:::

Every electric vehicle programme I have worked on began with a spreadsheet. Not a sophisticated simulation, not a three-dimensional model, but a few dozen rows of numbers: mass, tyre rolling resistance, drag coefficient, frontal area, a drive cycle, an estimate of auxiliary power. From those numbers came the first estimate of battery size, motor power and range, and remarkably often the final production car landed within ten per cent of that early estimate.

The reason is that the physics of moving a car along a road is well understood and fairly simple. Four forces dominate: the resistance of the tyres, the drag of the air, the component of gravity along a slope, and the inertia the vehicle must overcome to accelerate. Get those right and you have most of the answer. This chapter is about getting them right, and about knowing which of them dominates in a given situation, because that is where the engineering leverage lies.

## The Forces on a Moving Vehicle

Consider a vehicle of mass *m* travelling at speed *v* up a slope of angle *θ*, as shown in Figure 2.1. To keep it moving, the powertrain must push the tyres against the road with a total tractive force *F*~t~ that balances the resistances and supplies any acceleration:

$$ F_t = F_{roll} + F_{aero} + F_{grade} + F_{acc} $$ {2.1}

Each term has its own physics and its own levers for the designer.

![**Figure 2.1** Forces acting on a vehicle climbing a gradient. The tractive force at the driven wheels must overcome rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag and the component of weight acting down the slope, with any surplus accelerating the vehicle.](figures/fig2_1.png)

### Rolling resistance

When a tyre rolls, its rubber is squashed as it enters the contact patch and recovers as it leaves. Rubber is not perfectly elastic, so some of the energy put into deforming it is lost as heat, a property called hysteresis. The result is a force opposing motion, roughly proportional to the load on the tyre:

$$ F_{roll} = C_{rr}\, m g \cos\theta $$ {2.2}

The coefficient of rolling resistance *C*~rr~ for a modern passenger car tyre on a smooth, dry road is typically between 0.006 and 0.012. Tyres designed specifically for electric vehicles, with stiffer constructions and silica-rich tread compounds, sit at the low end. For small gradients, cos θ is so close to one that it is usually ignored.

The coefficient is not quite constant. It rises gently with speed, it is higher for under-inflated tyres, higher on rough or wet surfaces and noticeably higher when the tyre is cold. In Europe, tyres carry an energy label that grades rolling resistance in classes from A to E. The difference between the best and worst classes is worth several per cent of a car's consumption, and for an EV that can be twenty or thirty kilometres of range. This is one of the reasons manufacturers are so particular about the tyres fitted at the factory, and why owners who fit cheaper replacements are sometimes surprised by their range.

### Aerodynamic drag

As the vehicle pushes through the air, it creates a region of high pressure at the front and a turbulent, low-pressure wake behind. The resulting drag force grows with the square of the speed relative to the air:

$$ F_{aero} = \frac{1}{2}\, \rho\, C_d A\, (v + v_w)^2 $$ {2.3}

Here *ρ* is the air density, about 1.2 kg/m^3^ at 20 °C and sea level, *C*~d~ is the drag coefficient, *A* is the frontal area and *v*~w~ is any headwind. Designers usually treat the product *C*~d~*A* as one number, because a low drag coefficient on a very large vehicle can still produce a lot of drag. A streamlined saloon might have *C*~d~ around 0.22 to 0.25 and *A* around 2.2 m^2^, giving *C*~d~*A* near 0.5 m^2^. A large SUV can have *C*~d~*A* above 0.85 m^2^.

Two features of Equation 2.3 matter enormously. First, drag rises with the square of speed, so the power needed to overcome it, *F*~aero~ × *v*, rises with the *cube*. Driving at 130 km/h rather than 100 km/h needs about 2.2 times as much power to overcome drag. Second, air density changes with temperature and altitude. Cold air is denser: at −10 °C it is around 1.34 kg/m^3^, about 12 per cent more than at 20 °C. That is one of several reasons winter range is lower.

### Gradient

On a slope of angle *θ*, a component of the vehicle's weight acts down the slope:

$$ F_{grade} = m g \sin\theta $$ {2.4}

Road gradients are normally quoted as a percentage, the rise divided by the horizontal run. A 10 % gradient means θ = arctan(0.10) ≈ 5.7°. For gradients up to about 15 %, sin θ is very close to the percentage divided by 100, a handy approximation.

Gradient force is not a loss in the same sense as rolling resistance or drag. Energy spent climbing a hill is stored as potential energy and can be partly recovered on the way down through regenerative braking. But it sets the peak force and sustained power the powertrain must deliver, and on a long mountain pass it can dominate everything else.

### Acceleration and rotating inertia

Finally, accelerating the vehicle requires a force given by Newton's second law. But the motor's rotor, the gears, the drive shafts and the wheels must also be spun up, and their rotational inertia adds to the effective mass:

$$ F_{acc} = \delta\, m\, \frac{dv}{dt} $$ {2.5}

The rotating mass factor *δ* is typically 1.03 to 1.10 for an electric car. It is larger than you might expect because a rotor's inertia, seen at the wheels, is multiplied by the square of the gear ratio. A rotor with an inertia of 0.03 kg·m^2^ behind a 9:1 reduction looks like 2.4 kg·m^2^ at the wheel, comparable to the inertia of the wheels and tyres themselves.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.1 — Road load at motorway speed**

A family crossover has a test mass of 1 900 kg, *C*~rr~ = 0.009 and *C*~d~*A* = 0.62 m^2^. Air density is 1.2 kg/m^3^. Find the road-load force and power at a steady 100 km/h and 130 km/h on a level road with no wind.

*Solution.* Convert speeds: 100 km/h = 27.78 m/s, 130 km/h = 36.11 m/s.

Rolling resistance is independent of speed in this simple model:
*F*~roll~ = 0.009 × 1 900 × 9.81 = 167.7 N.

Aerodynamic drag, with ½ρ*C*~d~*A* = 0.5 × 1.2 × 0.62 = 0.372 kg/m:
at 100 km/h, *F*~aero~ = 0.372 × 27.78^2^ = 287.0 N;
at 130 km/h, *F*~aero~ = 0.372 × 36.11^2^ = 485.1 N.

Total force and power at the wheels:
at 100 km/h, *F* = 454.7 N, *P* = 454.7 × 27.78 = 12.6 kW;
at 130 km/h, *F* = 652.8 N, *P* = 652.8 × 36.11 = 23.6 kW.

Raising the speed by 30 % nearly doubles the power. Note also that at 100 km/h drag is already 63 % of the total, and at 130 km/h it is 74 %. On the motorway, aerodynamics is the main thing.
:::

## Power, Energy and the Road-Load Curve

Multiplying the tractive force by speed gives tractive power, and integrating power over time gives energy. For steady driving on a level road, the energy per unit distance is simply the road-load force, since energy = force × distance. That gives us a remarkably useful result: *on a level road at constant speed, the energy consumption per kilometre at the wheels, in kJ/km, equals the road-load force in newtons.* To convert to the more familiar kWh per 100 km, divide the force in newtons by 36.

So the crossover in Worked Example 2.1 needs 454.7 / 36 = 12.6 kWh per 100 km at the wheels at a steady 100 km/h, and 18.1 kWh per 100 km at 130 km/h.

![**Figure 2.2** Road-load power for the crossover of Worked Example 2.1, split into rolling and aerodynamic components. Rolling losses grow linearly with speed; aerodynamic losses grow with its cube and dominate above about 70 km/h.](figures/fig2_2.png)

Figure 2.2 plots the road-load power over the full speed range. At town speeds, rolling resistance dominates and mass is the key parameter. Above about 70 km/h for this vehicle, drag takes over and shape becomes the key parameter. The crossover point depends on the ratio of *C*~rr~*mg* to ½ρ*C*~d~*A*, and it is a useful number to know for any vehicle you work on, because it tells you which lever to pull.

It also explains a pattern that confuses many new EV owners. A petrol car is often most efficient on a steady motorway run, because its engine is inefficient at the light loads of town driving. An EV is the opposite. Its powertrain is efficient across almost the whole load range, it recovers energy when braking, and it has no idle losses, so its consumption is lowest in gentle town driving and highest on a fast motorway. Range figures quoted from mixed test cycles tend to flatter electric cars on long motorway trips.

### From wheels to battery

The battery must supply the energy at the wheels plus the losses in the gear, motor and inverter, plus the auxiliary loads. A convenient approximation is:

$$ P_{batt} = \frac{P_{wheel}}{\eta_{dt}} + P_{aux} \quad (P_{wheel} > 0) $$ {2.6}

$$ P_{batt} = \eta_{dt}\, P_{wheel} + P_{aux} \quad (P_{wheel} < 0) $$ {2.7}

Here *η*~dt~ is the combined efficiency of the gearbox, motor and inverter, and *P*~aux~ covers the low-voltage loads, the cooling pumps and fans and, above all, cabin heating and cooling. During regeneration, *P*~wheel~ is negative, and only a fraction *η*~dt~ of it reaches the battery. In practice the drivetrain efficiency varies strongly with torque and speed, as we will see in Chapter 8, and serious simulations use a full efficiency map. For first estimates, a constant value of 0.85 to 0.90 is reasonable.

## Drive Cycles and Standard Tests

Real driving is not steady. Vehicles accelerate, cruise, brake and stop. To compare vehicles fairly, regulators define standard *drive cycles*: speed-time profiles that a car follows on a chassis dynamometer in a laboratory.

In Europe, the United Kingdom, Japan and many other markets, the current standard is the Worldwide harmonised Light vehicles Test Procedure (WLTP), which uses the Worldwide harmonised Light vehicles Test Cycle (WLTC). The Class 3 cycle used for most cars lasts 1 800 seconds and covers about 23.3 km in four phases, named Low, Medium, High and Extra High, with a top speed of just over 131 km/h. Figure 2.3 shows a simplified representation of its shape. For battery electric vehicles, the test procedure repeats the cycle until the battery is depleted, with shortened variants allowed to save laboratory time, and the range is calculated from the energy used.

![**Figure 2.3** A simplified representation of the four-phase WLTC Class 3 speed profile (Low, Medium, High, Extra High). The real cycle has many more micro-transients; this sketch preserves the phase durations and peak speeds for illustration.](figures/fig2_3.png)

The United States uses a different set of procedures maintained by the Environmental Protection Agency, including an urban cycle and a highway cycle, with additional tests for high speed, air conditioning and cold temperature. China uses its own CLTC cycle, which has lower average speeds and tends to produce higher range figures than WLTP for the same vehicle. As a rough guide, the same car will typically show its highest range on CLTC, somewhat less on WLTP and less again on the US combined label figure.

None of these cycles is "right". Each is a compromise between repeatability and realism. As an engineer, the important point is to know which cycle a range figure came from, and to simulate your own vehicle on the duty cycles your customers actually drive.

### Simulating a drive cycle

To compute energy consumption over a cycle, a simple backwards-facing simulation steps through the speed trace at one-second intervals. At each step it calculates the acceleration from the change in speed, evaluates Equation 2.1 to get the wheel force, multiplies by speed for wheel power, and then applies Equations 2.6 and 2.7 to get battery power. Summing battery power over time gives battery energy. Dividing by distance gives consumption.

This is the same model used in every major vehicle simulation tool, just with more detail. A spreadsheet version, a few hundred rows long, will get you within a few per cent of a much more expensive tool if your input parameters are good. Problem 9 at the end of this chapter asks you to build one.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.2 — How much braking energy is there to recover?**

The crossover from Example 2.1 decelerates from 100 km/h to rest. Its rotating mass factor is 1.05. How much kinetic energy does it have, and how much could reach the battery if the drivetrain efficiency during regeneration is 85 % and road loads absorb an average of 15 % of the kinetic energy during the stop?

*Solution.* Kinetic energy including rotating parts:

*E*~k~ = ½ δ *m* *v*^2^ = 0.5 × 1.05 × 1 900 × 27.78^2^ = 769 600 J ≈ 0.214 kWh.

Road loads absorb 15 %, leaving 0.85 × 0.214 = 0.182 kWh at the wheels for braking. After drivetrain losses: 0.85 × 0.182 = 0.155 kWh reaches the battery.

That is enough energy to drive the car about 1.2 km at 100 km/h. In a town with a stop every few hundred metres, regeneration makes a very large difference; on a motorway it hardly matters.
:::

## Sizing the Powertrain

The road-load equation tells us what the vehicle needs. Now we turn it round and ask what the powertrain must provide. Four requirements usually set the size of the motor and inverter: acceleration, top speed, gradeability and sustained performance.

### The tractive-effort envelope

An electric motor driven by an inverter has a characteristic shape, shown in Figure 2.4. Up to a *base speed*, it can deliver its full peak torque, limited by the current the inverter can supply. Above base speed, the motor's back-EMF approaches the available battery voltage and the drive can no longer push full current into the windings. From there it can deliver roughly constant *power*, so torque falls as speed rises. At very high speed, power begins to fall as well. We will explain the reasons in Chapter 8; for now, the important point is the shape.

![**Figure 2.4** Tractive-effort envelope of an electric drive, translated to the wheels, with road-load curves for level road and several gradients. The region below base speed is torque-limited; above it, the drive is power-limited. The intersection of the envelope with each road-load curve gives the maximum speed on that gradient.](figures/fig2_4.png)

Translated to the wheels through a gear ratio *G*, a wheel rolling radius *r*~w~ and a transmission efficiency *η*~g~, motor torque *T*~m~ and speed *ω*~m~ become:

$$ F_t = \frac{T_m\, G\, \eta_g}{r_w}, \qquad v = \frac{\omega_m\, r_w}{G} $$ {2.8}

### Acceleration

The time to accelerate from rest to a target speed is the most marketed number on any performance car, and for an EV it can be estimated surprisingly well with a two-phase model: constant force up to base speed, then constant power above it.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.3 — Estimating 0–100 km/h**

The crossover (*m* = 1 900 kg, δ = 1.05) has a motor with 350 N·m peak torque and 160 kW peak power, a gear ratio of 9.0, a transmission efficiency of 95 % and a tyre rolling radius of 0.34 m. Estimate the 0–100 km/h time, ignoring road loads at first.

*Solution.* Peak wheel force:

*F*~t~ = 350 × 9.0 × 0.95 / 0.34 = 8 801 N.

Peak wheel power = 0.95 × 160 = 152 kW. The vehicle reaches the constant-power region at a base speed of:

*v*~b~ = 152 000 / 8 801 = 17.3 m/s (62 km/h).

*Phase 1 (constant force).* Acceleration *a* = 8 801 / (1.05 × 1 900) = 4.41 m/s^2^. Time to 17.3 m/s = 17.3 / 4.41 = 3.9 s.

*Phase 2 (constant power).* With constant power *P*, the kinetic energy rises at rate *P*, so:

*t* = δ*m*(*v*~2~^2^ − *v*~b~^2^) / 2*P* = 1 995 × (27.78^2^ − 17.3^2^) / (2 × 152 000) = 1 995 × 472.4 / 304 000 = 3.1 s.

Estimated 0–100 km/h time: about 7.0 s. Including road loads, which average around 300 N over the run, adds roughly 0.2 s, giving about 7.2 s. Real cars also lose a little time at launch while torque ramps up and traction control manages wheel slip.

*Check traction.* Can the tyres transmit 8.8 kN? If the motor drives the rear axle carrying about 55 % of the static weight, plus some load transfer under acceleration, the available friction force on dry tarmac with μ ≈ 1.0 is roughly 0.58 × 1 900 × 9.81 ≈ 10.8 kN. So the car is power- and torque-limited, not traction-limited. On a wet road with μ ≈ 0.6, the limit would fall to around 6.5 kN and launch would be traction-limited. This is why powerful single-motor cars often feel tamer in the rain, and why many performance EVs use two motors.
:::

### Top speed

Top speed is reached where the tractive-effort envelope meets the level-road load curve, or where the motor reaches its maximum safe speed, whichever comes first. For our crossover, a maximum motor speed of 16 000 rpm with a 9.0 gear ratio gives a wheel speed of 1 778 rpm and a road speed of about 228 km/h. Power is no constraint at that point in the simple model, so this car would be limited by motor speed. In practice, manufacturers usually limit EV top speed electronically to well below the mechanical maximum, often between 160 and 200 km/h, because the energy cost of sustained very high speed is severe and because the motor and inverter would overheat if asked to deliver near-peak power continuously.

That trade between acceleration and top speed is governed largely by the gear ratio. A higher ratio multiplies torque, improving launch, but spins the motor faster at a given road speed. A lower ratio does the reverse. Motor designers have pushed maximum speeds to 18 000 rpm and beyond precisely to let vehicle engineers use higher ratios without sacrificing top speed, and the payoff is a smaller, lighter motor for the same wheel torque.

### Gradeability and towing

The gradeability requirement asks the vehicle to start and climb on a steep slope, often 25 to 30 % for passenger cars, and to sustain a given speed on a long, moderate gradient. Starting on the slope is a torque requirement; sustaining speed is a thermal requirement, because the motor and inverter must deliver that power continuously without overheating.

For towing, the trailer adds mass, rolling resistance and, above all, drag. A large caravan can easily double the aerodynamic force, which is why EVs towing caravans often see their motorway range halved. Sustained climbing with a trailer is one of the toughest thermal cases for a drive unit.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.4 — Hill start and sustained climb**

Can the crossover start on a 30 % gradient, and what continuous power does it need to climb a 6 % motorway gradient at 100 km/h?

*Solution.* A 30 % gradient means θ = arctan(0.30) = 16.7°, sin θ = 0.287, cos θ = 0.958.

*F*~grade~ = 1 900 × 9.81 × 0.287 = 5 349 N.
*F*~roll~ = 0.009 × 1 900 × 9.81 × 0.958 = 160.7 N.

Total required to hold and start the car: about 5 510 N. Available: 8 801 N. The car can start on the slope with a margin for some acceleration.

For the 6 % climb, sin θ ≈ 0.0599:
*F*~grade~ = 1 900 × 9.81 × 0.0599 = 1 117 N.
At 100 km/h the level road load from Example 2.1 is 454.7 N, so the total is 1 572 N and the wheel power is 1 572 × 27.78 = 43.7 kW.

Dividing by the drivetrain efficiency (0.88, say) gives about 50 kW from the battery. That is less than a third of peak power, but the motor and inverter must be able to sustain it for the ten or twenty minutes a mountain motorway climb might last. Continuous ratings, not peak ratings, decide this case.
:::

### Peak versus continuous ratings

Electric machines and power electronics have two ratings. The *peak* rating is what they can deliver for a short time, often 10 to 30 seconds, before temperatures climb too high. The *continuous* rating is what they can deliver indefinitely with the cooling system working normally. For passenger cars the continuous power is often only 40 to 60 % of the peak.

That gap is a deliberate design choice. Acceleration bursts are short, and designing for continuous peak power would make the drive unit much bigger and more expensive. But it means performance under sustained load, such as repeated launches on a track day or a long climb with a trailer, is governed by thermal design. A car that is quick in a magazine test can disappoint on its third lap.

## Range: The Equation and the Reality

With consumption in hand, range follows directly:

$$ \mathrm{Range} = \frac{E_{usable}}{c} $$ {2.9}

where *E*~usable~ is the usable battery energy and *c* is the average consumption from the battery, in kWh per km. Simple as it looks, every term hides complexity.

*Usable* energy is less than the nominal or gross energy of the pack. Manufacturers keep a buffer at the top and bottom of the state-of-charge range to protect the cells, and they may release some of it as the battery ages to keep range stable. Usable energy also depends on temperature and power: a cold battery has higher internal resistance, so more energy is lost as heat inside it, and its voltage sags sooner under load.

*Consumption* depends on speed, driving style, temperature, weather, tyre condition, payload, road surface and, very heavily, on the use of climate control. Figure 2.5 shows how range typically varies with steady speed and ambient temperature.

![**Figure 2.5** Indicative range at steady speed for a mid-size EV with 75 kWh usable energy, at three ambient temperatures. Cold weather affects range most at low speed, where heating is a large share of total power; high speed is dominated by aerodynamic drag.](figures/fig2_5.png)

### Why winter hurts

Cold weather reduces range through several mechanisms acting together:

1. **Cabin heating.** A resistive heater may draw 3 to 6 kW at full output and typically averages 1 to 3 kW on a cold day. At 50 km/h, 2 kW of heating adds 4 kWh per 100 km, which can be a third of total consumption. At 120 km/h it adds less per kilometre because the journey takes less time.
2. **Battery heating.** The pack may need warming before it can accept regenerative braking or deliver full power.
3. **Higher internal resistance.** Losses inside the cells rise when they are cold.
4. **Denser air and stiffer tyres.** Drag and rolling resistance both increase.
5. **Shorter trips.** Every journey starts with a cold cabin and battery, and on short trips the warm-up energy is a large share of the total.

Reductions of 20 to 30 % on cold winter days are common, and on very short trips in freezing weather the effective loss can exceed 40 %. A heat pump, which we discuss in Chapter 14, can cut heating energy by half or more. Preconditioning while plugged in moves the warm-up energy onto the grid.

::: problem
**Field Problem — "My range collapses on the motorway"**

*Symptom.* An owner reports that their EV shows 400 km of range around town but only reaches about 260 km on a motorway run at 125–130 km/h.

*Likely causes.* (1) Normal aerodynamic behaviour: drag power rises with the cube of speed, so a 30–40 % drop between gentle mixed driving and fast motorway driving is expected. (2) Headwind, rain or roof boxes increasing drag. (3) Low tyre pressures. (4) A cold battery or heater use in winter. (5) Rarely, a fault such as a dragging brake calliper or a drivetrain problem.

*How to tell them apart.* Compare consumption at a steady 100 km/h on a calm, dry day with the manufacturer's figures or a similar car. If it is within about 10 %, behaviour is normal and the answer is education, not repair. If consumption is high even at moderate speed, check tyre pressures, look for a hot wheel after a drive (a dragging brake), and inspect for added drag. Only then suspect the powertrain.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 2.5 — Estimating range**

The crossover has 75 kWh of usable energy, a drivetrain efficiency of 0.88 and auxiliary loads of 0.5 kW in mild weather. Estimate its range at a steady 100 km/h and 130 km/h. Then estimate range at 100 km/h on a cold day, when auxiliaries rise to 3.0 kW and road loads increase by 8 %.

*Solution.* At 100 km/h, wheel energy is 12.63 kWh per 100 km. Divide by 0.88: 14.35 kWh. The journey of 100 km takes 1 hour, so auxiliaries add 0.5 kWh. Consumption is 14.85 kWh per 100 km. Range = 75 / 0.1485 = 505 km.

At 130 km/h, wheel energy is 18.13 kWh per 100 km; divided by 0.88 gives 20.60 kWh. The journey takes 0.77 h, adding 0.38 kWh. Consumption is 20.98 kWh per 100 km. Range = 357 km.

Cold day at 100 km/h: wheel energy rises 8 % to 13.64 kWh; divided by 0.88, 15.50 kWh. Auxiliaries add 3.0 kWh. Consumption is 18.50 kWh per 100 km. Range = 405 km, about 20 % below the mild-weather figure, before allowing for any reduction in usable energy from the cold battery itself.
:::

## Mass, Efficiency and the Spiral

There is a feedback loop in EV design that every engineer should keep in mind. Suppose range falls short of target. The obvious fix is a bigger battery. But a bigger battery adds mass, which raises rolling resistance and the energy needed to accelerate, which raises consumption, which reduces the range gain from the extra cells. The heavier car also needs stronger brakes and suspension, and perhaps a bigger motor to keep its acceleration, all adding more mass. Engineers call this the *mass spiral*.

For a passenger car, the effect is moderate. Adding 10 % to battery energy might add 5 % to vehicle mass and 2 to 3 % to consumption, so the net range gain is about 7 %. For a heavy truck, where the battery can be several tonnes and payload is money, the spiral is far more punishing. The alternative lever, improving efficiency, works in the opposite direction: a more efficient car needs a smaller battery for the same range, which is lighter and cheaper, which makes the car more efficient still. Good EV programmes chase efficiency relentlessly for exactly this reason. A one per cent gain in drivetrain efficiency is worth about one per cent of battery cost, and battery cost is often a third of the vehicle's bill of materials.

::: casefile
**Case File 2 — The SUV variant that missed its range target**

*The complaint.* A manufacturer develops a high-riding SUV version of an existing electric hatchback, sharing the same 77 kWh usable battery and drive unit. Early prototypes return a WLTP range of 410 km against a target of 450 km. The hatchback achieves 480 km.

*State it in numbers.* To reach 450 km the SUV must consume no more than 77 / 450 = 17.1 kWh per 100 km on the cycle. It currently uses 77 / 410 = 18.8 kWh per 100 km. The gap is 1.7 kWh per 100 km, about 9 %.

*Draw the boundary.* The battery and drive unit are unchanged and proven on the hatchback, so the difference must lie in road load or auxiliaries: mass, tyres, aerodynamics, ride height, wheel size, or the thermal system.

*Simplest model.* Compare road-load coefficients from the coast-down tests. The SUV is 120 kg heavier (6 %), uses 20-inch wheels with a wider tyre (*C*~rr~ 0.0095 against 0.0085), and has *C*~d~*A* of 0.70 m^2^ against 0.57 m^2^ for the hatchback. Using the WLTC average speed of about 46 km/h and a rough split of wheel energy on that cycle, the model attributes about 45 % of the gap to aerodynamics, 35 % to tyres and 20 % to mass.

*Measure.* Wind-tunnel work shows that the larger wheels with open spoke designs and the raised ride height account for most of the aerodynamic increase: air flowing under the car and through the wheel arches is more turbulent.

*Dominant terms.* Aerodynamics and tyres, together roughly 80 % of the gap.

*Fix and cross-check.* The team specifies 19-inch aerodynamic wheel covers as standard, low-rolling-resistance tyres in an A-class label grade, an active air dam that lowers at speed, and a ride height that drops 15 mm above 90 km/h. Range rises to 448 km. The 20-inch wheels remain available as an option, with the lower range figure declared. Marketing is unhappy about the wheel covers, which the customer clinic describes as "plain". Engineering points out that each inch of wheel diameter is costing about 10 km.

*Lesson.* Mass is usually blamed first because it is the easiest to see. On a modern EV in a mixed test cycle, aerodynamics and tyres are often worth more.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Tractive force must overcome rolling resistance (proportional to mass), aerodynamic drag (proportional to speed squared), gradient, and the inertia of the vehicle and its rotating parts.
- On a level road at constant speed, wheel energy per kilometre equals the road-load force; dividing the force in newtons by 36 gives kWh per 100 km.
- Rolling resistance dominates at low speed and aerodynamic drag at high speed. Drag power grows with the cube of speed, so EVs are most efficient in town and least efficient on fast motorways.
- Drive cycles such as WLTC, the US EPA cycles and China's CLTC give standardised but different consumption and range figures; always ask which cycle a number came from.
- An electric drive delivers constant torque up to base speed and roughly constant power above it. Acceleration, top speed, gradeability and continuous ratings together size the motor, inverter and gear ratio.
- Range equals usable energy divided by consumption. Cold weather reduces range through heating, battery effects, denser air and short trips.
- Efficiency improvements compound favourably; extra battery mass compounds unfavourably (the mass spiral).
:::

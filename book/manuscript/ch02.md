Pick up a toaster's power cord after making a few rounds of toast and it feels cool. Touch the glowing element inside (please don't) and it would burn you instantly. Yet the same current flows through both, because they are part of the same series loop. Why does one get red hot and the other stay cool?

The answer is **resistance**, and it is one of the most useful ideas in electrical engineering. Resistance is the reason a kettle boils, a fuse blows and a light bulb glows. It is also the reason long cables waste energy, phone chargers get warm and electric car designers worry about the thickness of every busbar. In this chapter we look at what resistance is, what decides how much of it a piece of material has, and how electrical energy is converted into heat, light and motion. By the end you will be able to work out the running cost of your electric shower, which is a surprisingly sobering exercise.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- explain what resistance is in physical terms and state its unit, the ohm;
- calculate the resistance of a conductor from its resistivity, length and cross-sectional area;
- predict how temperature changes the resistance of metals and thermistors;
- use conductance, the reciprocal of resistance;
- read resistor values, tolerances and preferred values;
- calculate power using P = W / t and P = V × I;
- calculate energy in joules and kilowatt-hours, and work out running costs;
- calculate the efficiency of a device or a chain of devices.
:::

## 2.1 What Resistance Really Is

In Chapter 1 we pictured a metal as a lattice of fixed positive ions with free electrons drifting between them. That picture was a little too tidy. The ions are not perfectly still; they vibrate because of heat. The lattice also contains imperfections: impurity atoms, missing atoms and the boundaries between tiny crystals. As electrons drift through, they keep bumping into these obstacles.

Each collision knocks the electron off course and takes away some of the energy it gained from the electric field. That energy does not vanish. It is passed to the lattice as extra vibration, which we experience as **heat**. Then the field accelerates the electron again, it collides again, and so on, millions of millions of times a second.

![Figure 2.1 — An electron's path through a conductor is a series of short accelerations interrupted by collisions with the lattice. Each collision transfers energy to the lattice as heat. This opposition to the flow of charge is what we call resistance.](fig/ch02_collisions.svg){width=100%}

This opposition to the flow of charge is **resistance**. Its symbol is **R** and its unit is the **ohm**, symbol **Ω** (the Greek capital omega), named after the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm. We will give the ohm a precise definition in the next chapter, when we meet Ohm's law. For now, think of it this way: a larger resistance means that a larger voltage is needed to push the same current through.

Two consequences follow straight away, and they explain the toaster.

First, **resistance converts electrical energy into heat**. This is sometimes a nuisance – the warmth of a phone charger is wasted energy – and sometimes exactly what we want, as in a kettle, toaster, hairdryer or electric radiator.

Second, **where the resistance is, the heat is**. The toaster's power cord is made of thick copper with a tiny resistance, so very little energy is turned into heat along its length. The element is made of a special alloy with a much higher resistance, so nearly all the energy is released there. Same current, very different resistance, very different temperature.

## 2.2 What Decides the Resistance of a Conductor?

If you have ever wondered why the cable to an electric cooker is so much thicker than the flex on a bedside lamp, this section is for you. Three things set the resistance of a piece of wire.

**Length.** A longer wire means more collisions along the way, so resistance is proportional to length. Double the length and you double the resistance.

**Cross-sectional area.** A thicker wire offers more parallel paths for the electrons, rather like a wider motorway carrying more traffic. Resistance is *inversely* proportional to the area of the cross-section. Double the area and you halve the resistance.

**Material.** Some materials simply resist more than others. This property is called **resistivity**, symbol **ρ** (the Greek letter rho), and its unit is the ohm metre (Ω m).

![Figure 2.2 — The resistance of a uniform conductor depends on its length L, its cross-sectional area A and the resistivity ρ of the material it is made from.](fig/ch02_geometry.svg){width=90%}

Putting these together gives one of the most practical equations in the book.

::: keyeq
**Resistance of a conductor**

R = {{frac|ρ L|A}}

where R is in ohms (Ω), ρ is resistivity in ohm metres (Ω m), L is length in metres (m) and A is cross-sectional area in square metres (m²).
:::

::: {.table-wrap}
| Material | Resistivity at 20 °C (Ω m) | Typical use |
|:---------|:--------------------------:|:------------|
| Silver | 1.59 × 10^−8^ | Specialist contacts |
| Copper | 1.72 × 10^−8^ | Cables, PCB tracks, windings |
| Gold | 2.44 × 10^−8^ | Connector plating |
| Aluminium | 2.82 × 10^−8^ | Overhead power lines |
| Tungsten | 5.6 × 10^−8^ | Old filament lamps |
| Nichrome | 1.10 × 10^−6^ | Heating elements |
| Carbon (graphite) | about 3.5 × 10^−5^ | Resistors, brushes |
| Pure silicon | about 2 × 10^3^ | Semiconductors (heavily affected by impurities) |
| Glass | 10^10^ to 10^14^ | Insulators |

: Table 2.1 — Approximate resistivities of common materials.
:::

Look at the range in Table 2.1. Glass resists about a million million million times more than copper. Few physical properties vary over such an enormous range, and that is precisely why we can make wires and insulators that do their jobs so well.

You might ask why power lines use aluminium when copper is a better conductor. The answer is weight and cost. For the same resistance, an aluminium conductor is thicker but roughly half the weight of a copper one, and much cheaper. On a line strung between pylons, weight matters a great deal.

### Getting the units right

The area of a cable is usually quoted in **square millimetres** (mm²). Household lighting circuits typically use 1.0 or 1.5 mm² cable; ring circuits for sockets use 2.5 mm²; a cooker or shower might use 6 or 10 mm². Before using R = ρL/A, you must convert mm² to m². Since 1 mm = 10^−3^ m, it follows that

::: eq
1 mm² = (10^−3^ m)² = 10^−6^ m²
:::

Forgetting this conversion gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of a million, which is the sort of mistake that is easy to spot if you sense-check, and embarrassing if you do not.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.1 — Resistance of a cable run**

A garden workshop is supplied through 50 m of twin cable with copper conductors of 1.5 mm² cross-section. Find the resistance of one conductor and of the complete loop (out and back).

**Step 1: Knowns in SI units.** ρ = 1.72 × 10^−8^ Ω m, L = 50 m, A = 1.5 mm² = 1.5 × 10^−6^ m².

**Step 2: Equation.** R = ρL / A.

**Step 3: Calculate for one conductor.**

R = {{frac|1.72 × 10^−8^ × 50|1.5 × 10^−6^}} = {{frac|8.6 × 10^−7^|1.5 × 10^−6^}} = 0.573 Ω

**Step 4: The loop.** Current goes out along one conductor and returns along the other, so the total length is 100 m and the resistance doubles: 1.15 Ω.

**Sense-check.** About half an ohm for 50 m of ordinary cable is reasonable. Any answer in the thousands of ohms would point to a unit error.

**Answer:** 0.573 Ω per conductor; about 1.15 Ω for the loop. In Chapter 3 we will see how this resistance causes a noticeable voltage drop when a heavy load is used at the far end.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 2.2 — Designing a heating element**

A heating element must have a resistance of 25 Ω. It is to be wound from nichrome wire of diameter 0.5 mm. What length of wire is needed?

**Step 1: Find the area.** The wire is circular, with radius r = 0.25 mm = 0.25 × 10^−3^ m.

A = π r² = π × (0.25 × 10^−3^)² = 1.963 × 10^−7^ m²

**Step 2: Rearrange for length.** From R = ρL / A, multiply both sides by A and divide by ρ:

L = {{frac|R A|ρ}}

**Step 3: Substitute.**

L = {{frac|25 × 1.963 × 10^−7^|1.10 × 10^−6^}} = 4.46 m

**Answer:** about 4.5 m of wire, which would be wound into a tight coil to fit inside the appliance.
:::

### Reasoning with proportions

Often you do not need the full equation at all, just its proportions. If a wire has a resistance of 2 Ω and you replace it with one made of the same material but twice as long, the new resistance is 4 Ω. If you keep the length but double the *diameter*, the area goes up by a factor of four (because area depends on diameter squared), so the resistance falls to a quarter: 0.5 Ω. This kind of quick reasoning is a valuable engineering habit, and it is a good check on calculator work.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.3 — Scaling a resistance**

A length of copper wire has a resistance of 0.8 Ω. It is replaced by a wire of the same material that is three times as long and has twice the diameter. What is the new resistance?

**Solution.** Tripling the length multiplies R by 3. Doubling the diameter multiplies the area by 2² = 4, which divides R by 4. Overall:

R~new~ = 0.8 × {{frac|3|4}} = 0.6 Ω
:::

## 2.3 Resistance and Temperature

Earlier I said that the ions in a metal vibrate more as the metal gets hotter. More vibration means more collisions, so **the resistance of a metal rises with temperature**. For most metals over everyday temperature ranges the rise is close to a straight line, which we can describe with a **temperature coefficient of resistance**, symbol **α** (alpha).

::: keyeq
**Resistance at temperature T**

R~T~ = R~0~ (1 + α ΔT)

where R~0~ is the resistance at a reference temperature (usually 20 °C), ΔT is the change in temperature from that reference, and α is the temperature coefficient in per degree Celsius (/°C).
:::

For copper, α is about 0.0039 /°C. That means the resistance of a copper wire rises by about 0.39 % for every degree of warming, or almost 4 % for every ten degrees. It does not sound like much until you consider a motor winding that warms by 60 °C in normal use.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.4 — A warm motor winding**

The copper winding of a motor has a resistance of 20 Ω at 20 °C. What is its resistance when the motor is running at 80 °C?

**Solution.** ΔT = 80 − 20 = 60 °C, α = 0.0039 /°C.

R~T~ = 20 × (1 + 0.0039 × 60) = 20 × 1.234 = 24.7 Ω

**Answer:** about 24.7 Ω – an increase of more than 23 %.
:::

The equation also works backwards, and engineers use it to measure the temperature of windings that are buried deep inside a machine where no thermometer can reach. This is called the **resistance method**, and it is used in the factory testing of motors and transformers.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.5 — Measuring temperature with resistance**

A transformer winding measures 12.0 Ω at 20 °C when cold. After a long test run it measures 14.4 Ω. Estimate the average winding temperature.

**Step 1: Rearrange.** Divide both sides of R~T~ = R~0~(1 + αΔT) by R~0~:

{{frac|R~T~|R~0~}} = 1 + α ΔT,   so   ΔT = {{frac|(R~T~ / R~0~) − 1|α}}

**Step 2: Substitute.** R~T~ / R~0~ = 14.4 / 12.0 = 1.20.

ΔT = {{frac|0.20|0.0039}} = 51.3 °C

**Step 3: Add to the starting temperature.** T = 20 + 51.3 = 71.3 °C.

**Answer:** about 71 °C.
:::

### Thermistors

Some materials behave very differently. A **thermistor** is a resistor made from metal oxides whose resistance changes strongly with temperature – far more than a metal's. The most common type is the **NTC** (negative temperature coefficient) thermistor, whose resistance *falls* as it warms. Figure 2.3 compares the two behaviours.

![Figure 2.3 — Left: the resistance of copper rises gently and almost linearly with temperature. Right: an NTC thermistor's resistance falls steeply and non-linearly as it warms, which makes it an excellent temperature sensor.](fig/ch02_temperature.svg){width=100%}

Thermistors are everywhere: in kettles, car engines, battery packs, central-heating controllers and digital thermometers. Because their resistance changes so much, a small temperature change produces a large, easily measured signal. In Chapter 8 you will see how a thermistor and a fixed resistor together make a simple temperature sensor, using the voltage divider.

## 2.4 Conductance: Looking at It the Other Way Round

Sometimes it is more natural to describe how *easily* a component lets current through rather than how strongly it resists. The reciprocal of resistance is called **conductance**, symbol **G**, and its unit is the **siemens**, symbol **S**.

::: keyeq
**Conductance**

G = {{frac|1|R}}

where G is in siemens (S) and R is in ohms (Ω).
:::

A 4 Ω resistor has a conductance of 1/4 = 0.25 S. A 20 mS conductance corresponds to 1 / 0.020 = 50 Ω. Large resistance means small conductance, and vice versa.

At this stage conductance may seem like an unnecessary extra. It earns its place later. When resistors are connected in parallel (Chapter 6) their conductances simply add, and in nodal analysis (Chapter 10) equations written with conductances are noticeably neater. Keep it in your back pocket.

## 2.5 Resistors in the Real World

A **resistor** is a component made to have a particular, stable resistance. It is the most common component in electronics; a typical circuit board contains dozens or hundreds of them. Resistors are used to set currents, divide voltages, protect delicate parts and convert currents into voltages that can be measured.

### Types of resistor

- **Carbon film and metal film resistors** are the familiar small cylinders with coloured bands and a wire at each end. Metal film types are more accurate and more stable, and are now the usual choice.
- **Surface-mount resistors** are tiny rectangular blocks soldered directly to the surface of a board. Some are smaller than a grain of salt. Their value is printed as a short code, or not at all.
- **Wirewound resistors** are made from resistance wire wound on a ceramic former. They handle high power and are used in power supplies, motor drives and test loads.
- **Variable resistors and potentiometers** have a sliding contact so that the resistance can be adjusted. A volume knob on an old radio is a potentiometer.
- **Sensing resistors** such as thermistors and light-dependent resistors (LDRs) change their resistance in response to temperature or light.

Figure 2.4 shows the circuit symbols you will meet. This book uses the rectangular symbol, which is the standard in the UK and Europe. American books and many datasheets use the zigzag. They mean exactly the same thing.

![Figure 2.4 — Resistor symbols. The rectangular IEC symbol is standard in the UK and is used throughout this book; the zigzag is common in American material.](fig/ch02_symbols.svg){width=100%}

### Reading a resistor's value

Through-hole resistors are marked with coloured bands. The first two bands give digits, the third gives a multiplier (the number of zeros to add) and the last gives the **tolerance**. Figure 2.5 shows an example: yellow (4), violet (7), red (× 100) and gold (± 5 %), making 4700 Ω or 4.7 kΩ. The full colour code is given in Appendix A, along with a well-worn memory aid.

![Figure 2.5 — Reading a four-band resistor. The bands are read from the end nearest a band; the tolerance band is usually set slightly apart.](fig/ch02_colour.svg){width=88%}

### Tolerance and preferred values

No manufacturing process is perfect, so every resistor's value is guaranteed only within a **tolerance**. A 4.7 kΩ resistor with ± 5 % tolerance might be anything from

::: eq
4700 − 5 % of 4700 = 4465 Ω    to    4700 + 5 % of 4700 = 4935 Ω
:::

and still be within specification. In most circuits that does not matter. Where it does, you can buy 1 % or even 0.1 % resistors, at a higher price.

You may also have wondered why resistors come in strange values such as 4.7, 6.8 and 8.2 rather than round numbers like 5 and 8. The values are chosen so that the tolerance bands of neighbouring values just overlap, so that every possible resistance is covered by some standard part. These **preferred values** form the **E-series**. The E12 series, with twelve values per decade, is:

::: eq
1.0   1.2   1.5   1.8   2.2   2.7   3.3   3.9   4.7   5.6   6.8   8.2
:::

and each value is available multiplied by 1, 10, 100, 1000 and so on. When your calculation says you need a 5.3 kΩ resistor, you will in practice choose the nearest preferred value, 5.6 kΩ, and check that the circuit still works. That small piece of judgement is a big part of real design.

### Power rating

Because a resistor turns electrical energy into heat, it gets warm, and if it gets too warm it will be damaged. Every resistor has a **power rating**: the maximum rate at which it can safely get rid of heat. Small through-hole resistors are commonly rated at 0.25 W or 0.5 W; wirewound types may handle 5, 10 or 50 W. We will learn to calculate the power in a resistor in Chapter 3, and checking that it is within the rating will become routine. First, though, we need to be clear about what power actually is.

## 2.6 Power: The Rate of Using Energy

In everyday speech "power" and "energy" are used loosely, almost as synonyms. In engineering they are carefully distinguished.

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. It is measured in **joules** (J). Lifting a 1 kg bag of sugar up by about 10 cm takes roughly one joule.

**Power** is the *rate* at which energy is converted or transferred. It is measured in **watts** (W), named after the Scottish engineer James Watt. One watt is one joule per second.

::: keyeq
**Power**

P = {{frac|W|t}}

where P is power in watts (W), W is energy in joules (J) and t is time in seconds (s).
:::

The distinction matters. A 3 kW kettle and a 60 W laptop might use similar amounts of energy over a day, because the kettle is on for a few minutes and the laptop for hours. The kettle's *power* is fifty times greater; it simply gets the job done quickly.

### Electrical power: P = V × I

Now we can bring together the ideas from Chapter 1. Voltage is energy per coulomb. Current is coulombs per second. Multiply them and the coulombs cancel:

::: eq
V × I = {{frac|joules|coulomb}} × {{frac|coulombs|second}} = {{frac|joules|second}} = watts
:::

So the power delivered to any component, whatever it is, is simply the voltage across it multiplied by the current through it.

::: keyeq
**Electrical power**

P = V × I

where P is in watts (W), V is in volts (V) and I is in amperes (A).
:::

This is one of the most used equations in all of electrical engineering. It applies to resistors, motors, LEDs, batteries, whole houses – anything.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.6 — Current drawn by a kettle**

A kettle is rated at 3 kW and connected to a 230 V supply. What current does it draw?

**Solution.** Rearrange P = V × I to give I = P / V.

I = {{frac|3000|230}} = 13.0 A

**Comment.** This is why kettles are fitted with 13 A plugs and fuses: a 3 kW kettle is right at the limit of what a standard UK socket outlet is designed to supply. It is also why plugging a kettle and a fan heater into the same extension lead is a bad idea.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 2.7 — A laptop charger**

The label on a laptop charger says "Output: 19.5 V DC, 3.33 A". What is its maximum output power?

**Solution.** P = V × I = 19.5 × 3.33 = 64.9 W, which the manufacturer would sell as a "65 W charger".
:::

Figure 2.6 gives some typical power ratings of household appliances. Notice the pattern: anything whose job is to produce *heat* – kettles, showers, hairdryers, ovens – sits at the top of the list. Producing heat electrically takes a great deal of power.

![Figure 2.6 — Typical power ratings of household appliances. The scale is logarithmic: each grid step is a factor of ten. Appliances that heat things dominate.](fig/ch02_appliances.svg){width=96%}

### Power in sources and loads

There is a subtlety here that will matter a great deal when we analyse circuits with several sources. A component can either **absorb** power (take energy from the circuit) or **deliver** power (give energy to the circuit). A resistor always absorbs power. A battery in a torch delivers power. But a phone battery on charge *absorbs* power, because energy is flowing into it.

The polarity rule from Chapter 1 tells us which is happening. If conventional current enters a component at its **+** terminal, the component is absorbing power. If current leaves by the + terminal, it is delivering power. We will use this rule – it is often called the **passive sign convention** – to check our answers in later chapters. In any complete circuit, the total power delivered by the sources must exactly equal the total power absorbed by everything else. Energy is conserved, always.

## 2.7 Energy and the Kilowatt-Hour

Rearranging P = W / t gives the energy used by a device of known power running for a known time:

::: eq
W = P × t
:::

With power in watts and time in seconds, the energy is in joules. But a joule is a very small amount of energy for household purposes – a 3 kW kettle uses 3000 joules *every second*. Electricity suppliers therefore use a much larger unit, the **kilowatt-hour** (kWh), which is the energy used by a 1 kW device running for one hour. On your bill it is simply called a "unit".

::: eq
1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3 600 000 J = 3.6 MJ
:::

To calculate energy in kWh, use power in **kilowatts** and time in **hours**.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.8 — What does the kettle cost?**

A household boils its 3 kW kettle for a total of 4 minutes each day. Electricity costs 28p per kWh. What does the kettle cost to run over a 30-day month?

**Step 1: Units.** P = 3 kW. Time per day = 4 minutes = 4/60 h = 0.0667 h.

**Step 2: Energy per day.** W = 3 × 0.0667 = 0.2 kWh.

**Step 3: Energy per month.** 0.2 × 30 = 6 kWh.

**Step 4: Cost.** 6 × 28p = 168p = £1.68.

**Answer:** about £1.68 a month.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 2.9 — The electric shower**

A 9.5 kW electric shower is used for a total of 10 minutes a day. At 28p per kWh, what does it cost per year?

**Solution.** Daily energy = 9.5 kW × (10/60) h = 1.583 kWh. Annual energy = 1.583 × 365 = 578 kWh. Annual cost = 578 × £0.28 = £162.

**Comment.** It is the high power, not the length of time, that makes the shower costly. Two minutes shaved off each shower saves about £32 a year. This is the sort of calculation that turns circuit theory into a household argument.
:::

### Charge, energy and the battery revisited

In Chapter 1 we met battery capacity in milliamp-hours. Engineers who design battery-powered products constantly move between charge, energy and power, and it is worth seeing all three ideas together:

- capacity in **ampere-hours** (A h) is a measure of *charge*;
- multiplying by the battery voltage gives **watt-hours** (W h), a measure of *energy*;
- dividing the energy by the power drawn gives the **running time**.

A 12 V, 7 A h sealed lead-acid battery, of the kind used in alarm systems, stores about 12 × 7 = 84 W h. Powering a 6 W alarm panel, it would last at most 84 / 6 = 14 hours, and in practice rather less, since it is unwise to discharge a lead-acid battery completely.

## 2.8 Efficiency

No real device converts energy perfectly into the form we want. A motor turns most of its electrical input into mechanical output, but some is lost as heat in its windings and bearings, and some as noise. A phone charger turns most of its input into useful charging energy, but it gets warm. **Efficiency**, symbol **η** (the Greek letter eta), measures how much of the input becomes useful output.

::: keyeq
**Efficiency**

η = {{frac|useful output power|input power}} × 100 %
:::

Efficiency can never exceed 100 %, because that would mean creating energy out of nothing. The difference between input and output power is the **power loss**, almost always in the form of heat.

::: example
**Worked Example 2.10 — Motor efficiency**

A small motor takes 400 W from its supply and delivers 340 W of mechanical power. Find its efficiency and its power loss.

**Solution.** η = 340 / 400 × 100 % = 85 %. Power loss = 400 − 340 = 60 W, which appears as heat in the motor.
:::

When energy passes through several stages in turn, the overall efficiency is the **product** of the individual efficiencies (written as decimals), not their sum or average. A charger that is 90 % efficient feeding a battery that is 95 % efficient at storing energy gives an overall efficiency of 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.855, or 85.5 %. Long chains of conversion lose more than people expect, which is one reason engineers work so hard to remove stages from power systems.

::: tip
**In Practice: Why Heat Is the Enemy**

In electronics, efficiency is not just about running costs. Every watt that is lost becomes heat that must be removed, with heatsinks, fans or careful board layout. A power supply that is 80 % efficient while delivering 100 W must get rid of 25 W of heat (because it draws 125 W). Improve it to 95 % and the heat falls to about 5 W. That is the difference between a hot box with a fan and a cool, silent one.
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Forgetting to convert mm² to m².** 1 mm² = 10^−6^ m².
- **Using diameter instead of radius** in A = πr², or forgetting that doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
- **Confusing power and energy.** Watts measure a rate; joules and kilowatt-hours measure an amount.
- **Mixing units in kWh calculations.** Use kilowatts and hours together, or watts and seconds together – never watts and hours.
- **Adding efficiencies.** For stages in a chain, multiply them.
- **Assuming resistance is fixed.** It changes with temperature, sometimes by a lot.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Resistance** (R) is the opposition to the flow of charge, caused by electrons colliding with the lattice. It is measured in ohms (Ω) and converts electrical energy into heat.
- The resistance of a conductor is R = ρL / A: proportional to length, inversely proportional to cross-sectional area and dependent on the material's **resistivity** ρ.
- The resistance of metals rises with temperature: R~T~ = R~0~(1 + αΔT). For copper, α ≈ 0.0039 /°C. NTC thermistors behave the opposite way, very strongly.
- **Conductance** G = 1/R is measured in siemens (S).
- Practical resistors have a **tolerance**, come in **preferred values** (E-series) and have a **power rating**.
- **Power** is the rate of converting energy: P = W / t. For any component, P = V × I.
- **Energy** W = P × t, in joules or kilowatt-hours. 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.
- **Efficiency** η = output / input × 100 %. For stages in a chain, multiply efficiencies.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

R = ρL / A     R~T~ = R~0~(1 + αΔT)     G = 1 / R     P = W / t     P = V × I     W = P × t     η = P~out~ / P~in~
:::

In November 1926 Edward Lawry Norton, an engineer at Bell Telephone Laboratories in New York, wrote a technical memorandum about the design of filters for telephone equipment. In it, almost in passing, he noted that any linear network could be represented not only by Thévenin's voltage source and series resistance, but equally well by a *current* source with a resistance in *parallel*. The memorandum was internal to Bell Labs and Norton never published the idea in a journal. Yet within a few years it was in every textbook, and it has carried his name ever since. (In the same year, the German engineer Hans Ferdinand Mayer published the same result independently, which is why some European books call it the Mayer–Norton theorem.)

Norton's theorem is the dual of Thévenin's. Where Thévenin uses a voltage source and a series resistance, Norton uses a current source and a parallel resistance. The two descriptions are completely interchangeable, and the ability to convert between them – called **source transformation** – turns out to be a remarkably powerful tool for simplifying circuits by hand.

This chapter completes Part Five. By its end you will have three great network theorems at your disposal and a flexible technique for reshaping circuits so that they become easy to solve.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- state Norton's theorem and find a Norton equivalent;
- convert between Thévenin and Norton equivalents;
- perform source transformations between a voltage source with series resistance and a current source with parallel resistance;
- simplify circuits by repeated source transformation;
- interpret the terminal characteristic of a source on a voltage–current graph;
- combine parallel voltage sources using Millman's theorem;
- choose between Thévenin and Norton forms for a given problem.
:::

## 15.1 Norton's Theorem

::: keyeq
**Norton's theorem**

Any linear two-terminal network of sources and resistors can be replaced, as seen from its terminals, by a single ideal current source I~N~ in parallel with a single resistance R~N~.

I~N~ is the short-circuit current at the terminals. R~N~ is the resistance seen looking into the terminals with all independent sources switched off – the same as R~Th~.
:::

![Figure 15.1 — The Thévenin and Norton equivalents of the same network. Each describes the network's terminal behaviour completely.](fig/ch15_equiv.svg){width=86%}

Finding a Norton equivalent therefore takes the same three steps as Thévenin, except that the first quantity you calculate is a short-circuit current rather than an open-circuit voltage:

1. **Short-circuit the terminals** and calculate the current that flows through the short. This is I~N~, and its direction (from a to b through the short) sets the direction of the current source arrow.
2. **Find R~N~** exactly as you would find R~Th~: switch off independent sources and look in, or use V~oc~/I~sc~, or use a test source.
3. **Draw** the current source I~N~ in parallel with R~N~.

## 15.2 Thévenin and Norton Are Two Views of One Thing

Because both equivalents describe the same network, they must give the same results for any load. Two loads in particular pin down the relationship.

- **Open circuit.** The Thévenin equivalent gives V~Th~. In the Norton equivalent, with nothing connected, all of I~N~ flows through R~N~, giving a voltage I~N~R~N~. These must be equal.
- **Short circuit.** The Norton equivalent gives I~N~. The Thévenin equivalent gives V~Th~/R~Th~. These must be equal.

From these two conditions:

::: keyeq
**Converting between Thévenin and Norton**

R~N~ = R~Th~     I~N~ = {{frac|V~Th~|R~Th~}}     V~Th~ = I~N~ R~N~
:::

So once you have one equivalent, the other takes a single division or multiplication. The direction matters: the Norton current source points towards the terminal that is positive in the Thévenin equivalent.

::: example
**Worked Example 15.1 — Norton from Thévenin**

In Worked Example 14.1 we found V~Th~ = 16 V and R~Th~ = 6 Ω. Find the Norton equivalent, and confirm that it gives the same load current for R~L~ = 2 Ω.

**Conversion.** I~N~ = 16 / 6 = 2.667 A; R~N~ = 6 Ω.

**Check with a 2 Ω load.** In the Norton circuit, the 2.667 A divides between R~N~ = 6 Ω and R~L~ = 2 Ω. By the current divider rule:

I~L~ = 2.667 × {{frac|6|6 + 2}} = 2.0 A

exactly as the Thévenin equivalent gave. (We also found I~sc~ = 2.667 A directly in Worked Example 14.4, which is I~N~.)
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 15.2 — A Norton equivalent found directly**

In the circuit of Figure 13.4(a) – a 24 V source in series with 6 Ω feeding node A, a 3 A current source pushing current into A, and a 3 Ω resistor from A to the reference – find the Norton equivalent seen between A and the reference, treating the 3 Ω as part of the network.

**Step 1: Short A to the reference.** The 3 Ω resistor is shorted out and carries nothing. The short carries the current from the 24 V source through 6 Ω (24/6 = 4 A) plus the 3 A from the current source:

I~N~ = 4 + 3 = 7 A

**Step 2: R~N~.** Switch off both sources: the 24 V becomes a short, the 3 A becomes an open. Looking in from A, we see 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel: R~N~ = 2 Ω.

**Step 3: Check.** The open-circuit voltage should be I~N~R~N~ = 7 × 2 = 14 V. That is exactly the V~A~ we found by superposition in Worked Example 13.3 ✓.

**Comment.** Notice how natural the Norton form is here. With the terminals shorted, the contributions of the two sources simply *add* as currents. Circuits with current sources or with sources feeding a common node often yield more easily to Norton than to Thévenin.
:::

### The terminal characteristic

There is a lovely way to see both equivalents at once. Plot the terminal voltage V of a source against the current I it delivers. For any Thévenin (or Norton) source, V = V~Th~ − IR~Th~, which is a straight line, as in Figure 15.2.

![Figure 15.2 — The terminal characteristic of the source in Worked Example 15.1. The line meets the voltage axis at V~Th~ (open circuit) and the current axis at I~N~ (short circuit). Its slope is −R~Th~.](fig/ch15_line.svg){width=72%}

The line crosses the voltage axis at the open-circuit voltage, V~Th~, and the current axis at the short-circuit current, I~N~. Its slope is −R~Th~. Every possible load corresponds to one point on this line. A Thévenin description emphasises the voltage intercept; a Norton description emphasises the current intercept; but it is the same line. An ideal voltage source would be a horizontal line (R~Th~ = 0) and an ideal current source a vertical one (R~N~ = ∞) – the two extremes we met in Chapter 4.

## 15.3 Source Transformation

The conversion between Thévenin and Norton forms is useful not only for whole networks but also for individual sources *inside* a circuit. Any voltage source E in series with a resistance R can be replaced by a current source E/R in parallel with the same R, and vice versa, without changing anything in the rest of the circuit. This is called **source transformation**.

![Figure 15.3 — Source transformation. A voltage source E in series with R is equivalent to a current source E/R in parallel with R.](fig/ch15_transform.svg){width=86%}

::: keyeq
**Source transformation**

E in series with R   ⟷   I = E/R in parallel with R

The current source points towards the terminal connected to the + side of the voltage source.
:::

Two conditions apply.

- **There must be a resistor.** An *ideal* voltage source on its own cannot be transformed (it would need an infinite current source in parallel with zero resistance), and neither can an ideal current source on its own. The resistor is part of the transformation.
- **The equivalence holds only outside the pair.** As with every equivalent in this book, the transformed pair behaves identically at its two terminals, but the current in the resistor *inside* the pair is generally different. If you need that internal current, return to the original circuit.

### Why it helps

The power of source transformation is that it lets you choose whichever form makes the next simplification possible. Voltage sources in **series** combine by adding, and so do resistances in series – so the Thévenin form is convenient when things are in series. Current sources in **parallel** combine by adding, and so do conductances in parallel – so the Norton form is convenient when things are in parallel. By switching back and forth, you can often reduce a multi-source circuit to a single source and a single resistor without writing any simultaneous equations.

::: example
**Worked Example 15.3 — The two-battery circuit by source transformation**

Find V~A~ in our familiar two-battery circuit (10 V with 2 Ω, 5 V with 1 Ω, 4 Ω load) using source transformations only.

**Step 1: Transform both batteries** (Figure 15.4(a)).

- 10 V in series with 2 Ω becomes 10/2 = 5 A in parallel with 2 Ω.
- 5 V in series with 1 Ω becomes 5/1 = 5 A in parallel with 1 Ω.

Both current sources now push current up into node A.

**Step 2: Combine** (Figure 15.4(b)). Everything is now in parallel between A and the reference. The current sources add: 5 + 5 = 10 A. The two source resistances combine: 2 ∥ 1 = 0.667 Ω.

**Step 3: Solve.** The 10 A flows into 0.667 Ω in parallel with the 4 Ω load: 0.667 ∥ 4 = 0.571 Ω.

V~A~ = 10 × 0.571 = 5.714 V

Our sixth method, and the same answer once again. This one needed no equations at all.
:::

![Figure 15.4 — (a) Both battery branches transformed into Norton form. (b) The parallel current sources and resistances combined.](fig/ch15_twobatt.svg){width=100%}

::: example
**Worked Example 15.4 — Reducing a ladder by repeated transformation**

In Figure 15.5, find the voltage across and current through the 4 Ω load.

![Figure 15.5 — A ladder network to be reduced by repeated source transformation.](fig/ch15_ladder.svg){width=62%}

**Step 1: Transform the source.** 36 V in series with 6 Ω becomes 36/6 = 6 A in parallel with 6 Ω.

**Step 2: Combine in parallel.** The 6 Ω is now in parallel with the 3 Ω shunt: 6 ∥ 3 = 2 Ω. We have 6 A in parallel with 2 Ω.

**Step 3: Transform back.** 6 A in parallel with 2 Ω becomes 6 × 2 = 12 V in series with 2 Ω.

**Step 4: Combine in series.** The 2 Ω source resistance adds to the 2 Ω series resistor: 4 Ω. We now have 12 V in series with 4 Ω – the Thévenin equivalent seen by the load.

**Step 5: Solve.** With the 4 Ω load: I~L~ = 12 / (4 + 4) = **1.5 A** and V~L~ = 1.5 × 4 = **6 V**.

**Comment.** Each step was a single multiplication, division or addition. For ladder networks this "transform, combine, transform back" rhythm is often the fastest method of all.
:::

## 15.4 Millman's Theorem: Sources in Parallel

Source transformation leads straight to a neat result for a common situation: several voltage sources, each with its own series resistance, all connected in parallel. This might be several batteries connected together, several solar panels feeding a common bus or several sensor outputs being averaged.

Transform each branch (E~k~ in series with R~k~) into a current source E~k~/R~k~ in parallel with R~k~. All the current sources are now in parallel, so they add; all the resistances are in parallel, so their conductances add. The open-circuit voltage is the total current divided by the total conductance:

::: keyeq
**Millman's theorem**

V = {{frac|E~1~/R~1~ + E~2~/R~2~ + E~3~/R~3~ + …|1/R~1~ + 1/R~2~ + 1/R~3~ + …}}

The common voltage of several sources in parallel, each with its own series resistance.
:::

In words, the common voltage is a weighted average of the source voltages, with each source weighted by its conductance. A source with a low internal resistance has a large say; a source with a high internal resistance has little influence.

![Figure 15.6 — Three batteries of slightly different voltages connected in parallel.](fig/ch15_millman.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 15.5 — Batteries in parallel**

Three 12 V lead-acid batteries in different states of charge are connected in parallel (Figure 15.6). Their EMFs and internal resistances are 12.6 V and 0.05 Ω, 12.4 V and 0.04 Ω, and 12.0 V and 0.1 Ω. With no load connected, find the common terminal voltage and the current in each battery.

**Step 1: Conductances.** 1/0.05 = 20 S; 1/0.04 = 25 S; 1/0.1 = 10 S. Total 55 S.

**Step 2: Weighted sum.** 12.6 × 20 + 12.4 × 25 + 12.0 × 10 = 252 + 310 + 120 = 682 A.

**Step 3: Common voltage.** V = 682 / 55 = **12.4 V**.

**Step 4: Battery currents** (positive means the battery is delivering current):

- Battery 1: (12.6 − 12.4) / 0.05 = **4 A delivered**.
- Battery 2: (12.4 − 12.4) / 0.04 = **0 A**.
- Battery 3: (12.0 − 12.4) / 0.1 = −4 A, so **4 A absorbed**: it is being charged by battery 1.

**Comment.** With nothing connected, 4 A circulates continuously from the fullest battery into the emptiest. This wastes energy and, with modern lithium cells and their very low internal resistance, can produce dangerously large currents. It is the reason, first met in Chapter 5, why battery packs are built from carefully matched cells.
:::

## 15.5 Norton With Dependent Sources

Everything from Section 14.5 carries over. When dependent sources are present, find I~N~ (the short-circuit current) with all sources active, find V~oc~ or use a test source for R~N~, and never switch off a dependent source.

::: example
**Worked Example 15.6 — The dependent-source network in Norton form**

Find the Norton equivalent of the network in Figure 14.5.

**Solution.** In Worked Example 14.5 we found I~sc~ = 3 A and R~Th~ = 1.33 Ω. So I~N~ = **3 A** and R~N~ = **1.33 Ω**. Check: I~N~R~N~ = 3 × 1.33 = 4 V = V~oc~ ✓.
:::

## 15.6 Thévenin or Norton?

Since the two equivalents are interchangeable, the choice is a matter of convenience and insight.

**Choose Thévenin when:**

- the load is in **series** with other elements you want to add on;
- the source is close to a voltage source in practice (a battery, a regulated supply, the output of most amplifiers), so R~Th~ is small;
- you want the load **voltage** directly, or you will use the voltage divider rule.

**Choose Norton when:**

- the load is in **parallel** with other elements;
- the source behaves more like a current source in practice (a solar cell, a photodiode, a transistor's output, an LED driver), so R~N~ is large;
- the short-circuit current is easier to calculate than the open-circuit voltage;
- you want the load **current** directly, or you will use the current divider rule.

In practice, experienced engineers switch between the two forms freely, often several times while working through a single problem, exactly as we did in Worked Example 15.4.

::: tip
**In Practice: Real Sources Lie Somewhere in Between**

No real source is ideal. A good bench power supply has an output resistance of a few milliohms – it is very close to an ideal voltage source, and the Thévenin form is natural. A photodiode delivers a current proportional to the light falling on it and has a parallel resistance of many megohms – it is very close to an ideal current source, and the Norton form is natural. Both forms are always valid; choosing the one that matches the physics makes the numbers easier and your intuition sharper.
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Getting the Norton current direction wrong.** It points towards the terminal that is + in the Thévenin form.
- **Transforming an ideal source with no resistor.** Source transformation needs a series resistor (voltage source) or a parallel resistor (current source).
- **Using the internal resistor current from a transformed circuit.** Only terminal behaviour is preserved.
- **Forgetting that R~N~ = R~Th~.** You never need to calculate it twice.
- **Switching off dependent sources** when finding R~N~.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Norton's theorem**: any linear two-terminal network is equivalent to a current source I~N~ in parallel with R~N~.
- **I~N~** is the short-circuit current; **R~N~ = R~Th~**.
- **Conversion**: I~N~ = V~Th~/R~Th~ and V~Th~ = I~N~R~N~.
- A source's **terminal characteristic** is a straight line from (0, V~Th~) to (I~N~, 0), with slope −R~Th~.
- **Source transformation**: E in series with R ⟷ E/R in parallel with R. Use it to simplify circuits step by step.
- **Millman's theorem**: the common voltage of parallel sources is the conductance-weighted average of their EMFs.
- Choose **Thévenin** for series and voltage-like problems, **Norton** for parallel and current-like problems.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

I~N~ = I~sc~     R~N~ = R~Th~     I~N~ = V~Th~ / R~Th~     V = (Σ E~k~/R~k~) / (Σ 1/R~k~)
:::

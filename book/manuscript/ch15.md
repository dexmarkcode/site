# Testing, Diagnosis and the Next Generation

::: chapter-intro
The final chapter turns from how electric vehicles work to how engineers prove that they work and find out why they do not. We look at the layers of testing from simulation to the proving ground, at battery, drive and inverter test methods, at diagnostics and fleet data, and at a structured approach to troubleshooting that ties together the Case Files of every earlier chapter. We then look ahead, with a critical eye, at the technologies most likely to shape the next generation of electric vehicles, and close with some thoughts on the engineer's role in what comes next.
:::

Every chapter of this book has ended with a Case File: a problem that arrived as a complaint, a failed test or a field report, and was solved by stating it in numbers, drawing a boundary, building a simple model, measuring, finding the dominant term, checking the fix and writing it down. I chose that structure deliberately. In my experience, the engineers who are most valuable on an electric vehicle programme are not always those who know the most about one component. They are those who can walk into a room where a problem has defeated everybody for a week, ask the right three questions, and find out where to look.

That skill rests on two foundations. One is the physical understanding built up in the previous fourteen chapters. The other is knowledge of how vehicles are tested and how evidence is gathered, which is the subject of the first half of this chapter. The second half looks forward, because an engineer who understands the present well is also the person best placed to judge the claims made for the future.

## The Layers of Testing

No single test can prove that a vehicle is fit for production. Instead, evidence is gathered at many levels, each faster and cheaper than the one above it but less representative of reality. Figure 15.1 shows the layers.

![**Figure 15.1** The layers of testing in EV development. Simulation and model-in-the-loop testing are fast and cheap; hardware-in-the-loop and component benches add real hardware; vehicle and fleet testing are the most realistic and the most expensive. A problem found low in the pyramid costs far less to fix than one found at the top.](figures/fig15_1.png)

**Simulation.** Vehicle energy models like those of Chapter 2, electrochemical and equivalent-circuit battery models, finite-element motor models and circuit simulations of converters let engineers explore designs before any hardware exists. *Model-in-the-loop* (MIL) testing runs control algorithms against plant models in simulation, and *software-in-the-loop* (SIL) runs the actual production code the same way.

**Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL).** The real control unit, a BMS or an inverter controller, is connected to a real-time simulator that emulates the battery, motor or vehicle it controls, down to sensor signals and actuator responses. HIL allows thousands of test cases, including dangerous faults such as cell over-voltage or short circuits, to be run safely and repeatably. Power HIL extends this to real power levels, for example emulating a battery to test a charger.

**Component test benches.** Battery cyclers and climatic chambers test cells, modules and packs. Dynamometers test motors and complete drive units. Double-pulse testers characterise power semiconductors. Each bench measures one component under controlled conditions.

**Vehicle testing.** Complete vehicles are tested on chassis dynamometers in climatic chambers, on proving grounds, and on public roads in extreme climates: hot-weather testing in deserts, cold-weather testing above the Arctic Circle, altitude testing in mountains. Durability programmes run vehicles day and night to accumulate hundreds of thousands of kilometres before launch.

**Fleet data.** Once vehicles are in customers' hands, telematics data from thousands or millions of cars becomes the largest test programme of all. Used carefully and with proper respect for privacy, it reveals problems no laboratory would find.

## Testing the Battery

### Characterisation

Battery characterisation measures the parameters needed for models and for the BMS. Standard tests include:

- **Capacity tests** at defined currents and temperatures, often C/3 at 25 °C as a reference.
- **Open-circuit voltage curves**, measured by charging or discharging in small steps and resting at each, or by very slow continuous cycling, in both directions to capture hysteresis.
- **Pulse resistance tests**, such as the *hybrid pulse power characterisation* (HPPC) test, which applies discharge and charge pulses at a series of states of charge and temperatures and records the voltage response. From these, the equivalent-circuit parameters of Chapter 4 are fitted.
- **Thermal characterisation**, using calorimeters to measure heat generation and the entropic coefficient.

::: example
**Worked Example 15.1 — Reading an HPPC pulse**

A 100 Ah cell at 50 % SoC and 25 °C rests at 3.700 V. A 10-second, 100 A discharge pulse is applied. The voltage immediately after the current starts is 3.580 V, and at the end of the pulse it is 3.548 V. Estimate *R*~0~ and the 10-second resistance, and the parameters of a single RC element if the RC time constant is found to be 20 s.

*Solution.* *R*~0~ = (3.700 − 3.580) / 100 = 1.20 mΩ.

10-second resistance = (3.700 − 3.548) / 100 = 1.52 mΩ.

The RC element contributes 0.32 mΩ after 10 s. With *τ* = 20 s, that equals *R*~1~(1 − e^−0.5^) = 0.393*R*~1~, so *R*~1~ = 0.32 / 0.393 = 0.81 mΩ and *C*~1~ = *τ*/*R*~1~ = 20 / 0.00081 ≈ 24 700 F.

These are the parameters of Worked Example 4.1, recovered from a measurement. In practice the fit uses the whole voltage curve and often two or three RC elements, and the test is repeated across the full matrix of SoC and temperature.
:::

### Ageing tests

Lifetime cannot be measured in real time before launch: a vehicle programme cannot wait ten years. Instead, cells are aged under accelerated conditions, at elevated temperatures, high SoC and aggressive cycles, in a carefully designed *test matrix*. Calendar ageing tests store cells at combinations of temperature and SoC; cycle ageing tests cycle them with different depths of discharge, C-rates and temperatures. The results are fitted to ageing models, often based on the Arrhenius relationship of Chapter 4 and on empirical dependence on SoC and throughput, which are then used to predict life under real customer usage profiles. The art lies in accelerating ageing without triggering mechanisms, such as lithium plating at very high C-rates, that would not occur in real use, which would make the predictions misleadingly pessimistic, or missing ones that would, which would make them dangerously optimistic.

### Abuse and safety tests

Cells and packs must pass abuse tests that show they fail safely. Examples include the UN 38.3 tests required for transporting lithium batteries, and the requirements of UN Regulation No. 100 Part II and of national standards such as GB 38031 for traction batteries: vibration, mechanical shock, crush, fire exposure, external short circuit, overcharge, over-discharge, over-temperature, immersion and the thermal propagation test described in Chapter 5. These tests are destructive, expensive and sometimes spectacular, and are carried out in purpose-built bunkers.

## Testing Drives and Power Electronics

Drive units are tested on dynamometers that can absorb and supply full power at full speed. Efficiency maps (Chapter 8) are measured point by point with precision power analysers and torque transducers. Thermal tests run continuous and repeated peak cycles to confirm derating behaviour and component temperatures, using thermocouples embedded in windings and, for rotors, wireless telemetry or infrared measurement. *NVH* tests in semi-anechoic rooms record sound and vibration across the operating range. Durability tests run accelerated duty cycles for thousands of hours.

Power electronics adds its own specialised tests. The *double-pulse test* switches a single device on and off twice at controlled current and voltage to measure switching energies and waveforms, which are the data behind Chapter 10's loss calculations. *Power cycling* tests heat and cool modules repeatedly by passing current through them, to verify the lifetime models. Insulation and partial-discharge tests check the high-voltage design. EMC tests in anechoic chambers check emissions and immunity.

## Diagnostics and Data

When something goes wrong in the field, the evidence comes from diagnostics. Every control unit monitors its inputs, outputs and internal states, and stores *diagnostic trouble codes* (DTCs) with *freeze-frame* data, a snapshot of key variables at the moment a fault was detected. Workshop tools read these over the diagnostic connector using the Unified Diagnostic Services protocol (ISO 14229). Connected vehicles can report faults and key data directly to the manufacturer.

That connectivity has changed how manufacturers find and fix problems. Instead of waiting for warranty claims to accumulate, engineers can watch distributions of key quantities, such as cell voltage spread, isolation resistance, inverter temperatures and charging power, across the whole fleet, and spot *outliers* and *trends* early.

::: example
**Worked Example 15.2 — Finding the outlier**

Across 5 000 cars of one model in a similar climate, the average winter consumption over a month is 17.2 kWh per 100 km, with a standard deviation of 0.9 kWh per 100 km. One car averages 20.4. How unusual is it? If consumption is roughly normally distributed, how many cars in the fleet would you expect to exceed 20.4 by chance?

*Solution.* *z* = (20.4 − 17.2) / 0.9 = 3.56 standard deviations above the mean.

For a normal distribution, the probability of exceeding *z* = 3.56 is about 0.0002, so about one car in 5 000 would be expected that high by chance alone.

One such car is therefore not alarming in itself; it could be a driver with a long, cold, stop-start commute. But if twenty cars from the same production week appeared above *z* = 3, that cluster would deserve investigation. Statistical thinking turns fleet data from noise into evidence, provided that you also understand what differs between the cars, such as usage, climate and specification.
:::

## A Structured Approach to Troubleshooting

The seven-step method of Chapter 1 has run through every Case File. It is worth adding some tools that make each step more effective.

**State it in numbers.** Write a *problem statement* that says what is wrong, where, when, how much, and also what is *not* wrong: which vehicles, conditions or components are unaffected. The "is / is not" comparison is often the fastest route to the cause. In Case File 13, the fact that the failures occurred only at one brand of charger was the key clue.

**Draw the boundary.** A *fault tree*, starting from the top-level symptom and branching into every possible contributing cause, keeps the investigation systematic. An *Ishikawa* (fishbone) diagram groups causes into categories such as design, manufacture, materials, environment, usage and measurement. Figure 15.2 shows a fault tree for one of the commonest complaints, reduced range.

![**Figure 15.2** A fault tree for "range lower than expected". Each branch can be tested against data: consumption and its components, usable energy, and the range estimate itself. Most investigations find the answer in one or two branches.](figures/fig15_2.png)

**Build a simple model.** An energy balance, a resistance calculation, a thermal network, a timing diagram: the simplest model that could explain the size of the gap. If the model cannot explain it, the boundary was drawn wrongly.

**Estimate, then measure.** Predict what you should see before you look. Measure with instruments whose accuracy you know. Be wary of measurements that agree with your expectation too readily.

**Find the dominant term.** Rank causes by their contribution. The Pareto principle applies to engineering problems as much as anywhere: usually one or two causes account for most of the effect.

**Cross-check the fix.** Ask what else the fix touches. Every Case File in this book had a cross-check, because every fix pushes on something else.

**Write it down.** Record the problem, evidence, cause and fix, and feed the lessons back into requirements, design rules, test cases and the safety analysis. A problem solved but not recorded will be solved again, at greater cost, by somebody else.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Intermittent interlock fault after a workshop visit**

*Symptom.* After a routine service that involved removing an under-floor panel, a car intermittently reports a high-voltage interlock fault and refuses to close its contactors, particularly after driving over rough roads.

*Likely causes.* (1) A high-voltage connector or service cover not fully seated, so the interlock contact opens with vibration. (2) A damaged interlock wire or pin, perhaps pinched when the panel was refitted. (3) A connector seal displaced, admitting water. (4) A genuine fault in the interlock monitoring circuit, unrelated to the service.

*How to tell them apart.* Check the service record for which connectors and covers were disturbed. Read the interlock-loop diagnostic data: some systems report loop resistance or which segment opened. Inspect every connector in the affected area for full engagement, locking-lever position and pin damage. Gentle wiggle testing while monitoring the loop often reproduces the fault. Treat any high-voltage connector disturbance as a priority and follow the manufacturer's safety procedures throughout.
:::

## Looking Ahead: Judging New Technology

Electric vehicle technology moves fast, and every month brings announcements of breakthroughs. Some will change the industry; many will not. Chapter 1 suggested a question to ask of any new technology: *which curve does it bend?* Here are a few more, which together form a practical checklist:

1. **What does it improve, and by how much, at which level?** A 50 % gain in cell energy density may be a 20 % gain at pack level once packaging and safety are included.
2. **What does it make worse?** Every technology trades something: cost, life, power, safety, temperature range, manufacturability.
3. **How mature is it?** Has it been demonstrated in a laboratory cell, a full-size cell, a pack, a vehicle, a fleet? *Technology readiness* and *manufacturing readiness* are different things; many technologies that work in the laboratory fail to scale.
4. **What will it cost at scale, and when?** The incumbent technology keeps improving while the new one matures. A challenger must beat where the incumbent will be, not where it is today.
5. **Does it fit the existing supply chain and factories?** Technologies that use existing equipment spread fast; those that need new factories spread slowly.

With those questions in mind, here is my assessment of the main technologies on the horizon at the time of writing. Treat it as a way of thinking rather than a forecast, and expect some of the details to have moved by the time you read this.

### Batteries

**Solid-state batteries** replace the liquid electrolyte with a solid. Paired with a lithium-metal anode, they promise much higher energy density, potentially faster charging and better safety. The difficulties are formidable: keeping intimate contact between solid layers as they expand and contract, preventing lithium dendrites, manufacturing thin, defect-free solid electrolyte layers at scale, and cost. Several companies have demonstrated large-format cells, and small-volume vehicle applications are emerging. Expect gradual introduction, possibly first as "semi-solid" designs with some liquid retained, rather than a sudden switch.

**Sodium-ion** cells use abundant materials and perform well in the cold, with lower energy density than LFP. They bend the *cost and supply security* curve rather than the energy curve, which makes them attractive for small cars, stationary storage and as part of hybrid packs that mix chemistries. They are already in production.

**Silicon-rich anodes** raise energy density and can improve fast charging, with the challenge of swelling discussed in Chapter 4. Silicon content is rising steadily, a classic incremental improvement on existing production lines.

**LMFP and manganese-rich cathodes** offer steps up in energy over LFP while retaining low cost and good safety, and reduce dependence on nickel and cobalt.

**Manufacturing innovations**, such as *dry electrode coating*, which eliminates the solvent drying ovens that consume much of a cell factory's energy and floor space, may matter as much for cost and carbon footprint as any new chemistry.

### Motors and power electronics

**Rare-earth-free machines**, wound-rotor and advanced reluctance designs, will grow as manufacturers seek supply security, and improved ferrite designs may find niches. **Axial flux** motors will spread in performance applications. Direct oil cooling and hairpin innovations will keep raising power density.

In power electronics, **silicon carbide** will continue to displace silicon as costs fall with larger wafers. **Gallium nitride** may move from chargers into traction inverters, particularly for 400 V systems, if higher-voltage and higher-current devices mature. Higher system voltages above 800 V are possible for heavy vehicles. **Integration**, combining inverter, charger, DC-DC converter and even battery management into shared hardware, is likely to be the biggest source of cost reduction.

### Charging and the grid

Charging will get faster, mainly by better thermal management of cells and packs rather than by higher-power chargers alone. **Bidirectional charging** and **smart charging** will turn the vehicle fleet into a large, flexible resource for the electricity system, provided that the commercial arrangements and standards mature. **Megawatt charging** will make battery-electric long-haul trucks practical on major routes.

::: casefile
**Case File 15 — The update that cost range**

*The complaint.* Two weeks after an over-the-air software update, owner forums fill with reports that winter range has fallen by 10 to 15 %. The update notes mention only "improved fast-charging performance in cold weather".

*State it in numbers.* Fleet data comparing 20 000 updated cars with 8 000 not yet updated, in the same regions and the same weeks, show average consumption 1.9 kWh per 100 km higher in updated cars at ambient temperatures below 0 °C, and no difference above 10 °C. The difference is largest for short trips.

*Draw the boundary.* Everything the update changed: battery thermal strategy, preconditioning logic, the motor heat mode used to warm the battery, the range estimator and the BMS power limits.

*Simplest model.* An extra 1.9 kWh per 100 km on short winter trips at around 40 km/h average corresponds to about 0.75 kW of additional average power. The update's purpose was to arrive at rapid chargers with a warmer battery. If the new logic warms the battery more often, not just before rapid charging, that would explain extra consumption of this size, concentrated in cold weather and short trips.

*Measure.* Logs from updated cars show that the motor heat mode now activates whenever battery temperature falls below 10 °C and the predicted trip is longer than 15 minutes, regardless of whether a charging stop is planned. The intended trigger was "navigation to a rapid charger". A condition in the code meant to combine the two was written as "or" instead of "and".

*Dominant term.* Unintended battery heating on ordinary journeys.

*Fix and cross-check.* A corrective update restores the intended logic within ten days. Fleet data confirm consumption returns to previous levels while the improvement in cold-weather fast-charging is retained. The validation process for future updates adds a fleet-level consumption comparison on a small group of updated vehicles in cold regions before wide release, and a review checklist for changes to thermal triggers. The manufacturer publishes an explanation to owners.

*Lesson.* Software updates are engineering changes and deserve the same rigour. Fleet data with a proper comparison group found the problem in days; without it, the investigation could have taken months.
:::

## A Closing Word

We began this book with a car that moved silently away from a standstill as though pushed from behind, and with the observation that its smoothness hides a great deal of engineering. I hope that you now see what lies behind that smoothness: a battery whose chemistry, structure, cooling and management are all designed around the limits of a few microns of electrode; a motor whose torque comes from carefully shaped magnetic fields and whose limits are set by heat; an inverter switching hundreds of amps in nanoseconds under the watchful eye of control software; a charging network built on standards negotiated across an industry; and a vehicle in which all of these must cooperate, safely, for fifteen years.

Electric vehicles are not the end of the story of transport. They raise real questions: about the sourcing and recycling of materials, about the electricity that powers them, about how much car we need. Engineers have a voice in answering those questions, and it is a stronger voice when it rests on numbers rather than slogans. The methods in this book, of following the energy, stating problems quantitatively, modelling simply and measuring carefully, apply just as well to those wider questions as to a failing contactor or a stubborn range shortfall.

Whatever part of this field you work in, I wish you interesting problems and the patience to solve them properly. And when a colleague asks, "but why does it do that?", I hope you will be the one who finds out.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Testing proceeds in layers: simulation (MIL, SIL), hardware-in-the-loop, component benches, vehicle testing and fleet data. Problems found early cost far less.
- Battery testing covers characterisation (capacity, OCV, HPPC pulses, thermal), accelerated ageing with careful test matrices, and destructive abuse and propagation tests.
- Drive and power electronics testing includes dynamometer efficiency and thermal tests, NVH, double-pulse switching characterisation, power cycling, insulation and EMC.
- Diagnostics (DTCs, freeze frames, UDS) and fleet telematics, analysed statistically, reveal problems early.
- Effective troubleshooting combines "is / is not" problem statements, fault trees and fishbone diagrams, simple models, careful measurement, ranking of causes, cross-checks and documentation.
- Judge new technology by which curve it bends, what it trades away, its maturity, its cost at scale against an improving incumbent, and its fit with existing manufacturing.
- Solid-state, sodium-ion, silicon anodes, LMFP, dry electrodes, rare-earth-free motors, SiC and GaN, bidirectional and megawatt charging each address different curves on different timescales.
:::

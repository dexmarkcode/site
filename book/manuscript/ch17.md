A carpenter does not use a chisel to drive in a nail. Nor, if they are any good, do they spend ten minutes deciding which tool to pick up. Years of practice have taught them to glance at a job and reach for the right tool almost without thinking.

You now have a full toolbox for analysing linear circuits: Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws, series–parallel reduction and the divider rules, delta–star transformation, nodal and mesh analysis with their supernodes and supermeshes, superposition, Thévenin, Norton, source transformation and maximum power transfer. Every one of them can solve a great many problems. The skill that remains is choosing the right one quickly, applying it cleanly and checking the result convincingly.

That skill is what this chapter is about. It is, in a sense, the most practical chapter in the book. In examinations, the students who do best are rarely those who know the most; they are those who choose a sensible method in the first minute and then work through it calmly. In professional life, the same habit separates engineers who solve problems from those who generate pages of algebra.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- summarise the strengths and weaknesses of each analysis method;
- count the equations each method would need before starting;
- follow a decision process for choosing a method;
- solve the same circuit by several methods and compare the effort;
- apply a systematic set of checks to any answer;
- approach unfamiliar and design-type problems with confidence.
:::

## 17.2 Count Before You Calculate

The single most useful habit you can develop is to **count the unknowns** each systematic method would need before you write anything else. It takes thirty seconds and frequently halves the work.

**Nodal analysis.** Start with the number of nodes, n. Subtract one for the reference. Then subtract one for each voltage source, because a voltage source either fixes a node voltage (if it is connected to the reference) or links two node voltages through a constraint (if it is floating).

::: eq
nodal unknowns = (n − 1) − (number of voltage sources)
:::

**Mesh analysis.** Start with the number of meshes, m = b − n + 1. Subtract one for each current source, because a current source either fixes a mesh current (if it is on the outer edge) or links two mesh currents (if it is shared).

::: eq
mesh unknowns = m − (number of current sources)
:::

(These counts are for planar circuits whose sources do not form awkward loops or cut-sets on their own, which covers almost everything you will meet. Dependent sources count like independent ones for this purpose, but each adds an extra relation for its controlling variable.)

Whichever number is smaller usually indicates the quicker method. If they are equal, choose the one whose equations look simpler, or the one that gives the quantity you want most directly.

The first box is the most important and the most often skipped. **Redraw the circuit neatly and label every node.** Many circuits in textbooks and examinations are drawn awkwardly, sometimes deliberately. A clean redraw, with the reference node as a single rail along the bottom, often reveals structure that was hidden – a series pair, a parallel group, a source in parallel with a resistor that can be transformed. And be absolutely clear about what you are being asked to find. A question asking for "the current in R~5~" needs a different approach from one asking for "all branch currents".

## 17.4 One Circuit, Four Methods

To see the decision process in action, let us take a single circuit and solve it in several ways, noting the effort each method requires.

![Figure 17.2 — The case-study circuit. We want the current in R~L~ and the power delivered by each source.](fig/ch17_case.svg){width=86%}

::: example
**Worked Example 17.1 — Choosing a method for Figure 17.2**

Find the current in R~L~ and the power delivered by each source in Figure 17.2. First count the unknowns for nodal and mesh analysis.

**Counting.** There are three nodes (1, 2 and the bottom rail), so n − 1 = 2. There is one voltage source, but it is connected in series with the 4 Ω resistor rather than directly to node 1, so it creates its own small node at the top of the source, whose voltage is fixed at 24 V. That extra node and its fixed voltage cancel out: **nodal analysis needs 2 equations.** There are three meshes, and one current source on the outer edge: **mesh analysis needs 2 equations.**

It is a tie. Nodal analysis gives voltages directly and the circuit is drawn with a natural reference rail, so let us start there.

**Method 1: Nodal analysis.**

Node 1: (V~1~ − 24)/4 + V~1~/12 + (V~1~ − V~2~)/6 = 0. Multiply by 12: 6V~1~ − 2V~2~ = 72.

Node 2: (V~2~ − V~1~)/6 + V~2~/3 − 2 = 0. Multiply by 6: −V~1~ + 3V~2~ = 12.

From the second, V~1~ = 3V~2~ − 12. Substituting: 18V~2~ − 72 − 2V~2~ = 72, so 16V~2~ = 144, **V~2~ = 9 V** and **V~1~ = 15 V**.

Current in R~L~: 9/3 = **3 A**.

**Method 2: Mesh analysis.** Clockwise mesh currents I~1~ (left: source, 4 Ω, 12 Ω), I~2~ (middle: 12 Ω, 6 Ω, R~L~) and I~3~ (right: R~L~, current source). The current source pushes 2 A *up* the right-hand edge, which is anticlockwise for mesh 3, so I~3~ = −2 A.

Mesh 1: 16I~1~ − 12I~2~ = 24.

Mesh 2: −12I~1~ + (12 + 6 + 3)I~2~ − 3I~3~ = 0, so −12I~1~ + 21I~2~ + 6 = 0.

From mesh 1, I~1~ = 1.5 + 0.75I~2~. Substituting: −18 − 9I~2~ + 21I~2~ + 6 = 0, so **I~2~ = 1 A** and **I~1~ = 2.25 A**.

Current in R~L~ (downwards): I~2~ − I~3~ = 1 − (−2) = **3 A** ✓.

**Method 3: Thévenin.** Remove R~L~ and find the equivalent seen from node 2.

V~oc~: with R~L~ removed, node 2 receives the 2 A source current, which must all flow through the 6 Ω into node 1. Node 1 then has (V~1~ − 24)/4 + V~1~/12 = 2, giving V~1~ = 24 V, so V~2~ = 24 + 2 × 6 = 36 V. So V~Th~ = 36 V.

R~Th~: short the 24 V source, open the 2 A source. From node 2: 6 Ω in series with (4 ∥ 12) = 6 + 3 = 9 Ω.

I~L~ = 36 / (9 + 3) = **3 A** ✓.

**Method 4: Superposition.**

24 V alone (current source open): the left part has a Thévenin equivalent at node 1 of 24 × 12/16 = 18 V with 4 ∥ 12 = 3 Ω. Then V~2~′ = 18 × 3/(3 + 6 + 3) = 4.5 V.

2 A alone (voltage source shorted): the resistance seen at node 2 is 3 ∥ (6 + 4 ∥ 12) = 3 ∥ 9 = 2.25 Ω, so V~2~″ = 2 × 2.25 = 4.5 V.

V~2~ = 4.5 + 4.5 = 9 V, and I~L~ = **3 A** ✓.

**Source powers.** The 24 V source carries (24 − 15)/4 = 2.25 A out of its + terminal: it delivers **54 W**. The current source pushes 2 A into node 2 at 9 V: it delivers **18 W**. Total 72 W. Resistors: 4 Ω: 20.25 W; 12 Ω: 18.75 W; 6 Ω: 6 W; R~L~: 27 W; total 72 W ✓.
:::

All four methods agree, as they must. Which was best? For the single question "what is the current in R~L~?", Thévenin was arguably the most elegant, and it would win outright if we were then asked to try several different loads. For "find all the powers", nodal analysis was the natural choice because it gave both node voltages at once. Superposition gave the satisfying insight that the two sources contribute *equally* to V~2~. Mesh analysis was perfectly serviceable but offered no special advantage here.

The lesson is not that one method is "right". It is that a moment's thought about **what you want** and **how many unknowns each method needs** will steer you to an efficient solution.

## 17.5 Checking Your Answers

An answer you have not checked is an answer you should not trust. Professional engineers check everything, because a wrong number in a design can cost money, time or, in some industries, lives. Here is a set of checks, roughly in order of how quickly they can be done.

**1. Units and orders of magnitude.** Does the answer have the right unit? Is it a sensible size? A current of 3 kA in a circuit of a few ohms and a 24 V battery is clearly wrong. So is a resistance of −5 Ω for a passive network.

**2. Limits.** Does the answer behave sensibly in extreme cases? If a resistor were made zero or infinite, would the answer change in the way you expect? For example, as R~L~ → 0 the load voltage should go to zero; as R~L~ → ∞ it should approach V~Th~.

**3. Bounds.** In a circuit with only voltage sources and resistors, every node voltage must lie between the lowest and highest source voltages (when measured from a common reference). In a parallel group, the equivalent must be less than the smallest resistor; in series, more than the largest.

**4. KCL at a spare node.** If you used nodal analysis, check KCL by computing the branch currents and adding them. If you used mesh analysis, pick any node and check that the currents balance.

**5. KVL round a spare loop.** If you used mesh analysis, compute node voltages and check them. If you used nodal analysis, walk round a loop you did not use.

**6. Power balance.** The total power delivered by the sources must equal the total power absorbed. This catches almost every error, because it involves every element in the circuit.

**7. A second method.** For important results, solve again by a different method. If the two agree, you can be very confident.

::: tip
**Thornton's Two-Minute Rule**

Whenever you finish a calculation, spend two minutes on checks 1, 2 and either 4 or 6 before moving on. In my experience this catches more than nine-tenths of all errors, and in an examination it is the best use of two minutes you will ever make.
:::

## 17.6 Tackling Unfamiliar Problems

Sooner or later you will meet a problem that does not look like any example in any book. Here is a general approach, adapted from the work of the mathematician George Pólya on problem solving.

**Understand the problem.** What is given? What is wanted? Draw the circuit. Label everything. Restate the question in your own words.

**Devise a plan.** Have you seen a similar problem? Can you solve a simpler version first – with one source instead of two, or with a resistor removed? Can you split the circuit into parts and use Thévenin to connect them? Can you work backwards from the answer?

**Carry out the plan.** Work neatly, one step at a time. Keep symbols as long as you sensibly can. Do not skip steps to save time; skipped steps are where errors hide.

**Look back.** Check the answer. Ask what you learned and whether there was a quicker route.

### Design problems

Design problems turn analysis inside out. Instead of "here is the circuit, find the current", they ask "find the component values that give this current". They feel harder at first, but the same tools apply. Usually the trick is to write the analysis equation with the unknown component as a symbol, then solve for it.

![Figure 17.3 — A thermistor divider feeding an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) whose input resistance is 20 kΩ.](fig/ch17_sensor.svg){width=56%}

::: example
**Worked Example 17.2 — Designing a sensor interface**

A 10 kΩ NTC thermistor (10 kΩ at 25 °C) is placed at the top of a divider across 5 V, as in Worked Example 8.8. The output drives an ADC input with a resistance of 20 kΩ. Choose the fixed resistor R so that the output is exactly 2.5 V at 25 °C.

**Step 1: Think.** For 2.5 V – half the supply – the bottom half of the divider must have the same resistance as the top: 10 kΩ. But the bottom half is now R *in parallel with* the 20 kΩ ADC input.

**Step 2: Set up the equation.** R ∥ 20 kΩ = 10 kΩ:

{{frac|1|R}} + {{frac|1|20}} = {{frac|1|10}},   so   {{frac|1|R}} = {{frac|1|20}},   R = 20 kΩ

**Step 3: Compare with the naive design.** Someone ignoring the ADC's input resistance would choose R = 10 kΩ. Then the bottom half would be 10 ∥ 20 = 6.67 kΩ, and the output at 25 °C would be 5 × 6.67/16.67 = **2.0 V** – a 20 % error, which would translate into a temperature error of several degrees.

**Step 4: Check at another temperature.** At 50 °C the thermistor is 3.59 kΩ. With R = 20 kΩ, the bottom half is still 10 kΩ, so V~out~ = 5 × 10/13.59 = 3.68 V, matching Worked Example 8.8, which assumed an ideal (non-loading) output.

**Lesson.** Every design that connects one circuit to another must account for loading. Thévenin's theorem and the loaded-divider analysis of Chapter 8 are the tools; the habit of asking "what is connected to this output?" is the skill.
:::

### Estimation

Finally, cultivate the habit of **estimating** before calculating. Before solving Figure 17.2, you might reason: "The 24 V source and 4 Ω could push about 6 A; the 2 A source adds a little; R~L~ is the smallest resistor on the right, so it will take a good share; I expect a few amps." When the answer comes out as 3 A, you have instant confidence. If it came out as 300 A or 0.003 A, you would know immediately that something had gone wrong.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Starting to calculate before counting.** Thirty seconds of counting can save half an hour of algebra.
- **Skipping the redraw.** Many mistakes come from misreading a messy diagram.
- **Using a sledgehammer.** A single-source series–parallel circuit does not need mesh analysis.
- **Using a teaspoon.** A five-node, three-source circuit should not be attacked with repeated source transformations.
- **Not checking.** Every answer deserves at least a power balance or a spare KCL.
- **Ignoring loading in design problems.** Always ask what is connected to an output.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- Each method has a natural domain; Table 17.1 summarises them.
- **Count unknowns first**: nodal = (n − 1) − (voltage sources); mesh = m − (current sources).
- Follow a **decision process**: redraw and label; single source → reduction; one element or varying load → Thévenin/Norton; otherwise choose nodal or mesh by count.
- Different methods give the **same answer**; choose the one that gives what you want with least effort.
- **Check** every answer: units, limits, bounds, KCL, KVL, power balance, a second method.
- For **unfamiliar problems**: understand, plan, carry out, look back.
- For **design problems**, write the analysis with the unknown as a symbol, then solve; always account for **loading**.
- **Estimate** before you calculate.

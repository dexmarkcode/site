I began this book with a broken torch on my desk – a cheap plastic one, handed to me years ago by a student who could not see why it would not light. The fault turned out to be a corroded spring contact: an open circuit, an infinite resistance where there should have been almost none. Ten minutes with a meter, guided by a clear picture of what the circuit *should* be doing, was all it took.

We have travelled a long way since then. You have learned to reduce networks, to write and solve nodal and mesh equations, and to replace whole circuits by Thévenin and Norton equivalents. But every method in this book has rested on idealisations: resistors with exact values, wires with no resistance, sources that never tire, meters that disturb nothing. Real circuits are messier. Resistors have tolerances, wires have resistance, meters load circuits, connections corrode, and things break.

This final chapter brings the theory down to the workbench. We look at how real components differ from their ideal models, how to measure accurately, how to find faults systematically and safely, how professionals use simulation software, and where your study of circuits goes next. It is a chapter about using what you know in the real world – which is, after all, the point of knowing it.

- describe the basic safety principles for working on electrical circuits;

- explain how the methods of this book extend to capacitors, inductors, AC and non-linear devices.

## 18.1 Real Components Are Not Ideal

### Tolerance and worst-case analysis

In Chapter 2 we met resistor tolerance: a 10 kΩ ± 5 % resistor may be anything from 9.5 kΩ to 10.5 kΩ. When several toleranced components are combined, the uncertainty in the circuit's behaviour can be larger or smaller than any single tolerance, depending on how they interact.

The simplest and safest way to deal with this is **worst-case analysis**: set every component to whichever end of its tolerance pushes the result furthest in one direction, then repeat for the other direction.

::: example
**Worked Example 18.1 — A divider with 5 % resistors**

A divider of two 10 kΩ ± 5 % resistors is connected to 12 V. Find the nominal output and the worst-case range.

**Nominal.** 12 × 10/20 = 6.0 V.

**Lowest output.** Make R~2~ (bottom) as small as possible and R~1~ (top) as large as possible: R~2~ = 9.5 kΩ, R~1~ = 10.5 kΩ.

V~min~ = 12 × {{frac|9.5|10.5 + 9.5}} = 5.70 V

**Highest output.** R~2~ = 10.5 kΩ, R~1~ = 9.5 kΩ: V~max~ = 12 × 10.5/20 = 6.30 V.

**Answer:** 6.0 V nominal, anywhere from 5.70 V to 6.30 V in the worst case: ± 5 %.
:::

Worst-case analysis is conservative. In a batch of real circuits, it is very unlikely that every component sits at the worst extreme at once. Figure 18.1 shows the result of simulating twenty thousand such dividers with resistor values scattered randomly across their tolerance bands. Most outputs cluster near 6 V; very few approach the worst-case limits. This statistical approach, called **Monte Carlo analysis**, is built into most circuit simulators and lets designers trade a tiny risk of failure for cheaper components.

![Figure 18.1 — Outputs of 20 000 simulated dividers built from 10 kΩ ± 5 % resistors. Nearly all lie well inside the worst-case limits of 5.70 V and 6.30 V.](fig/ch18_tolerance.svg){width=86%}

### Other departures from the ideal

- **Temperature.** Resistance changes with temperature (Chapter 2). Precision circuits use resistors with a low temperature coefficient, quoted in parts per million per degree (ppm/°C).
- **Power rating.** A resistor run near its rating gets hot, and its value drifts. Keep resistors well within their ratings.
- **Wires and connections.** A metre of thin wire may have a resistance of 0.1 Ω; a corroded or loose connection may have far more. In low-voltage, high-current circuits these "invisible" resistances matter.
- **Sources.** Batteries have internal resistance that rises as they discharge and as they get cold. Power supplies have limits on the current they can deliver.
- **Meters.** As Chapter 8 showed, every meter draws some energy from the circuit.

None of this makes the ideal methods useless. It means that a good engineer uses them with judgement, adds the non-ideal elements to the model when they matter, and checks predictions against measurements.

## 18.2 Measuring Well

### Choosing what to measure

Measuring **voltage** is easy and safe: you touch the probes to two points without disturbing the circuit. Measuring **current** is harder: you must break the circuit to insert the meter, and a mistake can blow its fuse or worse. For this reason experienced engineers usually measure voltages and *calculate* currents using Ohm's law. If you know a resistor's value and measure the voltage across it, you know its current. Every resistor in a circuit is, in effect, a built-in ammeter.

### Measuring very small resistances: the four-wire method

Suppose you need to measure a current-sense resistor of 10 mΩ (0.010 Ω). An ordinary ohmmeter connects to it with two test leads, each of which might have a resistance of 0.1 Ω plus contact resistance at the probe tips. The meter measures the total: about 0.21 Ω. The leads have swamped the very thing you wanted to measure, giving a reading twenty times too large.

The solution, invented by Lord Kelvin in the nineteenth century, is the **four-wire** or **Kelvin** measurement shown in Figure 18.2. One pair of leads carries a known test current through the resistor. A *separate* pair of leads, connected right at the resistor's terminals, measures the voltage across it.

![Figure 18.2 — Four-wire (Kelvin) measurement. The test current flows through the outer leads; the voltmeter senses the voltage directly across R~x~ through separate leads that carry almost no current.](fig/ch18_kelvin.svg){width=78%}

The trick is that the voltmeter draws almost no current, so there is almost no voltage drop in the sense leads, however long or resistive they are. The voltmeter sees only the voltage across R~x~ itself. Dividing by the known test current gives R~x~ accurately. You will find Kelvin connections on precision ohmmeters, on battery testers and on the current-sense resistors of almost every modern power supply, whose circuit boards have separate "sense" tracks running to the resistor's pads for exactly this reason.

::: example
**Worked Example 18.2 — Two-wire versus four-wire**

A 10 mΩ resistor is measured using a test current of 1 A. Each lead has 0.1 Ω of resistance. Find the voltage reading with (a) a two-wire connection and (b) a four-wire connection, and the resulting resistance values.

**(a) Two-wire.** The meter measures the voltage across the resistor *and* both leads: 1 × (0.010 + 0.1 + 0.1) = 0.21 V, implying 0.21 Ω. Error: 2000 %.

**(b) Four-wire.** The sense leads carry negligible current, so they drop negligible voltage. The voltmeter reads 1 × 0.010 = 0.010 V, implying 10 mΩ. Correct.
:::

### Safety ratings

Multimeters carry a **category rating** (CAT II, CAT III, CAT IV) and a voltage, printed near the input sockets. These indicate the kind of installation the meter can safely be used on, from household appliances (CAT II) to distribution boards (CAT III) to the supply intake (CAT IV), where the energy available in a fault is enormous. Always use a meter and leads rated for the job, check them before use, and never rely on a cheap meter for mains work.

## 18.3 Finding Faults

Fault-finding is where circuit analysis earns its keep in daily life. Without analysis, you can only swap parts until something works. With analysis, you can predict what each measurement *should* be, compare it with what it *is*, and reason your way to the fault.

### A method

1. **Gather symptoms.** What exactly is wrong? What still works? When did it start?
2. **Know the healthy circuit.** Calculate, or look up, the expected voltages at key points.
3. **Measure, starting with the supply.** Is the power actually there? A surprising number of faults are a flat battery, a blown fuse or a switch in the wrong position.
4. **Compare and hypothesise.** Which single fault would produce *all* the readings you see?
5. **Test the hypothesis** with a further measurement before replacing anything.
6. **Fix, then verify** that every reading is now correct.

Node-voltage measurements are the workhorse. With the meter's black lead on the circuit's 0 V reference and the red lead touched to each test point in turn, you are measuring the node voltages of Chapter 10 directly. Every reading that differs from the expected value is a clue.

![Figure 18.3 — The series–parallel network of Chapter 7, with test points TP-A and TP-B. Normal readings are 40 V and 16 V.](fig/ch18_fault.svg){width=96%}

::: example
**Worked Example 18.3 — Building a fault table**

For the circuit of Figure 18.3, normal readings are TP-A = 40 V and TP-B = 16 V (Worked Example 7.2). Predict the readings for each of the following single faults, and hence build a table that a technician could use.

We re-analyse the circuit with each fault in place, using the reduction methods of Chapter 7. An open circuit is modelled as an infinite resistance, a short as zero.

::: {.table-wrap}
| Fault | TP-A (V) | TP-B (V) | Supply current (A) |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| None (healthy) | 40.0 | 16.0 | 4.0 |
| R~1~ open | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| R~2~ open | 48.0 | 19.2 | 2.4 |
| R~3~ open | 48.0 | 0 | 2.4 |
| R~4~ open | 43.2 | 28.8 | 3.4 |
| R~5~ open | 41.1 | 20.6 | 3.8 |
| R~4~ shorted | 36.0 | 0 | 4.8 |
| R~2~ shorted | 0 | 0 | 12.0 |

: Table 18.1 — Predicted test-point readings for single faults in Figure 18.3.
:::

**Using the table.** Suppose a technician measures TP-A = 48 V and TP-B = 0 V. Only one fault in the table matches: R~3~ open. If instead TP-A = 48 V and TP-B = 19.2 V, the culprit is R~2~ open. Notice how much information two readings carry. Notice too that some faults look alike at one test point and differ at the other – which is why several test points are better than one.
:::

Manufacturers of complex equipment produce exactly this kind of table, or its computerised equivalent, for their service engineers. Each line is simply a circuit analysis problem.

### The half-split method

When a fault lies somewhere along a long chain – a string of lamps, a series of amplifier stages, a long cable – the most efficient search strategy is to **test in the middle**. The result tells you which half contains the fault. Then test in the middle of that half, and so on.

![Figure 18.4 — Half-splitting a string of eight elements. Three tests are enough to locate one faulty element among eight.](fig/ch18_halfsplit.svg){width=90%}

With eight elements, testing one by one could take up to seven tests; half-splitting always takes three (because 2³ = 8). With 64 elements, it takes six instead of up to 63. My father, working along his fairy lights one bulb at a time each December, would have saved a great deal of time with this method – though I suspect he quite enjoyed the ritual.

::: example
**Worked Example 18.4 — Half-splitting a string of lamps**

Eight lamps in series across 230 V have stopped working. A voltmeter connected from the supply's neutral end to the junction between lamps 4 and 5 reads 0 V. What does this tell you, and where do you test next?

**Reasoning.** In a healthy, working string, the voltage at the midpoint would be about half the supply. With an open-circuit lamp somewhere, no current flows, so no healthy lamp drops any voltage. Every point on the neutral side of the break sits at the neutral potential (0 V), and every point on the live side sits at the full 230 V.

Our reading of 0 V at the midpoint means the midpoint is on the neutral side of the break: the faulty lamp is among lamps **5 to 8**, between the midpoint and the live end. (Had we read 230 V, it would be among lamps 1 to 4.)

**Next test.** Measure at the junction between lamps 6 and 7. If that reads 230 V, the break is in lamp 5 or 6; if 0 V, in lamp 7 or 8. One more test pins it down.

**Safety note.** This example involves mains voltage. In practice, such testing should only be done by a competent person using a properly rated meter, or better still with the supply disconnected, using a continuity test across each section.
:::

## 18.4 Working Safely

No book on practical circuits would be complete without a word on safety. The physics is straightforward: the current through your body depends on the voltage across it and its resistance, and a current of a few tens of milliamps through the chest can stop the heart. Dry skin may have a resistance of 100 kΩ or more, but wet or broken skin far less. At mains voltages, a lethal current is entirely possible.

The principles that keep engineers alive are simple, and they are written into UK law in the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989:

- **Work dead wherever possible.** Isolate the supply, lock it off if you can, and *prove* it is dead with a tested voltage indicator before touching anything.
- **Treat every conductor as live until proven otherwise.**
- **Beware of stored energy.** Large capacitors in power supplies and motor drives can hold a dangerous charge long after the supply is removed. Discharge them safely and check.
- **Use the right equipment.** Meters and leads must be rated for the circuit; insulated tools; appropriate protective equipment.
- **Know your limits.** Mains installation work in the UK is regulated. If you are not qualified, do not do it.

The battery-powered and low-voltage circuits you will build while learning are generally safe to the touch, but they can still deliver enough current to burn fingers, melt wires or start fires – a short-circuited lithium battery is a genuine fire hazard. Respect every circuit.

## 18.5 Letting the Computer Help: SPICE

In Chapter 10 I mentioned SPICE, the circuit simulator that uses nodal analysis to solve circuits automatically. Free versions such as LTspice, ngspice and Qucs-S are widely available, and many engineers check every design with one before building it.

A SPICE simulator reads a **netlist**: a text description of the circuit in which each line names a component, the two nodes it connects and its value. Node 0 is always the reference. Here is the netlist for the case-study circuit of Figure 17.2:

```
* Case study from Chapter 17
V1  a  0  24
R1  a  1  4
R2  1  0  12
R3  1  2  6
RL  2  0  3
I1  0  2  2
.op
.end
```

Each line reads naturally once you know the format. V1 is a 24 V source from node a (positive) to node 0. R1 is 4 Ω between a and 1. I1 is a 2 A current source; by SPICE convention its current flows from the first node through the source to the second, so this one pushes 2 A into node 2. The `.op` line asks for the DC operating point. Running it produces V(1) = 15 V and V(2) = 9 V, exactly as we found by hand.

Writing a netlist is precisely the "label the nodes and list the connections" method of Chapter 4. If you can do that, you can use SPICE.

::: tip
**Use Simulation Wisely**

A simulator will happily give you a precise, wrong answer if you give it a wrong circuit. Use it to check your hand calculations and to explore "what if" questions quickly – but always estimate the answer first and be suspicious of any result you cannot explain. The engineer who understands the circuit is in charge; the computer is the assistant.
:::

## 18.6 The Road Ahead

Everything in this book has dealt with **linear resistive circuits driven by DC sources**. That is the right foundation, and it is not a small one. But it is a foundation, and you will build on it. Here is a brief look at what comes next, and how the ideas you now own carry forward.

### Capacitors, inductors and time

A **capacitor** stores energy in an electric field; an **inductor** stores it in a magnetic field. Unlike resistors, their voltage and current are related through rates of change, so circuits containing them respond over time. When a capacitor charges through a resistor from a DC supply, its voltage rises along a smooth curve (Figure 18.5). The speed of the response is set by the **time constant**, τ = RC: after one time constant the capacitor has reached about 63 % of its final voltage, and after five it is effectively fully charged.

![Figure 18.5 — A capacitor charging through a resistor from 12 V. With R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 µF, the time constant RC is 1 second.](fig/ch18_rc.svg){width=84%}

The good news is that Kirchhoff's laws, Thévenin's theorem and nodal analysis all still apply. Finding the time constant of a complicated circuit, for example, comes down to finding the Thévenin resistance seen by the capacitor – a skill you already have.

### Alternating current and impedance

In AC circuits, voltages and currents vary sinusoidally. Engineers represent them with **phasors**, using complex numbers, and they replace resistance with a more general quantity called **impedance**, which describes how resistors, capacitors and inductors respond at a given frequency. With that one change, every method in this book – series and parallel combination, dividers, nodal and mesh analysis, superposition, Thévenin, Norton and maximum power transfer – carries over directly. Students who have mastered DC analysis often find AC analysis surprisingly familiar.

### Non-linear devices

Diodes, LEDs and transistors are not linear, so superposition and the simple forms of Thévenin's theorem do not apply to them directly. But linear analysis still does most of the work. A favourite technique is the **load line**: find the Thévenin equivalent of the linear circuit driving the device, draw its terminal characteristic (the straight line of Chapter 15) on the same graph as the device's curved characteristic, and read off where they cross.

![Figure 18.6 — The load line of a 5 V, 1 kΩ Thévenin source plotted with an LED's characteristic. The operating point is where they intersect.](fig/ch18_loadline.svg){width=84%}

In Figure 18.6, the straight line runs from 5 V on the voltage axis to 5 mA on the current axis – the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current of the source. The LED's curve crosses it at about 1.96 V and 3.0 mA. That is the same answer you would get from the simple "subtract the forward voltage" method of Chapter 3, but now you can see *why* it works and how to do better when it matters. For transistors, engineers then use linear **small-signal models** – built from resistors and the dependent sources of Chapter 12 – to analyse how small signals are amplified. Every technique from Parts Four and Five comes into play.

## 18.7 A Last Word

When you started this book, a circuit diagram may have looked like a puzzle in a foreign language. I hope it now looks like a story you can read: a source pushing charge, resistors taking their share of the energy, currents dividing at nodes and voltages adding round loops. You can simplify it, systematically solve it, replace parts of it with simpler equivalents, check your answers and use what you find to design or repair real things.

Keep practising. Work the exercises, and then invent your own: draw a circuit, predict its behaviour, build it on a breadboard, and measure. Nothing builds understanding faster than seeing a meter confirm a number you calculated with a pencil. And when, one day, someone hands you a torch that will not light, you will know exactly where to begin.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Trusting nominal values blindly.** Consider tolerance, temperature and lead resistance where they matter.
- **Measuring low resistances with two wires.** Use a four-wire connection.
- **Replacing parts before diagnosing.** Predict, measure, compare, then act.
- **Testing elements one at a time along a long chain.** Half-split instead.
- **Working on live circuits unnecessarily.** Isolate, lock off and prove dead.
- **Believing a simulator without question.** Estimate first; understand the result.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- Real components have **tolerances**; **worst-case analysis** gives safe limits and **Monte Carlo** analysis gives realistic spreads.
- Measure **voltages** where possible and calculate currents. For **low resistances**, use a **four-wire (Kelvin)** connection.
- **Fault-finding** means predicting healthy readings, measuring, and finding the single fault that explains every reading. **Fault tables** are built from circuit analysis.
- The **half-split** method finds a fault in a chain of 2^n^ elements in n tests.
- **Safety**: work dead, prove dead, beware stored energy, use rated equipment.
- **SPICE** netlists list each component with its two nodes and value; simulators solve them by nodal analysis.
- The methods of this book extend to **capacitors and inductors** (time constants), **AC** (impedance and phasors) and **non-linear devices** (load lines and small-signal models).

In 1845 a twenty-one-year-old student at the University of Königsberg submitted a short paper as part of a seminar exercise. Gustav Robert Kirchhoff had been thinking about how currents divide in networks of wires, and he set down two simple rules that allowed any such network to be analysed. His supervisor recognised their importance at once. Nearly two centuries later, those two rules remain the foundation of every circuit analysis method in existence, from the pencil-and-paper techniques in this book to the simulation software used to design microprocessors with billions of transistors.

Kirchhoff went on to an extraordinary career. With Robert Bunsen (of burner fame) he founded the science of spectroscopy and discovered two new chemical elements. But for electrical engineers, those two student rules are his great legacy. They are **Kirchhoff's current law** and **Kirchhoff's voltage law**, universally shortened to **KCL** and **KVL**.

What makes them so powerful is that they are not really new physics at all. Each is a careful statement of a principle you already believe: that charge cannot appear from nowhere or vanish into nothing, and that energy is conserved. Once you see them that way, they stop being rules to memorise and become common sense. In this chapter we will learn both laws, practise applying them with correct signs, and then use them together with Ohm's law to solve our first genuinely multi-loop circuit.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- state Kirchhoff's current law and explain why it is true;
- apply KCL at a node and across a closed boundary;
- state Kirchhoff's voltage law and explain why it is true;
- apply KVL round any closed loop with consistent signs;
- find the voltage between any two points by following a path;
- combine KCL, KVL and Ohm's law to analyse single-loop and single-node-pair circuits;
- use the branch-current method to solve a circuit with two sources.
:::

## 5.1 Kirchhoff's Current Law

::: keyeq
**Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)**

The total current flowing into a node equals the total current flowing out of it.

Σ I~in~ = Σ I~out~
:::

Why must this be so? Remember from Chapter 1 that current is the flow of charge, and charge cannot be created or destroyed. A node is just a junction of wires; it has no way of storing charge. So whatever charge flows into a node every second must flow out of it every second. If it did not, charge would pile up at the junction, which does not happen in any circuit we will meet. KCL is simply **conservation of charge**, written for a junction.

Think of a road junction at rush hour. If 300 cars a minute arrive from the north and 200 from the east, and nobody is parking at the junction, then 500 cars a minute must leave along the other roads. The junction does not create or swallow cars.

![Figure 5.1 — Currents at a node. Two currents flow in and two flow out. KCL says the total in must equal the total out.](fig/ch05_kcl_node.svg){width=50%}

::: example
**Worked Example 5.1 — Finding an unknown current at a node**

In Figure 5.1, currents I~1~ = 3 A and I~2~ = 5 A flow into the node, and I~3~ = 2 A flows out. Find I~4~, which flows out.

**Solution.** By KCL, total in = total out:

3 + 5 = 2 + I~4~

I~4~ = 8 − 2 = 6 A
:::

### The algebraic form

KCL can also be written in a form that is more convenient when you have many currents or are writing equations systematically. Choose a sign convention – say, currents **leaving** the node are positive and currents **entering** are negative. Then:

::: eq
Σ I = 0   (summed algebraically over all branches at the node)
:::

For Figure 5.1, this gives −3 − 5 + 2 + I~4~ = 0, which again gives I~4~ = 6 A. The two forms are equivalent; use whichever feels clearer. In nodal analysis (Chapter 10) we will use the "sum of currents leaving equals zero" form throughout, because it makes the equations very regular.

### When the direction is unknown

In Chapter 4 I said that you may choose any reference direction for an unknown current, and that a negative answer simply means the current flows the other way. KCL is where this freedom first pays off.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.2 — A negative answer**

Four wires meet at a node. Currents of 4 A and 1 A flow in along two of them. A current of 7 A flows out along the third. The current in the fourth wire, I~x~, is marked with an arrow pointing *out* of the node. Find I~x~.

**Solution.** KCL: in = out.

4 + 1 = 7 + I~x~,   so   I~x~ = 5 − 7 = −2 A

**Interpretation.** The negative sign means our guessed direction was wrong. The current in the fourth wire is 2 A flowing **into** the node. Check: in = 4 + 1 + 2 = 7 A, out = 7 A. ✓
:::

### KCL for a closed boundary

KCL is not limited to single junctions. Draw any closed boundary round part of a circuit – a whole sub-circuit, a group of nodes, even an entire printed circuit board. Provided nothing inside the boundary stores charge, **the total current crossing the boundary inwards equals the total current crossing it outwards**. The same reasoning applies: charge cannot accumulate inside.

This "generalised" KCL is surprisingly handy. If a circuit board is connected to the outside world by four wires and you know the currents in three of them, the fourth is fixed. It is also exactly the idea behind the **supernode** we shall meet in Chapter 12, where we draw a boundary round two nodes joined by a voltage source.

It also explains a familiar safety device. A **residual current device** (RCD) in a UK consumer unit compares the current in the live conductor with the current in the neutral. If the appliance and its wiring are healthy, KCL says these must be equal. If some current is leaking to earth – possibly through a person – the two differ, and the RCD trips in a fraction of a second. It is Kirchhoff's current law saving lives every day.

## 5.2 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

::: keyeq
**Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)**

Around any closed loop in a circuit, the sum of the voltage rises equals the sum of the voltage drops.

Equivalently, the algebraic sum of all voltages round a closed loop is zero: Σ V = 0.
:::

KVL is **conservation of energy**. Recall that voltage is energy per unit charge. Imagine following a single coulomb of charge all the way round a loop and back to where it started. Along the way it gains energy in some places (passing through a source from − to +) and loses energy in others (passing through resistors from + to −). When it arrives back at its starting point, it must have exactly the same energy it started with, otherwise we could make it go round again and again, gaining energy from nothing each time. So the total gains must equal the total losses.

My favourite way to picture KVL is a hill walk. Suppose you set off from your car, climb a hill, wander along a ridge, drop into a valley, and finally walk back to the car park. However complicated the route, the total height you climbed must equal the total height you descended, because you finished at the same height you started. Potential in a circuit works in exactly the same way. Figure 5.3 makes the analogy explicit.

### Applying KVL with correct signs

The only difficulty with KVL is getting the signs right. Here is a method that works every time.

1. **Mark the polarity** (+ and −) of every voltage in the loop. For sources, the polarity is given. For resistors, use the passive sign convention: the end where your chosen current enters is +.
2. **Choose a direction** to walk round the loop – clockwise is the usual habit – and a starting point.
3. **Walk round the loop.** For each element, note which terminal you meet first:
   - if you enter at the **+** terminal and leave at −, it is a **drop**: write **+V**;
   - if you enter at the **−** terminal and leave at +, it is a **rise**: write **−V**.
4. **Set the sum equal to zero** when you arrive back at the start.

You could equally use the opposite rule (rises positive, drops negative). The answer will be identical. What matters is that you are consistent within a single equation.

![Figure 5.2 — A single loop with a source and three resistors. The polarities of the resistor voltages follow the clockwise current. Walking clockwise, we meet a rise through the source and three drops.](fig/ch05_kvl_loop.svg){width=62%}

::: example
**Worked Example 5.3 — Finding an unknown voltage with KVL**

In Figure 5.2, V~1~ = 8 V and V~3~ = 10 V. Find V~2~.

**Step 1: Polarities.** These are marked on the figure.

**Step 2: Walk clockwise from the bottom of the source.**

- Through the source from − to +: a rise, **−24**.
- Through R~1~, entering at +: a drop, **+8**.
- Through R~2~, entering at +: a drop, **+V~2~**.
- Through R~3~, entering at +: a drop, **+10**.

**Step 3: Sum to zero.**

−24 + 8 + V~2~ + 10 = 0,   so   V~2~ = 6 V

**In words.** The source raises the potential by 24 V; the three resistors must between them drop exactly 24 V. Since 8 + 10 = 18, the remaining 6 V must appear across R~2~.
:::

Figure 5.3 shows the potential at each point round this loop, measured from the bottom of the source. It is the hill walk drawn as a graph: one climb of 24 V and three descents that add up to 24 V, returning to exactly where we began.

![Figure 5.3 — The potential round the loop of Figure 5.2, measured from the − terminal of the source. The source lifts the charge by 24 V; the three resistors bring it back down in steps of 8 V, 6 V and 10 V.](fig/ch05_staircase.svg){width=94%}

### KVL is true for every loop

KVL applies to *every* closed loop in a circuit, not only the meshes. In a circuit with several meshes you can write KVL round each mesh, or round a larger loop that encloses several meshes. The equations from larger loops are not new information, though – they can always be obtained by adding the mesh equations together – so for solving circuits we normally use only the meshes.

### Finding the voltage between two points

Here is one of the most practical consequences of KVL. **The voltage between two points is the same whichever path you follow between them.** Back on our hill walk, the height difference between the car park and the summit is the same whether you take the steep path or the gentle one.

So to find the voltage between any two points A and B, you can walk *any* convenient path from B to A, adding up the rises and subtracting the drops. This "path method" is a fast way of finding voltages once some currents are known, and it is a great way of checking an answer: try a second path and see whether you get the same result.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.4 — Voltage by two different paths**

In Figure 5.2, find the voltage between the top-left corner (the top of the source, call it P) and the bottom-right corner (between R~2~ and R~3~, call it Q), using two different paths.

**Path 1: via the bottom of the source.** Take the bottom-left corner (the − terminal of the source) as our zero. Going up through the source raises the potential by 24 V, so P is at +24 V. Now go from the zero point rightwards along the bottom to Q. On the way we pass through R~3~ from its − end to its + end (the current flows leftwards through it, entering at the right), so the potential rises by 10 V: Q is at +10 V. Therefore V~PQ~ = 24 − 10 = 14 V.

**Path 2: from P through R~1~ and R~2~ to Q.** Starting at P and moving clockwise, R~1~ drops 8 V and R~2~ drops 6 V. So Q is 14 V below P: V~PQ~ = 8 + 6 = 14 V. ✓

**Lesson.** Both paths agree, as KVL guarantees. Notice that in Path 1 we chose a zero point and worked out the potential of each node relative to it. That is usually clearer than walking "backwards" through elements, and it is precisely the idea behind nodal analysis.
:::

Walking against the current is where most sign slips happen. Every engineer makes them from time to time; the professionals are the ones who check by a second route.

## 5.3 Single-Loop Circuits

Now let us combine KVL and Ohm's law to *solve* a circuit, rather than merely checking given voltages. The simplest case is a single loop, where KCL tells us that the same current flows through every element. We need only one unknown, the loop current I.

![Figure 5.4 — A single loop containing two sources that oppose each other. E~1~ tries to push current clockwise; E~2~ tries to push it anticlockwise.](fig/ch05_two_sources.svg){width=62%}

::: example
**Worked Example 5.5 — Two opposing sources**

In Figure 5.4, E~1~ = 12 V, E~2~ = 4 V, R~1~ = 2 Ω and R~2~ = 6 Ω. Find the current, the voltage across each resistor and the power in each element.

**Step 1: Choose a direction.** Let the loop current I flow clockwise. Then current enters R~1~ at its left end and R~2~ at its right end, so those are their + terminals. The resistor voltages are 2I and 6I.

**Step 2: KVL, walking clockwise from the bottom of E~1~.**

- Up through E~1~ from − to +: rise, −12.
- Through R~1~ from + to −: drop, +2I.
- Down through E~2~: E~2~ has its + terminal at the top, so walking downwards we enter at + — a drop, +4.
- Through R~2~ from + to −: drop, +6I.

−12 + 2I + 4 + 6I = 0

**Step 3: Solve.** 8I = 8, so I = 1 A. It is positive, so the current really does flow clockwise. That makes sense: the larger source wins.

**Step 4: Resistor voltages.** V~R1~ = 2 × 1 = 2 V; V~R2~ = 6 × 1 = 6 V.

**Step 5: Powers.**

- E~1~: current leaves its + terminal, so it **delivers** 12 × 1 = 12 W.
- E~2~: current enters its + terminal, so it **absorbs** 4 × 1 = 4 W. It is being charged.
- R~1~ absorbs 1² × 2 = 2 W; R~2~ absorbs 1² × 6 = 6 W.

**Step 6: Power balance.** Delivered = 12 W. Absorbed = 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 W. ✓
:::

This example contains a general result that is worth stating: in a single loop, **I = (net EMF) / (total resistance)**, where the net EMF is the sum of the sources pushing one way minus those pushing the other. It is exactly what happens when you charge a battery from a higher-voltage supply through a resistor.

## 5.4 Single-Node-Pair Circuits

The mirror image of a single loop is a circuit in which every element is connected between the same two nodes. Everything is in parallel, so KVL tells us that **every element has the same voltage**, V. This time KCL provides the equation.

![Figure 5.5 — A single-node-pair circuit. Every element is connected between the top and bottom nodes, so each has the same voltage V across it.](fig/ch05_node_pair.svg){width=80%}

::: example
**Worked Example 5.6 — Current sources in parallel with resistors**

In Figure 5.5, a 6 A source pushes current up into the top node and a 2 A source draws current down out of it. R~1~ = 4 Ω and R~2~ = 12 Ω. Find V, the current in each resistor and the power in each element.

**Step 1: Define V** as the voltage of the top node relative to the bottom one. Then the current flowing down through R~1~ is V/4, and through R~2~ is V/12.

**Step 2: KCL at the top node.** Currents in = currents out:

6 = 2 + {{frac|V|4}} + {{frac|V|12}}

**Step 3: Solve.** Multiply every term by 12 to clear the fractions: 72 = 24 + 3V + V, so 4V = 48 and **V = 12 V**.

**Step 4: Resistor currents.** I~R1~ = 12 / 4 = 3 A; I~R2~ = 12 / 12 = 1 A. Check KCL: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 ✓.

**Step 5: Powers.**

- The 6 A source pushes current out of its top terminal, which is at +12 V: it **delivers** 12 × 6 = 72 W.
- The 2 A source draws current *into* its top terminal (+12 V): it **absorbs** 12 × 2 = 24 W.
- R~1~ absorbs 12 × 3 = 36 W; R~2~ absorbs 12 × 1 = 12 W.

**Step 6: Balance.** Delivered 72 W; absorbed 24 + 36 + 12 = 72 W. ✓
:::

Notice the beautiful symmetry between this example and the last one. In the single loop, one current is shared and KVL gives the equation. In the single node pair, one voltage is shared and KCL gives the equation. This *duality* between series and parallel, voltage and current, loops and nodes runs right through circuit theory. It will reappear in the relationship between mesh and nodal analysis, and between Thévenin and Norton.

## 5.5 The Branch-Current Method

Single loops and single node pairs are useful, but most circuits have several loops *and* several nodes. To solve them we need to use KCL and KVL together. The most direct way of doing this is called the **branch-current method**:

1. Assign an unknown current, with a reference direction, to every branch.
2. Write KCL at all but one of the essential nodes. (The last node's equation is never new: it can always be obtained from the others.)
3. Write KVL round each mesh, expressing resistor voltages in terms of branch currents using Ohm's law.
4. Solve the resulting simultaneous equations.
5. Check with a power balance or a second KVL path.

The method always works, but it tends to produce more equations than necessary. In Part Four you will learn mesh and nodal analysis, which are cleverer ways of organising the same ideas to reduce the number of unknowns. Seeing the branch-current method first will help you appreciate why those methods are so efficient – and it is all you need for circuits of modest size.

![Figure 5.6 — A two-source circuit for branch-current analysis. Reference directions have been chosen for the three branch currents; they need not be correct.](fig/ch05_branch.svg){width=86%}

::: example
**Worked Example 5.7 — Two batteries sharing a load**

In Figure 5.6, E~1~ = 10 V, E~2~ = 5 V, R~1~ = 2 Ω, R~2~ = 1 Ω and R~3~ = 4 Ω. Find all three branch currents and check the power balance.

**Step 1: Branch currents.** I~1~ flows right through R~1~ into node A; I~2~ flows left through R~2~ into node A; I~3~ flows down through R~3~ from A to B. These are guesses; the maths will correct any that are wrong.

**Step 2: KCL.** There are two essential nodes, A and B, so we need one KCL equation. At A:

I~1~ + I~2~ = I~3~    (1)

**Step 3: KVL in the left mesh** (E~1~, R~1~, R~3~), walking clockwise from B. Up through E~1~: rise, −10. Through R~1~ in the direction of I~1~: drop, +2I~1~. Down through R~3~ in the direction of I~3~: drop, +4I~3~.

−10 + 2I~1~ + 4I~3~ = 0    (2)

**Step 4: KVL in the right mesh** (R~3~, R~2~, E~2~), walking anticlockwise from B so that we go up through E~2~ first. Up through E~2~ from − to +: rise, −5. Leftwards through R~2~ in the direction of I~2~: drop, +1I~2~. Down through R~3~ in the direction of I~3~: drop, +4I~3~.

−5 + I~2~ + 4I~3~ = 0    (3)

**Step 5: Solve.** Substitute (1) into (2) and (3) to eliminate I~3~:

(2): 2I~1~ + 4(I~1~ + I~2~) = 10, which gives 6I~1~ + 4I~2~ = 10

(3): I~2~ + 4(I~1~ + I~2~) = 5, which gives 4I~1~ + 5I~2~ = 5

Multiply the first by 5 and the second by 4, then subtract:

30I~1~ + 20I~2~ = 50
16I~1~ + 20I~2~ = 20

14I~1~ = 30,   so   I~1~ = 15/7 = 2.143 A

Substituting back: 4(2.143) + 5I~2~ = 5, so 5I~2~ = 5 − 8.571 = −3.571 and **I~2~ = −0.714 A** (exactly −5/7 A).

Then I~3~ = I~1~ + I~2~ = 2.143 − 0.714 = **1.429 A** (exactly 10/7 A).

**Step 6: Interpret.** I~2~ is negative, so current actually flows *rightwards* through R~2~ and down into the + terminal of E~2~. The stronger 10 V battery is supplying the 4 Ω load and also charging the 5 V battery.

**Step 7: Check by power balance.**

- E~1~ delivers 10 × 2.143 = 21.43 W.
- E~2~: current of 0.714 A enters its + terminal, so it absorbs 5 × 0.714 = 3.57 W.
- R~1~: 2.143² × 2 = 9.18 W; R~2~: 0.714² × 1 = 0.51 W; R~3~: 1.429² × 4 = 8.16 W.

Absorbed total = 3.57 + 9.18 + 0.51 + 8.16 = 21.42 W, which matches the 21.43 W delivered to within rounding. ✓

**Step 8: A second check.** The voltage at A relative to B is V~AB~ = 4 × I~3~ = 5.71 V. By the path through E~1~: 10 − 2 × 2.143 = 5.71 V ✓. By the path through E~2~: 5 + 1 × 0.714 = 5.71 V ✓ (the current through R~2~ really flows from A towards E~2~, so A is higher than the top of E~2~).
:::

That was a fair amount of work for a modest circuit: three unknowns, three equations and some careful algebra. If you found the simultaneous equations heavy going, do not worry. Appendix B gives a step-by-step guide to solving them, including the use of determinants, and Part Four will show you methods that need fewer unknowns. For now, the important thing is that you have seen KCL, KVL and Ohm's law working together to solve a real problem, and that you have checked the answer in two independent ways.

::: tip
**In Practice: Parallel Batteries**

Worked Example 5.7 shows why you should never connect batteries of different voltages, or different states of charge, directly in parallel. The higher-voltage battery drives a current into the lower one, wasting energy and, with low-resistance batteries such as lithium cells, potentially causing dangerous overheating. Battery packs are carefully designed with matched cells and protection circuits for exactly this reason.
:::

## 5.6 When Do Kirchhoff's Laws Apply?

KCL and KVL are extremely general. They apply to circuits containing any kind of component – resistors, diodes, transistors, capacitors, inductors – and to both DC and AC. They hold for linear and non-linear circuits alike, because they rest only on conservation of charge and energy.

There is one assumption hidden underneath, which you should know about even though it will not affect anything in this book. Kirchhoff's laws assume a **lumped** circuit: one whose physical size is small compared with the wavelength of the signals in it, so that changes propagate across the whole circuit effectively instantaneously. For mains-frequency power within a building, or DC in any circuit, that is overwhelmingly true. At radio and microwave frequencies, or along transmission lines hundreds of kilometres long, it breaks down, and engineers use more advanced theory. That is for a later book.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Mixing sign conventions within one equation.** Decide whether drops or rises are positive and stick to it for the whole loop.
- **Getting the polarity of a resistor voltage wrong.** The + end of a resistor is where your *chosen* current enters it.
- **Writing too many KCL equations.** With n essential nodes, only n − 1 KCL equations are independent.
- **Using KVL round loops that are not closed.** You must return to your starting point.
- **Panicking about negative answers.** They simply mean the true direction is opposite to the one you chose.
- **Skipping the checks.** A power balance or a second KVL path takes a minute and catches most errors.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **KCL**: total current into a node equals total current out. It expresses conservation of charge. It also applies to any closed boundary.
- **KVL**: around any closed loop, rises equal drops; the algebraic sum of voltages is zero. It expresses conservation of energy.
- When applying KVL, mark polarities, choose a direction, write +V for drops and −V for rises (or the reverse, consistently), and set the sum to zero.
- The **voltage between two points** is the same along any path between them.
- In a **single loop**, the same current flows everywhere: I = net EMF / total resistance.
- In a **single node pair**, every element has the same voltage; KCL gives the equation.
- The **branch-current method** assigns a current to every branch and uses KCL at (n − 1) essential nodes and KVL round each mesh.
- Always **check** with a power balance or a second path.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

KCL: Σ I~in~ = Σ I~out~     KVL: Σ V = 0 round any closed loop
:::

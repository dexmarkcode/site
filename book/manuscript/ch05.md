# Battery Pack Design and Thermal Management

::: chapter-intro
A pile of good cells does not make a good battery. The pack must connect hundreds or thousands of cells safely, carry hundreds of amps with little loss, keep every cell at a comfortable and uniform temperature, survive a crash, keep water out for fifteen years, and stop one failing cell from taking the rest with it. This chapter covers how packs are configured, built, cooled, heated and protected, and how they are taken apart again at the end of their lives.
:::

I once heard a battery engineer describe pack design as "the art of making a few thousand things that don't like each other behave as one". It is a good description. Cells come out of the factory with slightly different capacities and resistances. Once inside a pack, they sit at slightly different temperatures, so they age at slightly different rates, so the differences grow. The pack designer's job is to keep those differences small and to make sure that when one cell does misbehave, the consequences are contained.

At the same time, the pack is a large structural component of the car, often the largest single part by mass. It has to be stiff, strong and watertight, it has to be built at high speed in a factory, and ideally it has to come apart again for repair and recycling. These requirements pull against each other constantly. This chapter is about how engineers resolve them.

## Requirements for a Traction Pack

Before looking at solutions, it helps to list what the pack must do. A typical requirements document for a passenger-car pack will cover at least the following:

- **Energy.** Usable energy at beginning and end of life, which together fix the installed energy and the buffer.
- **Voltage window.** Minimum and maximum pack voltage, set by the inverter, the charger and the charging standards it must work with.
- **Power.** Peak discharge and regenerative charge power over defined durations at defined temperatures and states of charge; continuous power; peak and sustained DC charging power.
- **Mass and volume.** Usually expressed as targets for pack-level gravimetric and volumetric energy density, and a package envelope that must fit under the floor between the wheels.
- **Life.** Capacity and resistance after a given number of years and kilometres, under defined climate profiles.
- **Safety.** Behaviour under crash, crush, penetration, fire exposure, immersion, overcharge, external short circuit, and single-cell thermal runaway.
- **Environmental.** Sealing, typically to IP67 or IP6K9K, vibration and mechanical shock, salt spray, thermal cycling and stone chips.
- **Cost.** Usually expressed per kilowatt-hour at pack level.
- **Serviceability and end of life.** What can be replaced, by whom, and how the pack will be recycled.

Notice that these requirements are partly in tension. The cheapest pack is not the safest, the lightest is not the easiest to repair, and the one that charges fastest will need the most cooling.

## Series and Parallel: Configuring the Cells

Chapter 3 showed how series connections set the voltage and parallel connections the capacity. Within those simple rules sit some subtle effects that every pack designer must understand.

### Cells in parallel

Cells connected directly in parallel always share the same terminal voltage. If one cell has a lower internal resistance than its neighbour, it carries more of the current. If one has a lower capacity, its state of charge moves faster, its OCV changes faster, and current redistributes between them to keep the voltages equal. This is self-balancing in a useful sense: parallel groups behave like one larger cell, and the BMS only needs to measure the group voltage.

But the current sharing is uneven, and uneven current means uneven heating and uneven ageing. The cells with lower resistance work harder, warm more and age faster, which raises their resistance and pushes the load onto the others. Over life, this tends to even out, but at the start it can matter. Busbar resistance also affects sharing: cells further from the pack terminals see a longer current path and carry less current, which is why careful designers lay out busbars to give every cell a similar path resistance.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.1 — Current sharing in a parallel pair**

Two cells are connected in parallel and together supply 300 A. Cell A has an effective resistance of 1.0 mΩ and cell B, perhaps through a slightly longer busbar path, has 1.2 mΩ. Their OCVs are equal. How is the current shared, and how different is their heating?

*Solution.* With the same voltage drop across both branches, current divides inversely with resistance:

*I*~A~ = 300 × 1.2 / (1.0 + 1.2) = 163.6 A, *I*~B~ = 300 × 1.0 / 2.2 = 136.4 A.

Heat: *P*~A~ = 163.6^2^ × 0.001 = 26.8 W; *P*~B~ = 136.4^2^ × 0.0012 = 22.3 W.

A 20 % difference in resistance causes a 20 % difference in heat. Over thousands of hours of use, cell A will run warmer and age faster. This is the reason that pack designers worry about microohms of difference in busbar paths.
:::

### Cells in series

Cells in series carry the same current, so they all move through the same number of amp-hours. If one cell has a lower capacity than the rest, it reaches empty first on discharge and full first on charge. Because the BMS must stop discharge when *any* cell reaches its minimum voltage, and stop charging when *any* cell reaches its maximum, the pack's usable capacity is limited by its weakest cell.

Worse, if cells start at different states of charge, which happens through differences in self-discharge, the pack's usable window shrinks further: one cell may hit the top while another still has room, and a different cell hits the bottom while the first still has charge. This is the job of *cell balancing*, covered in Chapter 6: to keep the series cells aligned in state of charge so that the whole pack can use the full capacity of its weakest member.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.2 — The weakest link**

A 96s1p pack uses 150 Ah cells. Through manufacturing variation and uneven ageing, one cell has a capacity of 141 Ah, while the others are all close to 150 Ah. The cells are perfectly balanced at the top of charge. What is the usable capacity of the pack, and how much energy is lost compared with a perfect pack, if the nominal pack voltage is 355 V?

*Solution.* Discharging from full, the weak cell reaches its minimum voltage after 141 Ah. The BMS stops the discharge there, although the other 95 cells still hold 9 Ah each. Usable pack capacity is 141 Ah.

Energy lost: 9 Ah × 355 V ≈ 3.2 kWh, out of 53.3 kWh, or 6 %.

One cell in ninety-six has cost 6 % of the range. If cells cannot be matched more closely, then the BMS, the cooling system and the cell supplier's quality all have to work to stop the spread from growing.
:::

### Choosing the configuration

In practice the series count is fixed by the voltage window, and the parallel count by energy, so the configuration follows from the cell choice. A 400 V-class pack with large prismatic cells might be 96s1p or 108s1p. An 800 V-class pack might be 192s1p or 200s2p. A pack built from small cylindrical cells might be 96s46p, containing over 4 400 cells. Large parallel counts are forgiving, because one weak cell is diluted among many, but they need protection: if one cell in a large parallel group short-circuits internally, all its neighbours discharge into it. Cylindrical cell packs therefore often use a fusible link, a thin wire bond or a narrow neck in the busbar, for every cell.

## Pack Architectures

Figure 5.1 shows the evolution of pack architectures over the past decade.

![**Figure 5.1** Pack architectures. (a) Module-based: cells are grouped into self-contained modules, which are mounted in the pack. (b) Cell-to-pack: modules are eliminated and cells are mounted directly in the pack structure. (c) Cell-to-body: the pack's top cover becomes the vehicle floor, and the cells contribute to the body's stiffness.](figures/fig5_1.png)

### Module-based packs

The traditional pack groups cells into *modules*, typically of 12 to 24 large cells or several hundred small ones. Each module has its own frame, end plates, busbars, sensing harness and sometimes its own monitoring electronics. Modules are then mounted in the pack enclosure, connected together and laid on cooling plates.

Modules have real advantages. They are a convenient unit for manufacturing, testing, handling and repair. A faulty module can be replaced without scrapping a whole pack. The same module can be used in several pack sizes for different vehicles. And the module frame provides a natural barrier against thermal propagation.

The disadvantage is overhead. All those frames, end plates and connections add mass, volume and cost that store no energy. In early module-based packs, the cells might account for only 50 to 60 % of the pack's mass and 35 to 45 % of its volume.

### Cell-to-pack

The *cell-to-pack* (CTP) approach eliminates the module and installs cells directly into the pack structure. The best-known example is the long, thin "blade" LFP cell, which spans the width of the pack and acts as a beam, but large prismatic cells and large cylindrical cells are also used this way. Removing modules can raise volumetric integration efficiency from around 40 % to 60 % or more, and gravimetric efficiency to 75 % or above. That recovered space is what made LFP competitive for mainstream cars.

The costs are reduced repairability, since individual cells are usually bonded in place, and the need for the pack structure and cells to be designed together.

### Cell-to-body and structural packs

The next step is to make the pack part of the vehicle's body structure. In *cell-to-body* or *cell-to-chassis* designs, the top of the pack becomes the cabin floor, and the cells, bonded with structural adhesive, contribute to the torsional stiffness of the car. This saves further mass by removing duplicated structure, and it can lower the floor or raise the battery height within the same vehicle height.

The trade-off is that the pack can no longer be easily removed or repaired, and the body and battery development programmes become tightly coupled. Some manufacturers have embraced this; others prefer to keep the pack as a separate, replaceable unit. There is no settled answer yet.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.3 — Integration efficiency**

A pack contains 75 kWh of cells with a cell-level energy density of 250 Wh/kg. The complete pack weighs 450 kg. What is the pack-level energy density, and what fraction of the pack's mass is cells? What would the pack weigh with the same cells if integration efficiency rose to 80 %?

*Solution.* Cell mass = 75 000 / 250 = 300 kg.

Pack energy density = 75 000 / 450 = 167 Wh/kg. Cell mass fraction = 300 / 450 = 67 %.

At 80 % integration, pack mass = 300 / 0.8 = 375 kg, a saving of 75 kg, and pack-level energy density rises to 200 Wh/kg. That 75 kg is about 4 % of the vehicle's mass, worth perhaps 1 to 1.5 % in consumption, all without changing the cells.
:::

## Electrical Design of the Pack

### The high-voltage path

Figure 5.2 shows the main elements of a pack's electrical architecture. The cells are connected in series by busbars. At one or more points, a *manual service disconnect* splits the string. The positive and negative ends of the string pass through the *battery disconnect unit* (BDU), sometimes called the battery junction box, which contains the main contactors, the pre-charge contactor and resistor, the main fuse, the current sensor and, often, a pyrotechnic disconnect. From there, connectors lead out to the vehicle.

![**Figure 5.2** Electrical architecture of a traction pack. The cell string, split by a service disconnect, is connected to the vehicle through the battery disconnect unit, which contains contactors, pre-charge, fuse and current sensing. Cell monitoring units measure every cell and report to the battery management controller.](figures/fig5_2.png)

**Contactors** are electromechanical switches designed to make and break high DC currents. Breaking DC is harder than breaking AC, because there is no natural zero crossing of current to extinguish the arc. Contactors therefore use sealed chambers filled with hydrogen or another gas, and permanent magnets to blow the arc away from the contacts. They are rated for a certain number of breaks under load; a contactor that has opened a few times at full short-circuit current may be at the end of its life.

**Fuses** for DC traction circuits must interrupt very high fault currents, potentially tens of kiloamps, at voltages up to 1 000 V. Their challenge is coordination: the fuse must not blow during a legitimate peak power pulse, but it must clear a short circuit before cables and busbars overheat. Because the pack's short-circuit current falls as the cells age, cool or discharge, a fuse sized for a warm, new, full pack may be too slow for a cold, old, empty one. Many designs therefore add a **pyrotechnic fuse** (pyrofuse), which uses a small explosive charge to cut a busbar within a few milliseconds when triggered by the BMS or the crash sensor. It is independent of the fault current and reliable at all states.

### Joining cells

Every cell connection is a potential failure point: a high-current path that must have low and stable resistance for fifteen years through vibration, thermal cycling and corrosion. The main joining methods are:

- **Laser welding**, now dominant for prismatic and cylindrical cells. It is fast, precise and automatable, but welding copper to aluminium is metallurgically tricky because the two form brittle intermetallic compounds.
- **Ultrasonic welding**, common for pouch-cell tabs, which bonds metals by high-frequency vibration under pressure without melting them.
- **Wire bonding**, used for cylindrical cells in some packs, in which thin aluminium wires connect each cell to a busbar and also act as a fusible link.
- **Bolted joints**, used for module-to-module connections and anywhere serviceability is needed. They are reliable if the torque, surface preparation and creep of the joint are controlled, and unreliable if not.

### Sensing and the low-voltage harness

Alongside the high-current path runs a network of small wires or a flexible printed circuit that measures every series cell voltage and a selection of temperatures. These connect to *cell monitoring units* (CMUs), each handling 12 to 18 cells, which communicate with the main *battery management controller*, either by a daisy-chained isolated serial link or, increasingly, wirelessly. Wireless BMS designs remove a significant amount of harness and connectors, which are themselves common sources of faults.

## Mechanical Design

The pack enclosure is a box that must do a surprising number of jobs at once.

It must protect the cells in a crash. Side impacts, especially against poles, are the most demanding case for floor-mounted packs, and the design typically uses a stiff, energy-absorbing perimeter frame of extruded aluminium or high-strength steel, with crossmembers to stop the pack folding. Cells are kept clear of the deformation zone.

It must survive *underbody impacts*, from kerbs, debris, potholes and speed humps. A floor plate of steel or aluminium, sometimes with a separate shield, protects the cells and cooling plates beneath them.

It must be sealed. Packs are typically designed to IP67, meaning dust-tight and able to withstand temporary immersion, and the seal has to last for the life of the vehicle through thermal cycling and vibration. Because the air inside heats and cools, the pack needs a *pressure equalisation vent*: a membrane that lets air through but keeps water out. A separate *burst vent* opens in the event of thermal runaway to release gases in a controlled direction, away from the cabin.

It must accommodate *cell swelling*. Cells grow slightly in thickness as they charge and as they age, by several per cent over life for some chemistries. Pouch and prismatic cells are usually held under a controlled compression, often with foam pads between them, so that swelling is resisted without crushing the cells.

And it must be light. Table 5.1 shows an indicative mass breakdown for a module-less pack.

## Thermal Management

Chapter 4 showed that cells are happiest at 15 to 35 °C, that they age rapidly when hot, that they lose power and risk lithium plating when cold, and that they generate several kilowatts of heat during fast charging. The thermal management system must deal with all of this, with one further requirement that is often underestimated: it must keep the cells at nearly the *same* temperature. A temperature spread of 5 °C across the pack is a common target, because cells that run hotter age faster and drift away from their neighbours.

### Cooling methods

**Air cooling** blows cabin air or outside air through gaps between cells or modules. It is cheap and simple and was used in several early EVs, but air has low heat capacity and the cells furthest downstream run hotter. Air-cooled packs have generally been associated with faster degradation in hot climates and limited fast-charging capability. It survives in some small and low-cost vehicles.

**Liquid cooling with cold plates** is now standard. A mixture of water and glycol flows through channels in aluminium plates that sit beneath or beside the cells, separated from them by a thermal interface material (a gap filler or pad) that is electrically insulating but conducts heat. The coolant carries the heat to a heat exchanger: a radiator at the front of the car for moderate loads, or a *chiller*, an evaporator of the air-conditioning circuit, when the pack must be cooled below ambient temperature, as during fast charging on a hot day.

**Direct refrigerant cooling** puts the air-conditioning refrigerant itself through the cold plates, removing the intermediate coolant loop. It is compact and powerful but harder to control uniformly, because the refrigerant changes phase as it travels through the plate.

**Immersion cooling** submerges the cells in a dielectric fluid that is pumped through the pack. It gives excellent heat transfer and uniformity and can help suppress thermal propagation, but adds mass and cost. It is used in some high-performance applications and is attracting wider interest.

### The thermal path

Heat generated inside a cell must travel through the cell's own materials, through its case, across the thermal interface material, through the cold plate wall and into the coolant. Each step has a thermal resistance, and the temperature drop across each is the heat flow multiplied by that resistance, exactly as voltage drop equals current times resistance in an electrical circuit. Figure 5.3 shows the analogy.

![**Figure 5.3** (a) Cross-section of a cell on a liquid-cooled plate. (b) The equivalent thermal resistance network. The temperature rise from coolant to cell core is the heat flow multiplied by the sum of the resistances.](figures/fig5_3.png)

$$ T_{cell} = T_{coolant} + \dot{Q}\,(R_{conv} + R_{plate} + R_{TIM} + R_{case} + R_{int}) $$ {5.1}

The weakest link is often the thermal interface material and the cell's internal resistance to heat flow across its layers. Large prismatic cells cooled only at the base can develop significant temperature gradients from bottom to top, and some designs therefore cool the large side faces instead.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.4 — Sizing the coolant flow**

During a fast charge, the pack in Worked Example 4.2 generates about 12.7 kW of heat. The coolant is a 50/50 mix of water and ethylene glycol, with specific heat capacity 3.4 kJ/(kg·K) and density 1 070 kg/m^3^. If the coolant temperature may rise by no more than 5 K across the pack, what flow rate is needed?

*Solution.* The heat carried by a fluid stream is *Q̇* = *ṁ c*~p~ Δ*T*, so

*ṁ* = 12 700 / (3 400 × 5) = 0.747 kg/s.

Volume flow = 0.747 / 1 070 = 0.000698 m^3^/s = 0.70 L/s, or about 42 litres per minute.

That is a substantial flow, requiring a pump of perhaps 100 to 200 W, with the pressure drop through the plates as a major design constraint. Allowing a larger coolant temperature rise halves the flow but increases the temperature spread across the pack. Most designs land somewhere between 3 and 8 K.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 5.5 — How hot does the cell get?**

Each cell in Example 5.4 generates 66 W. The total thermal resistance from cell core to coolant is 0.25 K/W. Coolant enters the pack at 25 °C and leaves at 30 °C. What is the hottest cell core temperature? What coolant inlet temperature is needed to keep it below 45 °C?

*Solution.* Temperature rise from coolant to core: 66 × 0.25 = 16.5 K. The hottest cell sits near the outlet, where coolant is at 30 °C, so its core reaches about 30 + 16.5 = 46.5 °C.

To keep the hottest core below 45 °C, the outlet coolant must be below 28.5 °C, so the inlet must be below 23.5 °C. On a 35 °C day, a radiator cannot achieve that, so the chiller must run, drawing power from the battery or the charger. This interaction between the battery, the air-conditioning system and the charging rate is why the fast-charging performance of an EV depends so heavily on ambient temperature, and why manufacturers spend so much effort on the thermal path.
:::

### Heating

In cold climates, the pack must be heated: to allow fast charging without lithium plating, to restore power, and to accept regenerative braking. Heat can come from a high-voltage resistive (PTC or thick-film) heater in the coolant loop, from the waste heat of the motor and inverter, and from a heat pump. Some drive units can deliberately run the motor inefficiently while stationary, injecting current that produces no torque but plenty of heat, a technique sometimes called *motor heat mode*. A few designs use self-heating cells with internal nickel foils, or apply high-frequency alternating current to warm the cells from within.

::: example
**Worked Example 5.6 — Warming a cold pack**

The 417 kg pack in Table 5.1 is at −10 °C and must be warmed to 15 °C before fast charging. Take the cells (300 kg) to have a specific heat of 1 000 J/(kg·K) and the remaining 117 kg an average of 900 J/(kg·K). How much energy is needed, and how long will a 7 kW heater take, ignoring losses?

*Solution.* Heat capacity = 300 × 1 000 + 117 × 900 = 405 300 J/K.

Energy for a 25 K rise = 405 300 × 25 = 10.1 MJ = 2.8 kWh.

Time with 7 kW = 2.8 / 7 = 0.40 h, or about 24 minutes. In practice losses to the cold air and the vehicle structure lengthen this. Triggering the warm-up while the car is still being driven to the charger, as navigation-linked preconditioning does, hides most of this time from the driver.
:::

## Containing Thermal Runaway

Chapter 4 described how a single cell can enter thermal runaway. The pack designer's question is: *what happens next?* The aim is to make sure that the failure of one cell cannot cascade into the failure of the whole pack, or at minimum that it does so slowly enough for occupants to get out. Several regulations, including China's GB 38031 standard, require that after a single-cell runaway there is no fire or explosion for at least five minutes after a warning to the occupants, and the trend everywhere is towards requiring no propagation at all.

Figure 5.4 shows how propagation proceeds and the main mitigation measures.

![**Figure 5.4** Thermal propagation in a pack. Without barriers, heat conducted from a failed cell drives its neighbours into runaway in sequence. Barrier materials, cooling and controlled venting delay or stop the cascade.](figures/fig5_4.png)

The main defences are:

- **Thermal barriers** between cells or groups of cells: mica sheets, aerogel pads, ceramic papers or intumescent materials that slow heat flow sideways while allowing normal cooling downward.
- **Spacing and heat sinking.** Cooling plates and busbars can carry heat away from the failed cell, if they keep working.
- **Venting management.** The gases vented from a failing cell are hot, flammable and full of particles. Channels or a dedicated *venting chamber* direct them away from neighbouring cells and out of the pack through the burst vent, away from the cabin.
- **Chemistry choice.** LFP cells release much less energy and gas in runaway, and LFP packs often pass propagation tests with simple barriers.
- **Detection.** Gas sensors, pressure sensors and rapid temperature or voltage changes allow the BMS to detect the first failing cell and warn occupants, often within a second of venting.

Proving that a pack does not propagate is difficult, because you must trigger runaway in one cell deliberately, in a repeatable way, without changing the system. Common methods include a small heater placed against a cell, nail penetration, or an internal short-circuit device built into a test cell during manufacture. Each method has limitations, and test engineers argue about which most closely represents a real field failure.

::: casefile
**Case File 5 — The corner that ran hot**

*The complaint.* During validation testing of a new pack, repeated fast-charge cycles show the cells in one rear corner reaching 47 °C while the rest of the pack stays below 40 °C. The BMS reduces the charging current when any cell exceeds 45 °C, so the car's 10–80 % charging time is 4 minutes longer than its target of 22 minutes.

*State it in numbers.* Temperature spread 7 K against a target of 5 K; the hot region covers about 24 cells, all on the last branch of the cold-plate circuit.

*Draw the boundary.* Cell heat generation (are those cells different?), the thermal interface (was gap filler applied correctly?) and coolant flow distribution (does that branch get its share?).

*Simplest model.* Cell resistance measurements show the hot cells are within the normal spread, so heat generation is not the cause. If the thermal path were the problem, the temperature difference between the hot cells' base and the coolant would be larger than elsewhere. If the flow were the problem, the coolant in that branch would be hotter. A simple flow network model of the cold-plate circuit predicts the last branch receives 19 % less flow than average because it has the longest return path.

*Measure.* Thermocouples in the coolant show the outlet of the rear branch at 34 °C, against 30 °C for the others, confirming low flow. Ultrasonic flow measurement confirms a 24 % deficit. In addition, an X-ray of one prototype reveals a small air pocket trapped at the high point of that branch, which reduces effective plate area.

*Dominant term.* Flow maldistribution, made worse by trapped air.

*Fix and cross-check.* The manifold is redesigned with balanced, reverse-return routing so every branch has the same path length, and the filling procedure is changed to vacuum filling to eliminate trapped air. Spread falls to 3.5 K and charging time to 21.5 minutes. The team checks that the new manifold's higher pressure drop is within the pump's capability at low temperature, when the glycol mixture is most viscous.

*Lesson.* In thermal problems, measure the coolant before blaming the cells. Flow distribution in parallel channels is rarely as even as the drawing suggests.
:::

::: problem
**Field Problem — "Charging slows dramatically after ten minutes"**

*Symptom.* A customer reports that DC charging starts at the expected high power but drops sharply after about ten minutes, well before 50 % SoC, on hot summer days.

*Likely causes.* (1) Normal thermal derating because cell temperature has reached the BMS limit. (2) A weak chiller or low refrigerant charge, so the pack cannot be cooled below ambient. (3) A failing coolant pump or blocked radiator. (4) The charger itself derating because its own cables or power modules are hot. (5) The battery arrived at the charger already hot after hard motorway driving.

*How to tell them apart.* Check whether the vehicle or the charger is limiting power: the vehicle's logs will show its requested current; the charger may report its own limit. If the vehicle limits, look at cell temperatures, coolant inlet temperature and chiller operation. Coolant inlet temperature rising steadily during the session points to insufficient cooling capacity. Compare with a second charge in the evening or a different charger.
:::

## Manufacturing, Second Life and Recycling

A battery pack's story does not end when it leaves the vehicle.

When capacity falls below what a driver will accept, often around 70 to 80 % of the original, a pack may still be valuable for less demanding duties. *Second-life* applications such as stationary storage for buildings, solar farms or charging hubs can use packs or modules that are no longer good enough for a car. The business case depends on the cost of testing, repackaging and warranting used cells, which has proved harder than early enthusiasts hoped, and on competition from new stationary cells that keep getting cheaper.

Eventually every cell must be recycled. The main industrial routes are:

- **Pyrometallurgy:** smelting cells to recover nickel, cobalt and copper as an alloy. Robust and tolerant of mixed feed, but it loses lithium and aluminium to slag unless further processed, and it uses a lot of energy.
- **Hydrometallurgy:** shredding cells into "black mass" and leaching it with acids to recover nickel, cobalt, manganese and lithium as high-purity salts. It recovers more material, including lithium, and dominates new plants.
- **Direct recycling:** recovering cathode material while keeping its crystal structure and relithiating it. Potentially the most efficient, but still largely at pilot scale and sensitive to mixed chemistries.

Regulation is pushing hard in this direction. The European Union's Battery Regulation sets requirements for carbon footprint declarations, minimum recycled content in new batteries in the coming years, high recovery rates for lithium, cobalt and nickel, and a digital *battery passport* recording each pack's composition and history. For engineers, the practical consequence is *design for disassembly*: using fasteners rather than adhesives where possible, labelling chemistries clearly, and making it safe and quick to discharge and dismantle a pack. These goals sit uncomfortably beside the structural, cell-to-body trend, and resolving that tension is one of the interesting challenges of the next decade.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Pack requirements span energy, voltage, power, mass, volume, life, safety, environmental sealing, cost and end of life, and they conflict.
- Parallel cells share voltage and self-balance but share current unevenly; series cells share current, and the weakest cell limits the whole string's usable capacity.
- Architectures have moved from modules to cell-to-pack and cell-to-body designs, raising integration efficiency at the cost of repairability.
- The high-voltage path includes busbars, service disconnect, contactors, pre-charge, fuses, pyrofuses and current sensing. Joints must stay low-resistance for life.
- The enclosure provides crash and underbody protection, sealing, venting and swelling control.
- Liquid cold plates are the standard cooling method. Temperature uniformity matters as much as average temperature. Fast charging makes cooling a limiting factor; cold climates require heating.
- Single-cell thermal runaway must be contained using barriers, venting control, detection and chemistry choice.
- Second life and recycling are increasingly regulated; design for disassembly is becoming a requirement.
:::

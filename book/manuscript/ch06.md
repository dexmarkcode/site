Every December of my childhood followed the same ritual. The box of fairy lights came down from the loft, my father plugged them in, and nothing happened. Then followed an hour of patient misery as he worked along the string, swapping each tiny bulb for a spare until, suddenly, all forty lit up at once. Somewhere in that string a single filament had failed, and because the bulbs were wired one after another in a chain, one break was enough to put out the lot.

Those old fairy lights were a **series** circuit. The lights in your house, by contrast, are wired in **parallel**: if one bulb fails, the others carry on regardless. The difference between the two arrangements is not a technicality. It decides how a circuit behaves when things go wrong, how voltage and current are shared, and how much power each part receives.

Series and parallel connections are the two simplest ways of joining components, and they appear in almost every circuit you will ever analyse. In this chapter we will derive the rules for combining resistors in series and in parallel, and we will meet two of the most useful tools in all of circuit analysis: the **voltage divider rule** and the **current divider rule**. You will use them constantly from here to the end of the book.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- calculate the total resistance of resistors in series and in parallel;
- explain how current and voltage are shared in series and parallel circuits;
- use the voltage divider rule to find the voltage across any resistor in a series chain;
- use the current divider rule to find the current in any branch of a parallel group;
- choose resistor values to achieve a required voltage or resistance;
- explain why domestic wiring uses parallel connection;
- compare series and parallel circuits and recognise the duality between them.
:::

## 6.1 Two Ways of Joining Components

Figure 6.1 shows the two arrangements side by side, using lamps so that you can picture them working.

![Figure 6.1 — Four lamps connected (a) in series and (b) in parallel across the same supply. In (a) the current must pass through every lamp in turn; in (b) each lamp is connected directly across the supply.](fig/ch06_fairy.svg){width=100%}

In the **series** circuit (a), there is only one path. The current leaves the supply, passes through each lamp in turn and returns. Remove or break any lamp and the path is broken for all of them.

In the **parallel** circuit (b), each lamp is connected directly between the same two wires. Each has its own path from one side of the supply to the other. Remove one lamp and the others carry on, because their paths are untouched.

Recall the definitions from Chapter 4. Components are in series when they are joined end to end at a node with nothing else attached, so **they carry the same current**. Components are in parallel when they are connected between the same two nodes, so **they have the same voltage across them**. Everything in this chapter follows from those two facts, together with Kirchhoff's laws and Ohm's law.

## 6.2 Resistors in Series

Figure 6.2 shows three resistors in series across a source. One current, I, flows through all of them.

![Figure 6.2 — Three resistors in series. The same current flows through each, and the source voltage is shared between them.](fig/ch06_series.svg){width=66%}

By Ohm's law, the voltage across each resistor is

::: eq
V~1~ = IR~1~     V~2~ = IR~2~     V~3~ = IR~3~
:::

By KVL, the source voltage equals the sum of the voltage drops:

::: eq
V~S~ = V~1~ + V~2~ + V~3~ = IR~1~ + IR~2~ + IR~3~ = I(R~1~ + R~2~ + R~3~)
:::

Now suppose we replaced all three resistors with a single resistor R~T~ that drew the same current from the same source. Then V~S~ = IR~T~. Comparing the two expressions, R~T~ must be R~1~ + R~2~ + R~3~. The same argument works for any number of resistors.

::: keyeq
**Resistors in series**

R~T~ = R~1~ + R~2~ + R~3~ + …

The total resistance of resistors in series is the sum of the individual resistances.
:::

This should feel natural. Putting resistors end to end is like making a longer wire, and we saw in Chapter 2 that resistance is proportional to length. Notice two consequences:

- the total series resistance is always **greater than the largest** individual resistance;
- adding another resistor in series always **increases** the total and **reduces** the current.

The single resistor R~T~ is called the **equivalent resistance**. "Equivalent" has a precise meaning here: as far as the source can tell, the original resistors and the single equivalent are indistinguishable. Same voltage, same current. This idea of replacing part of a circuit by something simpler that behaves identically at its terminals is one of the most important in this book. It will carry us all the way to Thévenin's theorem.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.1 — A series circuit in full**

In Figure 6.2, V~S~ = 24 V, R~1~ = 2 Ω, R~2~ = 4 Ω and R~3~ = 6 Ω. Find the total resistance, the current, the voltage across each resistor and the power in each.

**Step 1: Total resistance.** R~T~ = 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 Ω.

**Step 2: Current.** I = V~S~ / R~T~ = 24 / 12 = 2 A. The same 2 A flows through every element.

**Step 3: Voltages.** V~1~ = 2 × 2 = 4 V; V~2~ = 2 × 4 = 8 V; V~3~ = 2 × 6 = 12 V. Check by KVL: 4 + 8 + 12 = 24 V. ✓

**Step 4: Powers.** P~1~ = 2² × 2 = 8 W; P~2~ = 2² × 4 = 16 W; P~3~ = 2² × 6 = 24 W. Total 48 W. The source delivers V~S~ × I = 24 × 2 = 48 W. ✓

**Observation.** In a series circuit the largest resistor takes the largest share of the voltage *and* the largest share of the power. This is the reason the element of a toaster glows while its cord stays cool, the puzzle we started Chapter 2 with.
:::

### What happens when something fails?

In a series circuit, an **open circuit** anywhere stops the current everywhere, as my father discovered every Christmas. Interestingly, the full supply voltage then appears across the break, because no current flows through the other resistors, so they drop no voltage. This is a useful fault-finding fact: in a series string that has stopped working, a voltmeter across the good components reads zero, and across the faulty one it reads the full supply voltage.

A **short circuit** across one element of a series circuit has the opposite effect. That element's resistance drops to zero, the total resistance falls, and the current *rises* through all the others. In a string of fairy lights, if one bulb shorted rather than went open, the remaining bulbs would each get a little more voltage and glow a little brighter – and, if several shorted, they would start to fail one after another.

### Sources in series

Sources can be connected in series too. Their voltages add if they **aid** each other (positive terminal of one joined to the negative of the next) and subtract if they **oppose** each other. This is how batteries are built: a 9 V battery is six 1.5 V cells in series, and a torch that takes three AA cells end to end gets 4.5 V. Put one of those cells in the wrong way round and the torch gets 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V, and glows very dimly, if at all. We met the opposing case in Worked Example 5.5.

## 6.3 The Voltage Divider Rule

In Worked Example 6.1 we found the current first and then each voltage. Often we want just *one* of the voltages, and there is a shortcut. Substituting I = V~S~ / R~T~ into V~x~ = IR~x~ gives:

::: keyeq
**The voltage divider rule**

V~x~ = V~S~ × {{frac|R~x~|R~T~}}

The voltage across any resistor in a series chain is the total voltage multiplied by that resistor's share of the total resistance.
:::

The rule says that **voltage divides in proportion to resistance**. A resistor that makes up a quarter of the total resistance gets a quarter of the total voltage. Check it against Example 6.1: R~3~ is 6 Ω out of 12 Ω, so it gets half of 24 V, which is 12 V. ✓

The most common application is the two-resistor **voltage divider**, or **potential divider**, of Figure 6.3. It produces an output voltage that is a fixed fraction of the input.

![Figure 6.3 — The two-resistor voltage divider. The output is taken across R~2~ and is a fixed fraction of V~S~, provided no significant current is drawn from the output.](fig/ch06_divider.svg){width=52%}

::: eq
V~out~ = V~S~ × {{frac|R~2~|R~1~ + R~2~}}
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 6.2 — Using the divider rule**

In Figure 6.3, V~S~ = 9 V, R~1~ = 10 kΩ and R~2~ = 20 kΩ. Find V~out~.

**Solution.**

V~out~ = 9 × {{frac|20|10 + 20}} = 9 × {{frac|2|3}} = 6 V

Notice that we never needed the current, and that we could leave the resistances in kilohms because they appear as a ratio. Ratios do not care about units, provided both resistances are in the *same* units.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 6.3 — Designing a divider**

A sensor needs about 5 V, and the only supply available is 12 V. The sensor draws negligible current. Design a divider with a total resistance of roughly 10 kΩ, using E12 values, and find the actual output.

**Step 1: Ideal ratio.** V~out~ / V~S~ = 5 / 12 = 0.417. So R~2~ should be 41.7 % of the total.

**Step 2: Ideal values.** With R~T~ = 10 kΩ: R~2~ = 4.17 kΩ and R~1~ = 5.83 kΩ.

**Step 3: Preferred values.** The nearest E12 values are R~2~ = 3.9 kΩ or 4.7 kΩ and R~1~ = 5.6 kΩ or 6.8 kΩ. Try R~1~ = 6.8 kΩ and R~2~ = 4.7 kΩ:

V~out~ = 12 × {{frac|4.7|6.8 + 4.7}} = 12 × 0.409 = 4.90 V

**Answer:** 6.8 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ give 4.90 V, within about 2 % of the target. If that is not close enough, E24 values or a trimming potentiometer could be used.
:::

There is an important condition hidden in Figure 6.3's caption and in the phrase "draws negligible current" in Example 6.3. The divider rule assumes the same current flows through R~1~ and R~2~. If you connect something to the output that draws a significant current, that is no longer true, and the output voltage falls. This is called **loading**, and it is so important that Chapter 8 is largely devoted to it.

### The potentiometer

A **potentiometer** is a voltage divider in a single component: a resistive track with a sliding contact, or **wiper**, that can be moved along it. The wiper divides the track into two parts, which act as R~1~ and R~2~. Turning the knob changes the ratio and therefore the output voltage, from zero at one end to the full input at the other. Volume controls, joystick sensors and the "trimmers" used to fine-tune circuits are all potentiometers.

## 6.4 Resistors in Parallel

Now consider resistors connected in parallel, as in Figure 6.4. All three are connected between the same two nodes, so the same voltage V appears across each.

![Figure 6.4 — Three resistors in parallel across a 24 V source. Each has the full supply voltage across it, and the total current is the sum of the branch currents.](fig/ch06_parallel.svg){width=74%}

By Ohm's law, the current in each branch is

::: eq
I~1~ = {{frac|V|R~1~}}     I~2~ = {{frac|V|R~2~}}     I~3~ = {{frac|V|R~3~}}
:::

By KCL at the top node, the total current is the sum of the branch currents:

::: eq
I~T~ = I~1~ + I~2~ + I~3~ = V ( {{frac|1|R~1~}} + {{frac|1|R~2~}} + {{frac|1|R~3~}} )
:::

If a single equivalent resistor R~T~ is to draw the same total current, then I~T~ = V / R~T~. Comparing the two expressions:

::: keyeq
**Resistors in parallel**

{{frac|1|R~T~}} = {{frac|1|R~1~}} + {{frac|1|R~2~}} + {{frac|1|R~3~}} + …

or, using conductance,  G~T~ = G~1~ + G~2~ + G~3~ + …
:::

The conductance form shows what is going on most clearly. Each parallel path offers its own conductance, its own ease of passage, and they simply add. Adding another path can only make it *easier* for current to flow. So:

- the total parallel resistance is always **less than the smallest** individual resistance;
- adding another resistor in parallel always **decreases** the total resistance and **increases** the total current.

That second point surprises many students. Connecting *more* resistors gives *less* resistance? Yes – in parallel, each new resistor is another path, another lane on the motorway.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.4 — Three resistors in parallel**

In Figure 6.4, R~1~ = 6 Ω, R~2~ = 12 Ω, R~3~ = 4 Ω and V = 24 V. Find the equivalent resistance, each branch current and the total current.

**Step 1: Equivalent resistance.**

{{frac|1|R~T~}} = {{frac|1|6}} + {{frac|1|12}} + {{frac|1|4}} = {{frac|2 + 1 + 3|12}} = {{frac|6|12}}

so R~T~ = 12 / 6 = 2 Ω. Notice that 2 Ω is less than the smallest resistor, 4 Ω, as it must be.

**Step 2: Branch currents.** I~1~ = 24 / 6 = 4 A; I~2~ = 24 / 12 = 2 A; I~3~ = 24 / 4 = 6 A.

**Step 3: Total current.** By KCL, I~T~ = 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 A. Check: V / R~T~ = 24 / 2 = 12 A. ✓

**Observation.** In a parallel circuit the *smallest* resistor takes the *largest* current and the largest power. This is the reverse of the series case.
:::

::: tip
**Calculator Tip**

To find a parallel resistance on a calculator, use the reciprocal key (often marked x^−1^ or 1/x): enter 6, press x^−1^, add 12 x^−1^, add 4 x^−1^, then press = and x^−1^ once more. The final reciprocal is the one people forget. If your answer is larger than the smallest resistor, you have forgotten it.
:::

### Two resistors in parallel: product over sum

For exactly two resistors, the reciprocal formula can be rearranged into a form that is quicker to use:

::: keyeq
**Two resistors in parallel**

R~T~ = {{frac|R~1~ × R~2~|R~1~ + R~2~}}

"Product over sum." This works for two resistors only.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 6.5 — Product over sum**

Find the equivalent resistance of 470 Ω in parallel with 1 kΩ.

**Solution.**

R~T~ = {{frac|470 × 1000|470 + 1000}} = {{frac|470 000|1470}} = 320 Ω
:::

Two special cases are worth memorising because they let you do many problems in your head:

- **Equal resistors.** n equal resistors R in parallel give R / n. Two 10 kΩ resistors in parallel make 5 kΩ; four 100 Ω resistors make 25 Ω.
- **Very unequal resistors.** If one resistor is much larger than the other (say ten times or more), the parallel combination is only slightly less than the smaller one. 100 Ω in parallel with 10 kΩ is 99 Ω. The large resistor barely matters. This is why a good voltmeter, with its very high resistance, hardly disturbs the circuit it measures.

### Working backwards

Sometimes you know the parallel resistance you want and one of the resistors, and need the other. Rearranging the reciprocal formula does the job.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.6 — Finding a missing parallel resistor**

A 330 Ω resistor is already fitted on a board. What resistor must be connected in parallel with it to give 200 Ω?

**Solution.**

{{frac|1|R~x~}} = {{frac|1|200}} − {{frac|1|330}} = {{frac|330 − 200|200 × 330}} = {{frac|130|66 000}}

R~x~ = 66 000 / 130 = 508 Ω. The nearest E12 value is 560 Ω, which would give 330 × 560 / 890 = 208 Ω; the E24 value 510 Ω gives 200.4 Ω.
:::

## 6.5 The Current Divider Rule

Just as voltage divides between series resistors, current divides between parallel ones. For the two-branch case of Figure 6.5, we can find I~1~ without first finding the voltage.

![Figure 6.5 — A current divider. A total current I~T~ splits between two parallel resistors.](fig/ch06_current_divider.svg){width=52%}

The voltage across the pair is V = I~T~ × R~T~ = I~T~ × R~1~R~2~ / (R~1~ + R~2~). Then I~1~ = V / R~1~, which simplifies to:

::: keyeq
**The current divider rule (two branches)**

I~1~ = I~T~ × {{frac|R~2~|R~1~ + R~2~}}     I~2~ = I~T~ × {{frac|R~1~|R~1~ + R~2~}}

Note carefully: the current in one branch depends on the **other** branch's resistance.
:::

The "other resistor on top" feature trips people up, so let us make sense of it. Current prefers the easier path. If R~2~ is large, current is discouraged from going that way and more of it flows through R~1~. So a large R~2~ means a large I~1~, which is exactly what the formula says.

For more than two branches it is neater to use conductances, and then the rule looks just like the voltage divider rule:

::: eq
I~x~ = I~T~ × {{frac|G~x~|G~T~}}
:::

Current divides in proportion to **conductance**, just as voltage divides in proportion to resistance. Here the duality between series and parallel is especially clear.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.7 — Splitting a current**

A current of 3 A enters a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 3 Ω. Find the current in each.

**Solution.** Using the two-branch rule:

I~6Ω~ = 3 × {{frac|3|6 + 3}} = 1 A     I~3Ω~ = 3 × {{frac|6|6 + 3}} = 2 A

**Check.** 1 + 2 = 3 A ✓. And both resistors have the same voltage: 1 × 6 = 6 V and 2 × 3 = 6 V ✓. The smaller resistor carries twice the current because it has half the resistance.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 6.8 — Three branches, using conductance**

A current of 14 mA divides between resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ in parallel. Find the current in the 2 kΩ resistor.

**Step 1: Conductances (in millisiemens).** G~1~ = 1 mS, G~2~ = 0.5 mS, G~3~ = 0.25 mS. G~T~ = 1.75 mS.

**Step 2: Divide.** I~2~ = 14 × 0.5 / 1.75 = 4 mA.

**Check.** The voltage across the group is I~T~ / G~T~ = 14 mA / 1.75 mS = 8 V, and 8 V / 2 kΩ = 4 mA. ✓
:::

## 6.6 Parallel Circuits at Home

Every socket and every light in your house is connected in parallel across the supply. Figure 6.6 shows the idea in simplified form. There are three good reasons for this.

![Figure 6.6 — Domestic loads are connected in parallel between the live (L) and neutral (N) conductors, so each receives the full supply voltage and can be switched independently. (The supply is AC, shown by the wave symbol, but the series–parallel principles are identical.)](fig/ch06_house.svg){width=86%}

1. **Every appliance gets the full voltage.** Appliances are designed for 230 V. In parallel, each receives exactly that, regardless of what else is switched on (apart from the small cable voltage drops we met in Chapter 3).
2. **Each appliance works independently.** Switching off the kettle does not switch off the lights, and a failed bulb in the hall does not affect the bulb in the kitchen.
3. **Appliances can be added freely.** Plugging in another appliance simply adds another parallel branch and draws more current from the supply.

That third point has a consequence. Because currents in parallel branches add, every appliance you switch on increases the total current in the supply cable. Each circuit is therefore protected by a fuse or circuit breaker in the consumer unit, rated for the cable it protects.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.9 — Breakfast time**

On a 230 V ring circuit protected by a 32 A breaker, a 3 kW kettle, a 1 kW toaster and a 100 W lamp are all on together. Find the total current and the equivalent resistance of the three loads.

**Step 1: Individual currents.** I = P / V for each: kettle 3000 / 230 = 13.04 A; toaster 1000 / 230 = 4.35 A; lamp 100 / 230 = 0.43 A.

**Step 2: Total current (KCL).** I~T~ = 13.04 + 4.35 + 0.43 = 17.8 A, comfortably under 32 A.

**Step 3: Equivalent resistance.** R~T~ = V / I~T~ = 230 / 17.8 = 12.9 Ω.

**Alternative.** Total power is 4100 W, so I~T~ = 4100 / 230 = 17.8 A directly. In a parallel circuit, powers add just as currents do.
:::

## 6.7 Series and Parallel Side by Side

It is worth gathering the results of this chapter into a single comparison. Read the two columns of Table 6.1 together and you will see that each statement about series circuits has a mirror image for parallel circuits, with voltage and current, and resistance and conductance, swapped over. This is the **duality** we first noticed in Chapter 5.

::: {.table-wrap}
| | Series | Parallel |
|:--|:--|:--|
| What is shared | The same **current** | The same **voltage** |
| What adds up | Voltages: V~T~ = V~1~ + V~2~ + … | Currents: I~T~ = I~1~ + I~2~ + … |
| Combining | Resistances add: R~T~ = R~1~ + R~2~ + … | Conductances add: G~T~ = G~1~ + G~2~ + … |
| Equivalent value | Greater than the largest | Less than the smallest |
| Divider rule | Voltage divides in proportion to R | Current divides in proportion to G |
| Largest power in | The **largest** resistor | The **smallest** resistor |
| Adding an element | Increases R~T~, reduces current | Decreases R~T~, increases current |
| An open circuit | Stops all current | Affects only its own branch |
| A short circuit | Removes one element; current rises | Shorts the whole group and the supply |

: Table 6.1 — Series and parallel circuits compared.
:::

The last row deserves emphasis. A short circuit across one branch of a parallel group puts a short across *every* branch and across the supply itself, because they all share the same pair of nodes. This is why a fault in one appliance can trip the breaker for a whole circuit, and why the protective devices exist.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Forgetting the final reciprocal** when adding parallel resistances.
- **Using "product over sum" for three or more resistors.** It only works for two. For more, use reciprocals, or combine two at a time.
- **Putting the wrong resistor on top** in the current divider rule. The current in one branch depends on the *other* resistor.
- **Applying the voltage divider rule to a loaded divider.** If current is drawn from the output, the simple rule no longer holds.
- **Mixing units in a ratio.** Both resistances must be in the same units, though they need not be in ohms.
- **Sanity check:** a series total must exceed the largest resistor; a parallel total must be below the smallest.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Series**: same current through each element. R~T~ = R~1~ + R~2~ + …, always greater than the largest.
- **Voltage divider rule**: V~x~ = V~S~ × R~x~ / R~T~. Voltage divides in proportion to resistance.
- **Parallel**: same voltage across each element. 1/R~T~ = 1/R~1~ + 1/R~2~ + …, or G~T~ = G~1~ + G~2~ + …; always less than the smallest.
- **Two in parallel**: R~T~ = R~1~R~2~ / (R~1~ + R~2~). **n equal** resistors: R/n.
- **Current divider rule**: I~1~ = I~T~ × R~2~ / (R~1~ + R~2~), or I~x~ = I~T~ × G~x~ / G~T~. Current divides in proportion to conductance.
- An open circuit in **series** stops everything; in **parallel** it affects only its own branch.
- Domestic loads are wired in **parallel** so each gets full voltage and works independently.
- Series and parallel are **duals** of one another.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

R~T~ = R~1~ + R~2~ + …     V~x~ = V~S~ R~x~ / R~T~     1/R~T~ = 1/R~1~ + 1/R~2~ + …     R~T~ = R~1~R~2~ / (R~1~ + R~2~)     I~1~ = I~T~ R~2~ / (R~1~ + R~2~)
:::

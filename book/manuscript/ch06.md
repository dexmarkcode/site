# Battery Management Systems: Estimation, Protection and Ageing

::: chapter-intro
The battery management system is the battery's nervous system and its conscience. It measures every cell, estimates quantities that cannot be measured directly, such as state of charge, state of health and available power, keeps the cells balanced, controls charging, and shuts everything down when something goes wrong. This chapter explains how it does each of those jobs and why some of them are much harder than they look.
:::

Ask a driver what the battery management system does and you will probably hear, "It shows how much charge is left." That is true, and it is the part of the BMS everyone sees. But the percentage on the dashboard is the output of an estimation problem that has occupied battery engineers for decades, and it is only one of a dozen jobs the system performs, many of them safety-critical.

A useful way to think about the BMS is as three layers. At the bottom is *measurement*: hardware that reads cell voltages, temperatures and current accurately and reliably. In the middle is *estimation*: software that turns those measurements into quantities that matter, such as how full the battery is, how healthy it is and how much power it can safely deliver. At the top is *control and protection*: decisions about contactors, charging, balancing, cooling and fault reactions. Weakness in any layer shows up in the others. A brilliant estimation algorithm cannot fix a current sensor with a drifting offset, and perfect measurements are wasted if the protection logic reacts too slowly.

## Architecture and Measurement

### Hardware architecture

Figure 5.2 in the previous chapter showed the typical arrangement. *Cell monitoring units*, each built around an analogue front-end chip, sit close to the cells and measure their voltages and temperatures. Each chip handles 12 to 18 series cells and also contains the switches used for passive balancing. The chips communicate, through an isolated daisy-chain link or a wireless network, with the *battery management controller*, which runs the estimation and control software. The controller also reads the pack current sensor, drives the contactors, monitors isolation and the interlock loop, and talks to the rest of the vehicle over CAN or Ethernet.

Separating the monitoring units from the controller has practical advantages. The analogue measurement happens close to the cells, keeping sense wires short, and the controller sits in a quieter environment at low voltage. Some systems are *distributed*, with monitoring units in each module; others are *centralised*, with all measurement electronics on one board connected to the cells by a long harness. Wireless designs remove the communication harness entirely, which saves weight and eliminates a frequent source of connector faults, at the cost of radio engineering and cyber-security work.

### Measurement accuracy

Measurement quality sets the ceiling on everything the BMS can do. Three quantities matter most.

**Cell voltage** must be measured to within a millivolt or two. That sounds excessive, but on the flat part of an LFP curve, a 2 mV error can correspond to 5 % or more of state of charge. Modern front-end chips achieve this accuracy across temperature and life, provided the board layout, filtering and reference voltages are designed carefully.

**Current** is measured by a shunt or a Hall-effect or fluxgate sensor, as described in Chapter 3. What matters is not just accuracy at high current but the *offset* at zero current. A sensor that reads 0.3 A when no current flows will add 7.2 Ah of phantom charge every day. Precision shunt measurement with careful offset calibration is now standard in high-quality systems.

**Temperature** is measured with thermistors at selected points. There are never as many as the engineers would like, because each one adds wires, cost and potential faults. A pack with 192 cells might have 32 to 48 temperature sensors, placed where thermal modelling predicts the hottest and coldest cells will be. The BMS must estimate the rest.

A subtle but important requirement is *synchronisation*. When the BMS estimates cell resistance by comparing changes in voltage and current, the measurements must be taken at the same instant. If voltage is sampled a few milliseconds after current during a rapidly changing load, the estimate will be wrong. Front-end chips with simultaneous sampling solve this.

## Estimating State of Charge

State of charge is the fraction of the battery's usable charge that remains. It cannot be measured directly. It must be inferred, and the BMS has three main sources of information.

### Coulomb counting

The most direct method is to keep a running total of the charge that has flowed in and out:

$$ \mathrm{SoC}(t) = \mathrm{SoC}(0) - \frac{1}{Q}\int_0^t \eta\, i(\tau)\, d\tau $$ {6.1}

Here *Q* is the cell or pack capacity in coulombs (or ampere-seconds), *i* is the current, positive for discharge, and *η* is the coulombic efficiency, very close to 1 for lithium-ion cells. Coulomb counting is simple, responds instantly and is very accurate over short periods. Its problems are that it needs an accurate starting value, an accurate capacity and, above all, an accurate current sensor. Any error in the current, however small, integrates over time, and the estimate drifts.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.1 — How fast does a coulomb counter drift?**

A 150 Ah pack has a current sensor with an uncorrected offset of 0.25 A. The car is driven for two hours a day and spends the rest of the time parked with the BMS asleep, and counting stops while asleep. How much SoC error accumulates per day? What if the BMS stays awake 24 hours a day, for example while the car is plugged in and managing a schedule?

*Solution.* While awake for 2 h: error = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 Ah, or 0.5 / 150 = 0.33 % of SoC per day.

Awake for 24 h: error = 0.25 × 24 = 6 Ah, or 4 % per day.

In a week of continuous wakefulness, the counter would be 28 % wrong. Coulomb counting alone is not enough; it must be corrected periodically by an independent measurement.
:::

### Open-circuit voltage

The second source is the open-circuit voltage. Chapter 4 showed that OCV is a known function of SoC for each chemistry. If the battery has rested long enough for the voltage to settle, the BMS can read the OCV and look up the SoC directly. This gives an absolute reference that does not drift.

The catches are that the battery must be at rest, which in a car means parked for perhaps an hour or more; that relaxation is slow at low temperature; that hysteresis means the OCV depends on whether the cell was last charged or discharged; and that on a flat curve, such as LFP's, a tiny voltage error means a large SoC error. For NMC, OCV correction works well. For LFP, it works well only near the top and bottom of charge, where the curve is steep.

### Model-based estimation and the Kalman filter

The third and most powerful approach combines the two using a model of the cell. The BMS runs the equivalent circuit model of Chapter 4 in real time, feeding it the measured current. The model predicts what the terminal voltage *should* be. The BMS compares the prediction with the measured voltage, and uses the difference to correct its estimate of SoC.

The standard tool for this is the *Kalman filter*, or more precisely its extensions for non-linear systems: the *extended Kalman filter* (EKF) and *unscented Kalman filter* (UKF). The idea is easier to grasp than the mathematics. At every time step the filter does two things.

First, it *predicts*: it uses coulomb counting and the model to move its estimate of SoC and the RC voltages forward, and it increases its uncertainty slightly to account for sensor errors and model imperfections.

Second, it *corrects*: it compares the model's predicted terminal voltage with the measured one. If they disagree, it adjusts the SoC estimate in the direction that would reduce the disagreement. How much it adjusts depends on a gain, the *Kalman gain*, which balances the uncertainty in the prediction against the uncertainty in the measurement:

$$ \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^{-} + K_k\,\big(V_{meas,k} - \hat{V}_k\big) $$ {6.2}

When the SoC estimate is uncertain and the voltage measurement is informative (on a steep part of the OCV curve), the gain is high and the voltage pulls the estimate firmly. When the OCV curve is flat, a voltage error says little about SoC, so the gain is low and the filter relies mainly on coulomb counting. The filter adapts automatically to the information available.

![**Figure 6.1** State-of-charge estimation over a two-day test with a deliberate 0.3 A current-sensor offset and a 6 % initial error. Pure coulomb counting keeps its initial error and drifts further; the model-based filter converges on the true value within the first hour and tracks it thereafter.](figures/fig6_1.png)

Figure 6.1 shows the difference this makes. The well-tuned filter recovers from a large initial error and stays close to the truth despite the sensor offset. Its accuracy depends on the quality of the cell model, which in turn depends on temperature-dependent parameter tables and on how well those parameters are updated as the cell ages. Production systems commonly achieve SoC errors of 2 to 3 % across most conditions for NMC, and slightly worse for LFP in the middle of the charge range.

### The LFP problem

LFP's flat OCV makes estimation genuinely harder. In the middle of the range, a 10 % change in SoC may shift the OCV by only 10 or 15 mV, and hysteresis effects are of a similar size. The Kalman filter correctly reduces its reliance on voltage there, so the estimate drifts with the coulomb counter until the battery reaches a steep region near full or empty, where the error is corrected in one jump. Owners notice this as a displayed charge that suddenly falls from, say, 30 % to 20 %.

The practical remedies are better current sensing, careful hysteresis modelling, and periodic full charges. Charging to 100 % takes the cell into the steep region at the top of the curve, giving the BMS a reliable reference. This is one reason manufacturers recommend that LFP vehicles be charged to full regularly, while recommending the opposite for nickel-based packs.

### What the driver sees

The SoC shown on the dashboard is rarely the raw estimate. It is usually the *usable* SoC, rescaled so that 0 % and 100 % correspond to the limits of the usable window rather than the cell's absolute limits. It is filtered to avoid visible jumps. And it may be adjusted at low temperatures, when less energy is actually available because of higher resistance. The range estimate goes further, combining SoC with a forecast of consumption based on recent driving, the planned route, elevation, temperature and climate control use. A large share of driver complaints about EVs concern this "guess-o-meter", and most of them are about the consumption forecast rather than the SoC.

## Estimating State of Health

State of health (SoH) describes how much the battery has aged. There are two common definitions, and they measure different things:

- **Capacity-based SoH**: the present capacity divided by the capacity when new. This governs range and is the basis of most warranties.
- **Resistance-based SoH**: how much the internal resistance has grown. This governs available power, especially in the cold, and fast-charging performance.

Neither can be measured directly in a vehicle without a full controlled discharge, which never happens in normal use. The BMS must infer them from everyday operation.

### Capacity estimation

The simplest method compares two SoC estimates made from OCV readings at different times, with the charge counted between them:

$$ Q = \frac{\int \eta\, i\, dt}{\mathrm{SoC}_1 - \mathrm{SoC}_2} $$ {6.3}

If the car rests overnight at 80 % SoC, is driven, and rests again at 35 %, and 66 Ah flowed in between, the capacity is 66 / 0.45 = 147 Ah. The method is only as good as the two OCV-based SoC estimates, so the BMS uses many such measurements, weights them by their reliability, and filters the result over weeks. A large SoC swing gives a more accurate result than a small one.

More sophisticated approaches estimate capacity as a parameter inside the Kalman filter itself, or use *incremental capacity analysis*. This technique plots the derivative of charge with respect to voltage, d*Q*/d*V*, during a slow, steady charge. The resulting curve has peaks corresponding to the phase transitions in the electrode materials. As the cell ages, the peaks shrink and shift in characteristic ways, as Figure 6.2 shows, and the pattern reveals not just how much capacity has been lost but *why*: loss of lithium inventory shifts the peaks, while loss of active material shrinks them.

![**Figure 6.2** Incremental capacity (d*Q*/d*V*) curves for a new and an aged cell during slow charging. The height and position of the peaks change with the underlying degradation mechanisms, allowing diagnosis from charging data alone.](figures/fig6_2.png)

Home AC charging, at a steady low current, provides ideal data for this kind of analysis, which is one reason why modern vehicles upload charging data to the manufacturer's servers. Cloud-based analytics can compare a vehicle's battery against thousands of similar ones and spot anomalies that no on-board algorithm could.

### Resistance estimation

Resistance is easier. Every time the current changes quickly, as it does constantly during driving, the instantaneous voltage change divided by the current change gives an estimate of *R*~0~:

$$ R_0 \approx \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} $$ {6.4}

The BMS collects thousands of such estimates, normalises them to a reference temperature and SoC using its parameter tables, and filters them. Growth in *R*~0~ is a good indicator of ageing and is also an early warning of problems such as a loose busbar joint, which shows up as a sudden step in the resistance of one cell group.

## Estimating State of Power

The vehicle's control unit needs to know, at every moment, how much power it may draw from the battery and how much it may push back into it, over the next few seconds. Ask for too little, and performance and regeneration suffer. Ask for too much, and a cell will hit its voltage limit, or be driven into lithium plating during regeneration.

The BMS computes these *power limits* from its model. Using the approach of Worked Example 4.1, it calculates the maximum current that keeps the weakest cell above its minimum voltage at the end of a defined pulse (often 2, 10 and 30 seconds), and the maximum charge current that keeps it below its maximum voltage. It then applies further limits for temperature, SoC, the ratings of fuses, contactors and busbars, and any fault conditions. The result, typically a map like Figure 6.3, is updated every few milliseconds and sent to the vehicle control unit.

![**Figure 6.3** Indicative discharge and regenerative power limits as a function of cell temperature at 50 % SoC. Low temperature cuts both, because resistance rises and plating risk grows; high temperature brings derating to protect the cells from further heating.](figures/fig6_3.png)

The regenerative limit is often the more restrictive in winter. A cold battery may accept only a few kilowatts of charging, which is why some EVs warn drivers that regenerative braking is limited on a cold morning, and why the friction brakes then do more of the work.

## Cell Balancing

Chapter 5 showed that in a series string, the cell with the lowest charge limits discharge and the cell with the highest charge limits charging. Cells drift apart in SoC because their self-discharge rates differ slightly, because they are at different temperatures, and because the monitoring electronics themselves draw slightly different currents from different cells. *Balancing* corrects this drift.

### Passive balancing

The almost universal approach is *passive* balancing: each cell has a resistor that can be switched across it to bleed away a small current, typically 50 to 200 mA. The BMS identifies cells with a higher SoC than the rest and discharges them gently until they match. The energy is wasted as heat, but the amounts are small, and the circuit is cheap and reliable. Balancing usually happens when the car is charging or parked, using the OCV to judge SoC accurately.

### Active balancing

*Active* balancing moves charge from high cells to low cells using switched-capacitor, inductor or transformer circuits, rather than burning it off. It is more efficient and can, in principle, extract the full capacity of a pack with mismatched cells by continuously transferring charge to the weak cells during discharge. But it costs more and is more complex. In automotive packs with well-matched cells, the benefit has rarely justified the cost, and active balancing remains uncommon. It is more attractive in second-life applications, where cells may be badly mismatched.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.2 — How long does balancing take?**

After a winter, one cell group in a 96s pack sits 3 % of SoC higher than the rest. The cells are 150 Ah, and the passive balancing current is 100 mA. How long will it take to bring the cell into line, and how much energy is dissipated?

*Solution.* Excess charge = 0.03 × 150 = 4.5 Ah.

Time at 100 mA = 4.5 / 0.1 = 45 hours.

Energy = 4.5 Ah × 3.7 V ≈ 16.7 Wh, dissipated as heat in the balancing resistor over that period, about 0.37 W.

Forty-five hours is a long time. Balancing usually runs only during charging and for some time after, so a gross imbalance may take several days of normal use to correct. Designers choose the balancing current by estimating the worst-case divergence rate of the cells over a period without balancing, and making sure balancing can keep up with it. A cell that needs balancing every day, as in the Field Problem in Chapter 4, is telling you something.
:::

## Protection and Fault Handling

Above all, the BMS must keep the battery safe. That means detecting dangerous conditions quickly and reliably, and reacting in a way that leads to a *safe state* without creating a new hazard. Opening the contactors is safe for the battery, but if it happens on a motorway at 120 km/h, the driver loses propulsion. Fault reactions are therefore graded.

Table: **Table 6.1** Typical BMS protection functions and reactions. Thresholds are indicative and depend on chemistry and supplier specification.

| Condition | Typical threshold (NMC) | Graded reaction |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Cell over-voltage | > 4.20–4.25 V | Reduce charge current; stop charging; open contactors if exceeded further |
| Cell under-voltage | < 2.8–3.0 V | Reduce discharge power; warn driver; open contactors at a lower limit |
| Over-current | Above fuse and contactor ratings | Limit power request; open contactors; fuse or pyrofuse clears short circuit |
| Over-temperature | > 55–60 °C | Derate power; increase cooling; stop charging |
| Under-temperature | < −20 to −30 °C | Restrict power and charging; heat pack |
| Isolation fault | Below 500 then 100 Ω/V | Warn; prevent contactor closure at next start |
| HVIL open | Loop broken | Open contactors within milliseconds |
| Contactor welded | Voltage across open contactor absent | Warn; prevent operation; request service |
| Thermal event | Rapid temperature or pressure rise, gas detection | Warn occupants; open contactors; maximise cooling |

### Functional safety

Several of these functions are safety-critical in the formal sense of ISO 26262, the automotive functional safety standard discussed in Chapter 14. Preventing cell over-charge, for example, is typically rated at a high *Automotive Safety Integrity Level* (ASIL), often ASIL C or D, because a failure could lead to a fire. That rating drives design requirements: redundant voltage measurement through independent paths, diagnostic coverage of the measurement chips, a separate safety microcontroller or a lock-step processor, and documented fault reaction times. In practice, many front-end chips include a second, independent over- and under-voltage comparator for exactly this purpose.

### Contactor diagnostics

Contactors can fail in two dangerous ways: welding shut, so the pack cannot be disconnected, or failing to close. The BMS checks both at every start and stop by measuring voltages on either side of each contactor. A contactor that should be open but shows no voltage across it is welded. Because contactor welds usually result from opening under heavy current or closing onto a large voltage difference, the BMS also logs every opening and the current at the time, building a picture of each contactor's remaining life.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Contactor weld fault**

*Symptom.* The vehicle reports a contactor fault and refuses to start; the stored code indicates the negative contactor is suspected welded.

*Likely causes.* (1) Genuine welding after an emergency opening under load, such as a crash signal or an isolation fault that forced the contactors open at high current. (2) Closure onto a large voltage difference because pre-charge had not completed. (3) A false indication from a failed voltage-sense path or an external voltage present on the vehicle side, such as a DC charger still connected. (4) Software timing in the weld check.

*How to tell them apart.* Review the event history for high-current openings. Check whether the fault occurs only after DC charging sessions, which would point to voltage remaining on the inlet side. A trained technician can verify contactor state with the pack isolated and measure contact resistance on the bench. A genuine weld requires replacement of the disconnect unit and investigation of what caused it.
:::

## Charging Control

The BMS decides how fast the battery may be charged. The classic charging method is *constant current, constant voltage* (CC-CV): charge at a fixed current until the highest cell reaches its maximum voltage, then hold that voltage while the current tapers away. CC-CV is still used for AC charging.

For DC fast charging, modern systems use multi-stage profiles that change the current as SoC and temperature change, as shown in Figure 6.5. The underlying goal is to keep the anode potential safely above the level at which lithium plating begins. Since anode potential cannot be measured directly in a production cell, the limits are derived from extensive testing of instrumented cells with reference electrodes and from electrochemical models, and stored as tables of permitted current versus SoC and temperature. Some newer systems estimate anode potential in real time using reduced electrochemical models.

![**Figure 6.5** A typical DC fast-charge profile for an 800 V car. Power rises quickly to its peak, holds while the cells are cool and the anode has headroom, then steps down as SoC rises. The shape of the curve, rather than the peak, determines the 10–80 % charging time.](figures/fig6_5.png)

Two points about Figure 6.5 deserve emphasis. First, the peak power that appears in advertising is held only briefly. What drivers experience is the *average* power over a typical session, usually 10 to 80 %. Second, the top 20 % of charge is slow for every lithium-ion battery, because the cells approach their voltage limit and plating risk rises. That is why the industry quotes 10–80 % times, and why, on a long journey, it is usually quicker to make two short stops than one long one.

::: example
**Worked Example 6.3 — Charging time from a profile**

A car has 80 kWh of usable energy. Its fast-charge profile, at a warm battery temperature, is approximately: 10–40 % at 230 kW, 40–60 % at 170 kW, 60–80 % at 110 kW. Charging losses are 6 %. Estimate the 10–80 % time and the average power.

*Solution.* Each 10 % of SoC is 8 kWh into the battery; allowing for losses, the charger must supply 8 / 0.94 = 8.51 kWh, but the battery-side power is what the profile describes, so we use battery energy and battery power.

10–40 %: 24 kWh at 230 kW = 0.104 h = 6.3 min.
40–60 %: 16 kWh at 170 kW = 0.094 h = 5.6 min.
60–80 %: 16 kWh at 110 kW = 0.145 h = 8.7 min.

Total ≈ 20.6 minutes. Average battery power = 56 kWh / 0.344 h = 163 kW, about 70 % of the peak. If the battery arrives cold and the first stage is limited to 120 kW, the first stage takes 12 minutes instead of 6.3, and the total stretches to around 26 minutes.
:::

## Managing a Battery Through Its Life

The BMS shapes how quickly the battery ages through decisions that the driver rarely sees.

The *usable window* is the most important. A pack designed to use 4 to 96 % of its true capacity will age more slowly than one using 0 to 100 %. Some manufacturers start with a slightly narrower window and gradually release the buffer as the cells age, so that the driver's usable range stays roughly constant for the first years. This is legitimate engineering, but it makes "how much has my battery degraded?" a surprisingly difficult question for an owner to answer from the dashboard.

*Charging defaults* matter too. Many vehicles default to a daily charge limit of 80 or 90 % for nickel-based packs, schedule charging to finish shortly before departure so the battery does not sit full, and precondition the pack before fast charging. Each of these extends life without the driver doing anything.

*Thermal strategy* while parked is another lever. Cooling a pack in a hot climate costs energy, but it slows calendar ageing; the right balance depends on the climate, the chemistry and how long the car is parked.

Finally, the BMS is the source of the *data* that make the battery's history visible: cumulative energy throughput, time spent at each temperature and SoC, fast-charge counts, capacity and resistance estimates. That history increasingly determines the value of a used vehicle, the eligibility of a pack for second life, and, under the European battery passport rules, information that must be available to regulators, repairers and recyclers.

::: casefile
**Case File 6 — The disappearing ten per cent**

*The complaint.* Owners of a new LFP-equipped model report that the displayed state of charge sometimes drops suddenly by 8 to 12 % within a few kilometres, usually when the battery is between 15 and 30 %. Several have been caught out with less range than expected.

*State it in numbers.* Data from 2 000 vehicles show that the jumps occur on average after nine days without a charge above 95 %, and that their size correlates with the time since the last full charge, about 1.2 % per day.

*Draw the boundary.* The SoC estimator (filter tuning, hysteresis model, OCV tables), the current measurement and the display filtering.

*Simplest model.* The drift rate of about 1.2 % per day is consistent with a coulomb counter running with an uncorrected error, which the filter cannot correct while the cells sit on the flat part of the LFP curve. When the cells finally reach the steep low-SoC region, the voltage information becomes decisive and the filter jumps to the truth.

*Measure.* Bench tests of the current measurement show a temperature-dependent offset of up to 0.18 A at low temperature, beyond the calibration range, combined with a self-consumption current of the vehicle's telematics module while the car sleeps that the BMS does not count, because it is asleep. Together these explain about 1.1 % per day.

*Dominant term.* Uncounted sleep-mode current, with a smaller contribution from sensor offset.

*Fix and cross-check.* The BMS wakes briefly every hour to integrate the sleep current, the current-sensor offset is recalibrated at every contactor opening, when current is known to be zero, and the hysteresis model is refined. The vehicle also prompts owners to charge to 100 % at least once a week, which the manufacturer already recommended but had buried in the handbook. Jump sizes fall below 3 %. The display filter is also changed to spread any remaining correction over several kilometres rather than one step.

*Lesson.* A good estimator can only be as good as the information it is given. With LFP, the information is sparse, so every source of unmeasured current matters.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The BMS has three layers: measurement, estimation and control/protection. Measurement quality, especially current offset and cell voltage accuracy, limits estimation.
- SoC is estimated by combining coulomb counting, which drifts, with OCV information, which needs rest and a steep curve. Kalman filters blend the two using a cell model and adapt to how informative the voltage is.
- LFP's flat OCV makes SoC estimation hard; regular full charges give the BMS a reference.
- SoH is estimated from capacity (using charge between two SoC points, or incremental capacity analysis) and from resistance growth.
- State of power limits are calculated from the cell model and further restricted by temperature, SoC and hardware ratings.
- Passive balancing bleeds charge from high cells; active balancing transfers it. Balancing current must keep pace with natural divergence.
- Protection reactions are graded to reach a safe state without creating new hazards. Key functions are safety-rated under ISO 26262.
- Fast-charging profiles keep the anode clear of lithium plating; 10–80 % time depends on the whole curve, not the peak.
- The usable window, charging defaults and thermal strategy chosen by the BMS strongly influence battery life.
:::

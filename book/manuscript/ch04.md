# Inside the Lithium-Ion Cell

::: chapter-intro
Every important property of an electric vehicle battery, from its range and charging speed to its lifetime and its behaviour in a fire, starts inside a single cell. This chapter opens one up. We look at how lithium ions shuttle between the electrodes, compare the cathode and anode chemistries now in production, examine the physical formats cells come in, model their electrical behaviour, and explain why they age and how they fail.
:::

A lithium-ion cell is easy to describe and extraordinarily hard to make well. On paper it is two coated metal foils, a plastic separator and a little liquid, rolled or stacked and sealed in a can. In practice the coatings are tens of microns thick and must be uniform across a metre of foil, the separator is thinner than a human hair, a speck of metal dust can cause a short circuit years later, and the liquid must be kept almost entirely free of water. The best cell factories in the world spend years learning to hold their scrap rates down.

As a vehicle engineer you are unlikely to design cells yourself. But you will choose them, specify them, test them and live with their behaviour for a decade. The decisions you make about how to charge them, cool them and protect them depend on understanding what is going on inside. So let us start with the chemistry, and keep it as physical as possible.

## How a Lithium-Ion Cell Works

A lithium-ion cell has two electrodes, a negative one (the *anode* during discharge) and a positive one (the *cathode* during discharge), separated by a porous plastic film called the *separator* and soaked in a liquid *electrolyte*. Each electrode is a thin layer of active material particles, mixed with a conductive carbon additive and a polymer binder, coated onto a metal foil that collects the current: copper for the negative electrode, aluminium for the positive.

![**Figure 4.1** Operation of a lithium-ion cell during discharge. Lithium ions leave the graphite anode, cross the electrolyte and separator, and insert themselves into the metal-oxide cathode. Electrons travel the other way through the external circuit, doing useful work. On charge, both flows reverse.](figures/fig4_1.png)

The key idea is *intercalation*. Both electrodes are made of materials with a layered or tunnelled crystal structure that can host lithium ions in the spaces between their atoms without being destroyed. In a charged cell, most of the lithium sits in the negative electrode, usually graphite, nestled between the carbon layers. During discharge, lithium atoms in the graphite give up an electron each and leave as positive ions. The ions travel through the electrolyte and separator to the positive electrode, where they slot into the crystal of a metal oxide or phosphate. The electrons cannot pass through the electrolyte, so they take the long way round, through the external circuit, and that is the current that drives the motor. Charging forces the process into reverse.

Because the lithium is always present as ions in the electrolyte and never as bulk metal (at least when things are working properly), the arrangement is sometimes called a "rocking chair" cell: the ions rock back and forth between two hosts. The voltage of the cell comes from the difference in how strongly each host material holds on to lithium. A cathode that holds lithium tightly and an anode that holds it loosely give a high voltage.

### The solid electrolyte interphase

There is one more ingredient, and it is so important that it deserves its own heading. The electrolyte, typically lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF~6~) dissolved in a mixture of organic carbonate solvents, is not stable at the very low potential of a charged graphite anode. On the first charge, a thin layer of electrolyte decomposes on the graphite surface and forms a film a few tens of nanometres thick. This *solid electrolyte interphase* (SEI) lets lithium ions through but blocks electrons, so once it has formed it prevents further decomposition.

The SEI is a blessing and a curse. Without it the cell would consume its own electrolyte continuously. But forming it uses up lithium permanently, typically 5 to 10 % of the cell's initial lithium during the factory's *formation* process, which is a slow, carefully controlled first charge. And the SEI never stops growing entirely. Every month and every cycle it thickens a little, consuming a little more lithium and raising the cell's resistance. As we shall see, slow SEI growth is the main reason a well-treated battery loses capacity over its life.

### Layered oxides: NMC and NCA

The first commercial lithium-ion cells, launched in 1991, used lithium cobalt oxide (LCO). It has a high voltage and good energy density, but cobalt is expensive, its supply is concentrated in a few countries with serious ethical concerns over mining practices, and LCO becomes unstable if too much lithium is removed. It remains common in phones but is not used for traction.

Its descendants are the *nickel manganese cobalt* oxides (NMC, sometimes written NCM) and *nickel cobalt aluminium* oxide (NCA). They keep the layered crystal structure of LCO but replace most of the cobalt. The numbers after NMC describe the ratio of nickel to manganese to cobalt: NMC 111 has equal parts, NMC 622 has 60 % nickel, 20 % manganese and 20 % cobalt, and NMC 811 has 80 % nickel. Nickel raises capacity; manganese and cobalt stabilise the structure. Over the past decade the industry has pushed steadily towards higher nickel and lower cobalt to raise energy density and reduce cost, and nickel contents of 85 to 90 % are now in production.

The price of that higher energy is reduced stability. High-nickel cathodes release oxygen at lower temperatures when they decompose, which makes thermal runaway more violent, and they are more sensitive to moisture during manufacture and to high voltages during use. They also tend to age faster if held at high state of charge. Cell makers fight these problems with surface coatings, doping elements and "single-crystal" particles that resist cracking.

### Phosphates: LFP and LMFP

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) has a completely different, olivine crystal structure in which oxygen is held tightly by phosphorus. It is far more stable than the layered oxides: it releases little oxygen even when severely abused, and its cells go into thermal runaway at higher temperatures and with less violence. It contains no nickel or cobalt, so it is cheaper and its supply chain is less fraught. It lasts a long time, often several thousand full cycles.

Its drawbacks are a lower voltage, around 3.2 to 3.3 V, and a lower capacity, giving less energy per kilogram and per litre. For years this confined LFP to buses, stationary storage and Chinese domestic vehicles. Two developments changed that. First, pack designs that eliminate modules, which we discuss in Chapter 5, recovered much of the volumetric penalty at the pack level. Second, falling cell prices made cost per kilowatt-hour more important than ultimate energy density for most mass-market cars. LFP now has a large share of the global EV market, especially in standard-range models.

LFP has one awkward electrical property, which we will meet repeatedly. Its open-circuit voltage is remarkably flat over most of its state-of-charge range, as Figure 4.2 shows. That flatness is pleasant for the power electronics but makes it hard for the battery management system to estimate how full the battery is from its voltage.

![**Figure 4.2** Typical open-circuit voltage curves for LFP and NMC cells against graphite. The NMC curve slopes steadily, which helps state-of-charge estimation. The LFP curve is almost flat between about 20 % and 90 %, with a tiny voltage change across a large change in charge.](figures/fig4_2.png)

Adding manganese to the phosphate structure gives *lithium manganese iron phosphate* (LMFP), which raises the voltage by about 0.4 V for part of the discharge and therefore the energy density by perhaps 15 to 20 %, while keeping most of the safety benefits. It is beginning to enter production, often blended with a small proportion of NMC.

## Anodes, Electrolytes and Separators

### Graphite and silicon

Almost every traction cell uses graphite, natural or synthetic, as its negative electrode. Graphite can host one lithium atom for every six carbon atoms, giving a theoretical capacity of 372 mAh/g. It operates at a potential only about 0.1 V above that of lithium metal, which keeps the cell voltage high, and its layered structure changes volume by only about 10 % when filled with lithium.

Silicon can store roughly ten times more lithium per gram, with a theoretical capacity over 3 500 mAh/g. The catch is that it swells by around 300 % when fully lithiated. Pure silicon anodes crack, lose contact and wear out their SEI within a few dozen cycles. Today's commercial approach is to blend a few per cent to perhaps ten per cent of silicon, usually as silicon oxide or as nano-structured silicon-carbon composites, into the graphite. This raises the anode capacity meaningfully and adds several per cent to cell energy density. Higher silicon contents are an active area of development.

At the other extreme, *lithium titanate* (LTO) anodes operate at about 1.55 V against lithium. They cannot form an SEI in the normal way and cannot plate lithium, so they tolerate extremely fast charging and very low temperatures, and last tens of thousands of cycles. But the cell voltage falls to about 2.3 V and the energy density is low. LTO appears in some buses and specialist applications.

### Electrolyte and separator

The electrolyte must conduct lithium ions well over a wide temperature range, stay stable across the full voltage window, wet the electrode pores, and form a good SEI. Standard electrolytes are carbonate mixtures such as ethylene carbonate with dimethyl or ethyl methyl carbonate, plus LiPF~6~ salt and a cocktail of additives, each present at a percent or so, that tune SEI formation, high-voltage stability and gas generation. Additive packages are among cell makers' most closely guarded secrets.

The carbonate solvents are flammable, which is the root of most lithium-ion fire risk. They also become viscous in the cold, slowing ion transport, which is one reason cells perform poorly at low temperature.

The separator is a microporous polyolefin film, polyethylene or polypropylene, typically 10 to 20 μm thick, often coated with ceramic particles to improve its stability at high temperature. It must let ions through freely, keep the electrodes apart reliably, and ideally shut down by melting its pores closed if the cell overheats.

### Beyond lithium-ion: a brief look

Two families of next-generation cells attract enormous attention. *Solid-state* cells replace the liquid electrolyte with a solid ceramic, sulphide or polymer conductor, which promises improved safety and, if paired with a lithium-metal anode, much higher energy density. *Sodium-ion* cells use sodium instead of lithium, with a hard-carbon anode; they have lower energy density, typically 120 to 160 Wh/kg at cell level, but use abundant, cheap materials and work well in the cold. Sodium-ion cells entered vehicle production in the mid-2020s in small cars. We look at both more critically in Chapter 15.

## Cell Formats

The same chemistry can be packaged in three physical forms, shown in Figure 4.3, and the choice affects everything from cooling to manufacturing.

![**Figure 4.3** The three cell formats. Cylindrical cells wind the electrodes into a jelly roll inside a steel can; prismatic cells use a flattened jelly roll or a stack inside a rigid aluminium case; pouch cells stack or fold the electrodes inside a flexible laminated foil.](figures/fig4_3.png)

**Cylindrical cells** wind long strips of electrode and separator into a spiral "jelly roll" inside a steel can. They are made at extraordinary speed and with very consistent quality, the steel can contains internal pressure well and the format includes built-in safety vents and current interrupt devices. Their drawbacks are a poor packing density, since circles leave gaps, and a large number of cells and connections per pack. The familiar 18650 (18 mm diameter, 65 mm long) gave way to the 21700, and larger formats such as the 4680 (46 mm diameter, 80 mm long) and other 46-series sizes now aim to cut the cell count by a factor of five or more. Larger cylinders need "tabless" or multi-tab electrode designs so that current and heat do not have to travel the full length of a long electrode strip.

**Prismatic cells** place a flattened jelly roll or a stack of electrode sheets into a rigid rectangular aluminium case. They pack efficiently, they are mechanically robust and their large capacity, often 100 to 300 Ah, means few cells per pack. Very long, thin "blade" prismatic designs can act as structural members of the pack. They are harder to cool uniformly in the middle, and swelling must be contained by the pack structure.

**Pouch cells** stack or Z-fold the electrodes inside a flexible aluminium-polymer laminate. They have the best gravimetric energy density of the three, because there is no rigid can, and they cool well across their large flat faces. But they have no mechanical strength, they need external compression to perform well and to resist swelling, and their sealed edges can be a weak point.

None of the formats is clearly superior; each moves cost and complexity between the cell and the pack. Table 4.2 summarises the trade.

Table: **Table 4.2** Comparison of cell formats.

|:---|:---|:---|:---|

## Electrical Behaviour and Modelling

From the outside, a cell is a voltage source whose voltage depends on how full it is, with some internal resistance and some sluggishness. To design the pack and its management system we need a model of that behaviour that is good enough without being impossibly detailed.

### Open-circuit voltage and state of charge

If a cell is left to rest for long enough, typically an hour or more, its terminal voltage settles to the *open-circuit voltage* (OCV). The OCV is a property of the chemistry and depends mainly on the *state of charge* (SoC), the fraction of the cell's usable charge that remains. Figure 4.2 showed typical OCV curves. For NMC, the OCV runs from about 3.0 V empty to 4.2 V full; for LFP, from about 2.5 V to 3.65 V, though almost all of the useful range lies between 3.2 and 3.35 V.

The OCV also changes slightly with temperature, and many chemistries, LFP especially, show *hysteresis*: the OCV after charging is a little higher than after discharging to the same SoC. These small effects matter a great deal to the accuracy of SoC estimation, as Chapter 6 explains.

### Internal resistance and dynamics

When current flows, the terminal voltage departs from the OCV. Part of the change is immediate, caused by the ohmic resistance of the electrodes, current collectors, electrolyte and contacts. Part builds up over milliseconds to seconds as the electrochemical reactions at the particle surfaces respond, and part builds up over tens or hundreds of seconds as lithium concentration gradients develop inside the particles and the electrolyte.

The standard way to capture this is the *equivalent circuit model* (ECM) in Figure 4.4. An ideal voltage source representing the OCV is followed by a series resistance *R*~0~, for the instantaneous response, and one or more parallel resistor-capacitor pairs, for the slower responses. A model with two RC pairs captures most of the behaviour relevant to vehicle use.

![**Figure 4.4** (a) A second-order equivalent circuit model of a cell. (b) Response of the terminal voltage to a 10-second discharge pulse: an instant drop across R~0~, a gradual further sag as the RC elements charge, and a slow recovery after the pulse.](figures/fig4_4.png)

The terminal voltage under a constant discharge current *I* that starts at time zero, for a model with one RC pair, is:

$$ V(t) = V_{OCV} - I R_0 - I R_1 \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_1}\right), \qquad \tau_1 = R_1 C_1 $$ {4.1}

The parameters are not constants. Resistance rises sharply at low temperature, roughly doubling for every 15 to 20 °C drop below room temperature in many cells, and it rises at very low and very high SoC. It also grows as the cell ages. A practical BMS stores the parameters in look-up tables indexed by temperature and SoC, and adapts them over life.

::: example
**Worked Example 4.1 — Peak power from a cell model**

A 100 Ah NMC cell at 50 % SoC and 25 °C has OCV = 3.70 V, *R*~0~ = 1.2 mΩ, *R*~1~ = 0.8 mΩ and *τ*~1~ = 20 s. The minimum allowed terminal voltage is 2.8 V. What is the maximum current the cell can supply for a 10-second pulse, and what power does it deliver? What does that imply for a 96-cell series pack?

*Solution.* At *t* = 10 s, the RC term has reached 1 − e^−0.5^ = 0.393 of its final value. The effective resistance at the end of the pulse is:

*R*~eff~ = 1.2 + 0.8 × 0.393 = 1.515 mΩ.

For the voltage to fall no lower than 2.8 V at the end of the pulse:

*I*~max~ = (3.70 − 2.80) / 0.001515 = 594 A.

Power at the end of the pulse = 2.80 × 594 = 1.66 kW per cell. That is about a 6C pulse.

A 96s pack of these cells could deliver about 96 × 1.66 = 160 kW for 10 seconds at this condition. In practice the BMS would set a lower limit to leave margin for cell variation, ageing and the voltage drop in busbars and contactors. Note also that at −10 °C, where the resistances might be three times higher, the same calculation gives under 200 A per cell: cold batteries feel weak.
:::

### Heat generation

The current flowing through the internal resistance generates heat. To a good approximation, the heat generated in a cell is:

$$ \dot{Q} = I^2 R_{eff} + I\, T\, \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} $$ {4.2}

The first term is the irreversible Joule heating. The second, the *reversible* or *entropic* heat, arises because the chemical reactions themselves absorb or release heat; *∂U*/*∂T* is the entropic coefficient, typically a fraction of a millivolt per kelvin, and its sign depends on the SoC and the direction of current. At high currents the Joule term dominates. At low currents, the entropic term can be comparable and can even make a cell cool slightly during part of a charge.

::: example
**Worked Example 4.2 — Heat during rapid charging**

The cell from Example 4.1 is charged at 2C (200 A). Its effective resistance during charging is 1.5 mΩ, and at this SoC its entropic coefficient is −0.1 mV/K. Cell temperature is 30 °C. Estimate the heat generated and the heat for a 192-cell pack.

*Solution.* Joule heat: 200^2^ × 0.0015 = 60 W.

Entropic heat: the current is negative on charge by the usual convention, so *I T ∂U/∂T* = (−200) × 303 × (−0.0001) = +6.1 W. Total ≈ 66 W per cell.

For 192 cells: about 12.7 kW. The cooling system must remove this continuously during a fast charge, or the cells will heat rapidly. A 100 Ah NMC cell weighs around 1.5 kg with a specific heat of about 1 000 J/(kg·K), so without cooling it would warm at 66 / 1 500 ≈ 0.044 K/s, or 2.6 K per minute. A 20-minute fast charge would add over 50 K. This is why fast-charging capability is as much a thermal problem as an electrochemical one.
:::

## Energy, Power and the Ragone Trade-off

Cell designers can trade energy against power by changing electrode thickness. Thick electrodes hold more active material relative to the inactive foils and separators, so they store more energy per kilogram. But lithium ions must travel further through the thick, tortuous coating, so the cell has higher resistance and delivers less power, and it charges more slowly because its electrodes are easier to drive into lithium plating. Thin electrodes do the opposite.

The trade is usually shown on a *Ragone plot*, energy density against power density. For EVs, typical energy cells deliver around 2 to 4 kW/kg for short pulses with energy densities of 200 to 300 Wh/kg. Cells for hybrids, which store little energy but must absorb and deliver high power repeatedly, have much thinner electrodes and reach 5 to 10 kW/kg at perhaps 80 to 120 Wh/kg.

When people talk about "energy density" they also need to say *where*. Cell-level figures are always higher than pack-level figures, because the pack adds structure, cooling, wiring and electronics. The ratio of pack energy density to cell energy density, sometimes called the *cell-to-pack ratio* or *integration efficiency*, has risen from about 50 to 60 % for early module-based packs to 70 to 80 % or more for the latest module-less designs. Chapter 5 explains how.

## Why Cells Age

Batteries do not fail suddenly in normal use; they fade. Capacity drops, resistance rises and, eventually, the vehicle's range and power fall below what the owner will accept. Warranties usually guarantee around 70 % of original capacity after eight years and a stated mileage. Most well-designed packs comfortably exceed that, but the margin depends heavily on how the cells are treated. Understanding the mechanisms tells you how to treat them.

Engineers separate ageing into two kinds. *Calendar ageing* happens with time even if the battery is not used. *Cycle ageing* is caused by charging and discharging. In real vehicles both happen together.

### The main mechanisms

- **SEI growth.** The SEI slowly thickens, consuming lithium and electrolyte and raising resistance. It is faster at high temperature and high SoC, because the anode is then at its lowest potential and most reactive. This is the dominant mechanism of calendar ageing.
- **Lithium plating.** If the anode is charged too quickly, or when it is cold, lithium ions arrive at the graphite surface faster than they can slip between its layers. They deposit instead as metallic lithium on the surface. Some of this lithium never comes back, and in the worst case it grows into needle-like dendrites that can pierce the separator. Plating is the main reason fast charging must be limited at low temperature.
- **Particle cracking.** Active particles expand and contract as lithium enters and leaves. Over many cycles they crack, exposing fresh surfaces on which new SEI forms, and fragments can lose electrical contact. High-nickel cathodes and silicon anodes are especially prone.
- **Cathode degradation.** The cathode surface can transform into less active structures, especially at high voltage, and transition metals can dissolve into the electrolyte, migrate to the anode and poison the SEI.
- **Electrolyte decomposition and gassing.** Side reactions consume the electrolyte and produce gas, which can swell pouch cells and raise internal pressure in hard-cased ones.

These mechanisms reduce capacity through two routes: *loss of lithium inventory*, where lithium becomes trapped in the SEI or plated metal and can no longer shuttle, and *loss of active material*, where electrode material becomes inaccessible. Each leaves distinct fingerprints in the cell's voltage curve, which advanced diagnostics can read.

### Stress factors

Figure 4.5 shows schematically how the main stress factors affect fade. Four of them dominate.

![**Figure 4.5** Indicative capacity fade over cycles under different conditions. High temperature and high average state of charge accelerate calendar ageing; fast charging at low temperature risks lithium plating and an accelerated "knee" in the curve.](figures/fig4_5.png)

*Temperature.* Chemical reaction rates follow the Arrhenius law, rising exponentially with temperature:

$$ k = A\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R\,T}\right) $$ {4.3}

where *E*~a~ is the activation energy, *R* = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant and *T* is the absolute temperature. For SEI growth, activation energies of the order of 50 kJ/mol are typical, which means the rate roughly doubles or trebles for every 15 °C rise. Batteries in hot climates age faster, and a battery parked in the sun at 100 % charge is having a bad time.

*State of charge.* Storing at high SoC accelerates calendar ageing, especially for NMC and NCA. Most manufacturers recommend daily charging to 80 or 90 % for nickel-based cells and full charges only before long trips. LFP cells are more tolerant of high SoC, and some manufacturers recommend charging them to 100 % regularly, partly to help the BMS recalibrate its SoC estimate on the flat LFP curve.

*Depth of discharge.* Deep cycles cause more mechanical stress than shallow ones. Many small cycles around the middle of the SoC range age a cell less than the same total charge throughput in full cycles.

*Charging rate and temperature.* Fast charging is not harmful in itself if the cell is warm and the charge rate is kept within the limits where plating cannot occur. Fast charging a cold battery is where the real damage happens.

::: example
**Worked Example 4.3 — The cost of heat**

Assume the calendar ageing rate of a cell follows Equation 4.3 with *E*~a~ = 50 kJ/mol. How much faster does it age when stored at 40 °C than at 25 °C?

*Solution.* Convert to kelvin: 298.15 K and 313.15 K. The ratio of rates is:

*k*~40~ / *k*~25~ = exp[(*E*~a~/*R*)(1/298.15 − 1/313.15)]
= exp[(50 000 / 8.314) × (0.003354 − 0.003193)]
= exp(6 014 × 0.0001607) = exp(0.966) ≈ 2.6.

The cell ages about 2.6 times faster at 40 °C. A pack that loses 2 % of its capacity per year to calendar ageing in a temperate climate might lose more than 5 % per year if kept constantly at 40 °C. This is why active cooling, even when parked in hot climates, can pay for itself in warranty savings.
:::

### The knee

Capacity fade is usually roughly linear, or slows a little over time, for most of a cell's life. But many cells eventually reach a *knee*, after which fade accelerates sharply. Knees are typically caused by lithium plating becoming self-reinforcing, by the electrolyte running low, or by the anode running out of spare capacity. A knee late in life is acceptable; one that arrives within the warranty period is a disaster. Predicting the knee from early test data is one of the hardest problems in battery engineering, and a major focus of research using physics-based models and machine learning.

## How Cells Fail Dangerously

A healthy lithium-ion cell is very safe. The danger comes when it is overheated, overcharged, crushed, penetrated or internally short-circuited. Then it can enter *thermal runaway*: a self-sustaining chain of exothermic reactions that heats the cell to many hundreds of degrees within seconds and vents hot, flammable and toxic gases.

The chain typically proceeds through recognisable stages as temperature rises:

1. Around 80 to 120 °C, the SEI begins to break down, exposing the lithiated anode to the electrolyte, and the resulting reactions release heat.
2. Around 130 to 170 °C, the separator softens and melts. If it shrinks enough, the electrodes touch and a large internal short circuit forms, releasing electrical energy as heat.
3. Above roughly 150 to 250 °C, depending strongly on chemistry, layered oxide cathodes decompose and release oxygen, which reacts with the electrolyte. This is the step that turns a hot cell into a violent one. LFP releases far less oxygen and does so at higher temperatures, which is why it is so much safer.
4. The flammable electrolyte vaporises and vents, and if it finds an ignition source it burns outside the cell.

A cell in runaway can reach 600 to 900 °C for nickel-based chemistries. The real danger in a pack is *propagation*: the heat from one failed cell triggering its neighbours, and so on until the whole pack is involved. Preventing propagation, or at least delaying it long enough for occupants to escape, is one of the main tasks of pack design, as Chapter 5 explains. Many regulations now require that a single-cell thermal runaway does not lead to fire or explosion outside the pack, or that occupants get adequate warning, typically five minutes, before hazardous conditions reach the cabin.

### Internal short circuits

The most worrying failure is an internal short circuit that develops without any external cause, typically from a metallic particle left in the cell during manufacture, a burr on an electrode edge or a dendrite. These are rare, of the order of one in tens of millions of cells for good manufacturers, but a large fleet contains billions of cells. That is why manufacturing cleanliness is so important, why cells undergo long ageing and self-discharge screening before shipment, and why BMS algorithms look for cells whose voltage drifts down faster than their neighbours.

::: problem
**Field Problem — One cell keeps falling behind**

*Symptom.* The BMS repeatedly flags a single cell group whose voltage is a few millivolts lower than the rest after the vehicle has rested overnight, and the balancing system is working hard on it.

*Likely causes.* (1) Higher self-discharge in that cell, possibly the early sign of a micro short circuit. (2) A cell with lower capacity from manufacturing variation, which sits at a different SoC for the same charge. (3) A faulty voltage-sensing connection or measurement channel. (4) A local temperature difference making the cell age faster.

*How to tell them apart.* Track the voltage difference over several days of rest. A steadily growing deficit during rest that the balancing has to recover each time indicates self-discharge, and its rate in mA can be estimated from the balancing charge. A difference that appears mainly at the ends of the SoC range, but not at rest in the middle, suggests a capacity difference. A difference that jumps or flickers suggests a measurement fault. A self-discharging cell is a potential safety issue and should be investigated, not simply balanced.
:::

::: casefile
**Case File 4 — The taxis that wore out their batteries**

*The complaint.* A city taxi operator running battery electric cars reports that a third of its fleet has lost more than 20 % of battery capacity in under three years, while private owners of the same model typically report around 8 % over the same period.

*State it in numbers.* Fleet vehicles average 90 000 km per year. Battery health readings from the operator's data show a median of 76 % state of health at 30 months, with the worst vehicles at 68 %. Private cars of the same age and similar cumulative energy throughput, collected from a sample of high-mileage owners, average 86 %.

*Draw the boundary.* The cells themselves (are the taxi batteries from a different production batch?), the usage pattern (charging rate, SoC window, temperature) and the BMS limits.

*Simplest model.* Cycle-count alone does not explain the gap, because the high-mileage private cars have done a similar number of equivalent full cycles. So the difference lies in how the cycles are done. The two candidates most likely to cause a gap of this size are temperature and charging conditions.

*Measure.* Charging logs show that the taxis charge almost exclusively at public rapid chargers, typically three times a day, between shifts, often starting when the battery is still cold after short urban trips in winter. Battery temperature at the start of rapid charging is below 10 °C in 40 % of winter sessions. The private cars rapid-charge rarely and mostly charge slowly at home overnight.

*Dominant term.* Fast charging at low cell temperature, very likely causing lithium plating. Teardown of two returned packs confirms grey metallic deposits on the anode surfaces of cells near the cold edges of the pack.

*Fix and cross-check.* The manufacturer issues a software update that preconditions the battery automatically when the driver navigates to a rapid charger, and lowers the permitted charge current when the cell temperature is below 15 °C. Charging time in winter rises by about six minutes per session, which the operator dislikes, so the manufacturer also adds a depot preconditioning mode using grid power. Capacity loss in the next cohort of taxis falls to about 10 % at 30 months.

*Lesson.* "Number of cycles" is not a stress factor in its own right. Temperature during charging is. A battery's history matters more than its mileage.
:::

### Chapter Summary

- A lithium-ion cell shuttles lithium ions between two intercalation hosts through an electrolyte, while electrons flow through the external circuit. The SEI layer on the anode makes this possible and slowly consumes lithium over life.
- Cathode choice sets voltage, energy, cost and safety. High-nickel NMC and NCA give the highest energy; LFP is cheaper, longer-lived and much safer, but has lower energy and a flat voltage curve. LMFP sits between them.
- Graphite dominates anodes; small additions of silicon raise energy density; LTO offers extreme life and fast charging at low energy.
- Cylindrical, prismatic and pouch formats move cost and complexity between the cell and the pack; none is universally superior.
- A cell behaves electrically like an OCV source with series resistance and RC elements; parameters vary strongly with temperature, SoC and age. Heat generation is dominated by *I*^2^*R*.
- Ageing comes from SEI growth, lithium plating, particle cracking and cathode degradation. Heat, high SoC, deep cycling and fast charging in the cold are the main stresses.
- Thermal runaway is a chain of exothermic reactions; nickel-based cathodes release oxygen and burn more violently than LFP. Pack design must prevent propagation from cell to cell.

### Problems

1. A 21700 cylindrical cell has a capacity of 5.0 Ah, a nominal voltage of 3.6 V and a mass of 70 g. Calculate its gravimetric energy density. How many such cells are needed for a 75 kWh pack?
2. Why does LFP have a lower cell voltage than NMC? Explain in terms of how strongly each cathode holds lithium.
3. A pack of LFP cells (3.2 V nominal, 280 Ah) must provide about 60 kWh. Design a series-parallel arrangement and state the nominal pack voltage. Compare the number of cells with that needed using 21700 NMC cells from Problem 1.
4. Using the model from Worked Example 4.1, calculate the 10-second pulse current at −10 °C if all resistances are three times higher and the OCV is 3.68 V.
5. A cell is discharged at a constant 150 A. Using Equation 4.1 with OCV = 3.65 V, *R*~0~ = 1.0 mΩ, *R*~1~ = 0.6 mΩ and *τ*~1~ = 15 s, calculate the terminal voltage immediately after the current starts, at 15 s, and as *t* tends to infinity.
6. Explain why a graphite anode with a little silicon added can raise cell energy density even though the silicon makes up only a few per cent of the anode mass.
7. With *E*~a~ = 45 kJ/mol, by what factor does the ageing rate change between 20 °C and 35 °C? Between 20 °C and 0 °C?
8. A battery has 2.5 % per year calendar fade at 25 °C and a further 0.004 % fade per equivalent full cycle. Estimate the state of health after eight years for a vehicle that completes 250 equivalent full cycles per year, assuming the effects simply add. What would change if the car lived in a climate averaging 35 °C, using the activation energy from Worked Example 4.3?
9. List the stages of thermal runaway in a high-nickel NMC cell and explain why LFP behaves differently at stage 3.
10. A fleet manager asks you whether rapid charging "ruins batteries". Write a 150-word answer that a non-engineer could understand, based on the evidence in this chapter.

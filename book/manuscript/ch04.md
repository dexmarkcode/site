When I was a student, a lecturer once projected a circuit diagram onto the screen and asked us how many nodes it had. Half the room said seven. A quarter said four. The rest kept their heads down. The correct answer was four, and the reason most of us got it wrong was not that the circuit was difficult. It was that nobody had ever taught us how to *read* a circuit diagram properly. We had been looking at the drawing instead of at the connections.

A circuit diagram is a language. Like any language it has a vocabulary (the symbols), a grammar (the rules about what connects to what) and conventions that experienced speakers take for granted. Before we can apply Kirchhoff's laws or write nodal equations, we need to be fluent in that language. This chapter is your phrasebook. It is perhaps the least mathematical chapter in the book, but I would argue it is one of the most important, because almost every mistake I see in mesh and nodal analysis begins with a misreading of the diagram.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- recognise the standard circuit symbols used in the UK and internationally;
- explain the behaviour of ideal voltage sources and ideal current sources, and how real sources differ;
- identify nodes, branches, loops and meshes in any circuit;
- recognise when two differently drawn circuits are actually the same;
- use a reference node and double-subscript notation for voltages;
- apply the passive sign convention to decide whether a component absorbs or delivers power;
- spot components that are in series or in parallel.
:::

## 4.1 Schematics, Not Pictures

A **circuit diagram**, or **schematic**, shows what is connected to what. It does not show where components physically sit, how big they are, what colour the wires are or how long they run. A wire on a schematic might be five millimetres of copper track on a circuit board or fifty metres of cable under a floor; the diagram treats both identically.

This abstraction is what makes analysis possible. By stripping away everything except the electrical connections, a schematic lets us focus on the only things that matter for calculating currents and voltages. But it also means you must learn to read the diagram on its own terms and not be misled by its layout. Two schematics that look completely different can describe exactly the same circuit, and two that look almost identical can behave entirely differently because of a single dot.

Engineers also use other kinds of drawing. A **wiring diagram** shows physical cable routes and terminal positions, and is used by installers. A **circuit board layout** shows the actual copper tracks. A **block diagram** shows the main functional parts of a system as labelled boxes. Each has its use, but for analysis the schematic is king.

## 4.2 The Vocabulary: Circuit Symbols

Figure 4.1 shows the symbols you will meet in this book, together with a few others that you will see in practice.

![Figure 4.1 — Common circuit symbols. This book follows the IEC conventions used in the UK and Europe. American texts use a zigzag for the resistor and sometimes different symbols for sources.](fig/ch04_symbols.svg){width=100%}

A few notes will help.

- **Cell and battery.** Strictly, a *cell* is a single electrochemical unit, and a *battery* is several cells connected together, although everyday speech uses "battery" for both. The longer line represents the positive terminal.
- **Voltage source.** The circle with + and − is a general voltage source. It might represent a battery, a bench power supply or the output of some other circuit. Its value is written beside it.
- **Current source.** The circle with an arrow is a current source, which we will discuss in detail shortly. The arrow shows the direction of the current it delivers.
- **Dependent source.** The diamond shapes represent sources whose value depends on a voltage or current somewhere else in the circuit. They are how we model transistors and amplifiers, and we will meet them properly in Chapter 12.
- **Ground.** The ground symbol marks the reference point from which other voltages are measured. Every point on a diagram that carries a ground symbol is connected together, even though no wire is drawn between them.
- **Wires and junctions.** A plain line is an ideal wire with zero resistance. Where wires join, a **dot** marks the junction. Where two lines simply cross without a dot, modern practice is that they are *not* connected. To avoid any doubt, many engineers avoid four-way junctions altogether and stagger them into two T-junctions.

::: tip
**In Practice: The Dot Matters**

If you are ever unsure whether two crossing lines on a diagram are connected, look for a dot. No dot, no connection. When you draw your own diagrams, always mark junctions with a clear dot and never draw a four-way junction. It is a small courtesy that prevents large mistakes.
:::

## 4.3 Ideal Voltage Sources

An **ideal voltage source** maintains a fixed voltage between its terminals *regardless of the current drawn from it*. Connect a 12 V ideal source to a 1 kΩ resistor and it delivers 12 mA at 12 V. Connect it to a 1 Ω resistor and it delivers 12 A, still at exactly 12 V. Connect it to a 0.001 Ω resistor and it delivers 12 000 A, still at 12 V.

No real source can do this. As we saw in Chapter 3, a real battery has internal resistance, so its terminal voltage falls as more current is drawn. The left-hand graph in Figure 4.2 shows the difference. The ideal source's terminal voltage is a horizontal line; the real source's line slopes down, and the slope is its internal resistance.

![Figure 4.2 — Characteristics of ideal and real sources. Left: an ideal voltage source keeps its voltage constant whatever current is drawn; a real one sags. Right: an ideal current source keeps its current constant whatever voltage appears across it; a real one falls away.](fig/ch04_source_graphs.svg){width=100%}

Why use an idealisation that cannot exist? Because it is *useful*. A good bench power supply, a car battery delivering modest current or the regulated 5 V rail in a computer are all very close to ideal over their normal working range. And when internal resistance does matter, we simply add it as a separate resistor in series with an ideal source, exactly as we did in Figure 3.5. The ideal source becomes a building block from which we can model real behaviour as accurately as we wish.

Two consequences of the definition will matter in later chapters:

1. **The current through an ideal voltage source is set by the rest of the circuit**, not by the source itself. A voltage source fixes voltage; the current is whatever the connected components demand.
2. **You must never place a wire directly across an ideal voltage source.** A short circuit across a fixed voltage implies infinite current, which is a mathematical contradiction. In real life, it means melted wires.

## 4.4 Ideal Current Sources

An **ideal current source** is the mirror image. It delivers a fixed current *regardless of the voltage across its terminals*. Connect a 2 A ideal current source to a 1 Ω resistor and the voltage across it settles at 2 V. Connect it to a 100 Ω resistor and the voltage rises to 200 V, so that the same 2 A still flows.

Current sources can seem strange at first, because the devices we handle every day – batteries and mains sockets – behave like voltage sources. But current sources are common in electronics:

- **LED drivers** in torches, car lamps and room lighting deliver a controlled current, because an LED's brightness depends on current rather than voltage.
- A **solar cell**, operating well below its open-circuit voltage, behaves very much like a current source whose value depends on the light falling on it.
- **Transistors** in their normal operating region behave like current sources controlled by an input signal, which is why we model them with dependent current sources.
- **Battery chargers** typically charge at a constant current for the first part of the charging cycle.

As with voltage sources, a real current source falls short of the ideal. It behaves like an ideal current source with a large resistance in **parallel**, so that some of its current leaks away internally when the voltage across it rises. The right-hand graph of Figure 4.2 shows the effect.

The two consequences of the definition are again mirror images of those for the voltage source:

1. **The voltage across an ideal current source is set by the rest of the circuit**, not by the source itself.
2. **You must never leave an ideal current source open-circuited.** Forcing a fixed current through an infinite resistance implies infinite voltage. Real current sources, such as the coil in a car's ignition system, will produce very high voltages if their circuit is interrupted, which is precisely how a spark plug fires.

::: tip
**A Memory Aid**

A voltage source decides the **voltage** and lets the circuit decide the current. A current source decides the **current** and lets the circuit decide the voltage. Never short a voltage source; never open a current source.
:::

## 4.5 The Grammar: Nodes, Branches, Loops and Meshes

To analyse a circuit systematically we need a few precise words for its structure. This part of the subject is called **circuit topology**, and it is the key to reading diagrams correctly.

**Branch.** A branch is a single two-terminal element: a resistor, a source, a lamp. A circuit with six components has six branches. (Some books count a chain of elements carrying the same current as one branch; I will point it out when the distinction matters.)

**Node.** A node is a point where two or more branches connect. Crucially, **a node includes all the wire connected to it**. Because a wire on a schematic has zero resistance, every point along it is at the same voltage, and the whole length of wire counts as a single node, however many corners it turns and however many dots appear on it. This is the idea that tripped up half of my lecture room.

**Essential node.** A node where three or more branches meet. These are the nodes where current genuinely divides, and they are the ones that matter most in nodal analysis.

**Loop.** A loop is any closed path through the circuit that starts at a node, passes through a sequence of branches and returns to the starting node without passing through any node twice.

**Mesh.** A mesh is a loop that does not contain any other loop inside it. If you imagine the circuit drawn flat on the page, the meshes are like the panes of a window. Meshes are the basis of mesh analysis in Chapter 11.

Figure 4.3 puts these ideas together.

![Figure 4.3 — A circuit with its nodes and meshes marked. The bottom wire, however long, is a single node (C). This circuit has six branches, four nodes (three of them essential) and three meshes.](fig/ch04_topology.svg){width=96%}

::: example
**Worked Example 4.1 — Counting the parts of a circuit**

For the circuit in Figure 4.3, count the branches, nodes, essential nodes and meshes.

**Branches.** There are six elements: the 10 V source, R~1~, R~2~, R~3~, R~4~ and the 2 A current source. So b = 6.

**Nodes.** Work along the wires and collect every point that is joined by plain wire.

- The top-left corner joins the + terminal of the voltage source to R~1~. That is one node (call it N).
- A joins R~1~, R~2~ and R~3~.
- B joins R~3~, R~4~ and the current source. Note that the wire running right from B to the top of the current source is part of node B.
- The whole bottom wire joins the voltage source, R~2~, R~4~ and the current source. It is all one node, C, even though it has two dots on it.

So n = 4.

**Essential nodes.** A has three branches, B has three and C has four. N has only two. So there are three essential nodes.

**Meshes.** Looking at the "window panes": the left pane bounded by the source, R~1~ and R~2~; the middle pane bounded by R~2~, R~3~ and R~4~; and the right pane bounded by R~4~ and the current source. So there are three meshes.
:::

There is a neat relationship between these numbers, which holds for any connected circuit that can be drawn flat on paper without crossing wires:

::: keyeq
**Meshes, branches and nodes**

m = b − n + 1

where m is the number of meshes, b the number of branches and n the number of nodes.
:::

For Figure 4.3, m = 6 − 4 + 1 = 3, which agrees with our count. This relationship tells you how many independent equations you will need in mesh analysis, and it is a good check that you have counted correctly.

## 4.6 Same Circuit, Different Drawing

Because a schematic shows only connections, the same circuit can be drawn in many different ways. Learning to see through the drawing to the underlying connections is a vital skill, especially when you need to redraw a messy circuit into a tidy form before analysing it.

Look at the two diagrams in Figure 4.4. At first glance they look different: in (a) both resistors stand vertically side by side, while in (b) R~1~ is drawn horizontally in the middle of the circuit. Now trace the connections. In both diagrams, one end of R~1~ connects to the top wire, which is the + terminal of the source, and the other end connects to the bottom wire, which is the − terminal. The same is true of R~2~. Electrically, the two diagrams are identical: both resistors are connected directly across the source.

![Figure 4.4 — The same circuit drawn two ways. In both (a) and (b), each resistor connects between the top node and the bottom node. Tracing connections, rather than looking at shapes, reveals that they are identical.](fig/ch04_redraw.svg){width=92%}

Here is a reliable method for checking whether two diagrams represent the same circuit, or for redrawing a confusing one:

1. **Label every node** with a letter, remembering that all connected wire is a single node.
2. **List each component** with the two nodes it connects between: for example, "R~1~: top–bottom; R~2~: top–bottom".
3. **Compare the lists.** If they match, the circuits are the same, however different the drawings look.
4. To redraw, place the nodes as neat horizontal lines or points on fresh paper, then draw each component between the nodes your list says it connects.

This node-listing method is exactly what circuit simulation programs do internally. A SPICE "netlist" is nothing more than a list of components and the nodes they connect, and it is a powerful way to think.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfall: Being Fooled by Layout**

Students often assume that components drawn side by side are in parallel, or that components drawn in a line are in series. Neither is necessarily true. Two resistors drawn far apart can be in parallel, and two drawn next to each other can be in neither series nor parallel. Only the node connections decide.
:::

## 4.7 Reference Nodes and Voltage Notation

In Chapter 1 we saw that voltage is always measured between two points. In a circuit with many nodes, writing "the voltage between node A and node C" every time becomes tiresome. Engineers solve this by choosing one node as the **reference node**, giving it a voltage of zero, and describing every other node's voltage relative to it.

The reference node is marked with the ground symbol. In mains equipment it may genuinely be connected to the earth; in a battery-powered gadget it is just the negative terminal of the battery, or whichever node is most convenient. The choice does not change any voltage *difference* in the circuit, only the numbers we write for individual node voltages – just as measuring heights from sea level or from the floor of a building changes the numbers but not the height of a staircase.

With a reference chosen, we write **V~A~** to mean "the voltage at node A relative to the reference". For the voltage between any two nodes we use **double-subscript notation**:

::: keyeq
**Double-subscript notation**

V~AB~ = V~A~ − V~B~

V~AB~ is the voltage at A measured relative to B. Note that V~BA~ = −V~AB~.
:::

The order of the subscripts matters. V~AB~ is positive if A is at a higher potential than B; V~BA~ is the same quantity with the opposite sign.

![Figure 4.5 — A circuit with the bottom node chosen as the reference (0 V). Node voltages V~A~ and V~B~ are measured relative to it.](fig/ch04_reference.svg){width=46%}

::: example
**Worked Example 4.2 — Node voltages and voltage differences**

In Figure 4.5, find V~A~, V~B~, V~AB~ and V~BA~.

**Step 1: V~A~.** Node A is connected directly to the + terminal of the 12 V source, whose − terminal is the reference. So V~A~ = 12 V.

**Step 2: Current.** The 4 Ω and 2 Ω resistors are in one loop with the source, so the same current flows through both. Total resistance is 6 Ω, so I = 12 / 6 = 2 A.

**Step 3: V~B~.** Node B is at the top of the 2 Ω resistor, whose bottom is the reference. V~B~ = I × 2 = 2 × 2 = 4 V.

**Step 4: Differences.** V~AB~ = V~A~ − V~B~ = 12 − 4 = 8 V. This is the voltage across the 4 Ω resistor, and indeed 2 A × 4 Ω = 8 V. ✓ And V~BA~ = −8 V.
:::

Node voltages are powerful because they make voltage differences easy. Once you know the voltage at every node relative to the reference, the voltage across *any* component is just a subtraction. This idea is the whole basis of nodal analysis in Chapter 10.

## 4.8 Reference Directions and the Passive Sign Convention

When we begin to analyse circuits, we will often not know in advance which way a current flows or which end of a component is more positive. That is not a problem. We simply **choose** a direction for each current and a polarity for each voltage, mark them on the diagram, and carry on. These choices are called **reference directions**.

If the answer comes out positive, the current really does flow in the direction we chose. If it comes out negative, it flows the other way, with the same magnitude. A current of −3 A flowing left is exactly the same as +3 A flowing right. **A negative answer is not a mistake**; it is information. This frees you from the worry of "guessing the direction right" before you start, and you should never waste time trying.

There is, however, one convention you must use consistently, because it controls the signs of power and of Ohm's law. It is called the **passive sign convention**.

::: keyeq
**The passive sign convention**

If the reference current arrow **enters** a component at the terminal marked **+**, then:

V = +IR for a resistor, and P = VI is the power **absorbed** by the component.
:::

![Figure 4.6 — The passive sign convention. The current arrow enters the component at its + terminal. With this labelling, Ohm's law has a positive sign and P = VI gives the power absorbed.](fig/ch04_psc.svg){width=64%}

If a component's labels follow this convention and P = VI turns out positive, the component is genuinely absorbing power. If P turns out negative, the component is actually *delivering* power to the rest of the circuit. A resistor always absorbs power, so any correct analysis will always give a positive P for every resistor. A source may go either way: a battery driving a torch delivers power; a battery on charge absorbs it.

::: example
**Worked Example 4.3 — Who absorbs and who delivers?**

Four components are labelled using the passive sign convention (current entering at +). Their measured values are:

- Component 1: V = 5 V, I = 2 A
- Component 2: V = 12 V, I = −3 A
- Component 3: V = −4 V, I = 1.5 A
- Component 4: V = 6 V, I = 1 A

Find the power for each, say whether it absorbs or delivers, and check that power balances.

**Solution.**

- P~1~ = 5 × 2 = +10 W: **absorbs** 10 W.
- P~2~ = 12 × (−3) = −36 W: **delivers** 36 W. (The negative current tells us the current actually leaves at the + terminal, which is what a source doing work looks like.)
- P~3~ = (−4) × 1.5 = −6 W: **delivers** 6 W.
- P~4~ = 6 × 1 = +6 W: **absorbs** 6 W.

**Balance check.** Total absorbed = 10 + 6 = 16 W. Total delivered = 36 + 6 = 42 W. These are *not* equal, so these four components cannot form a complete circuit on their own: something else must be absorbing the remaining 26 W. In a complete circuit the sum of P over every component, using the passive sign convention, must be exactly zero.
:::

That last point is one of the most valuable checks in circuit analysis. **In any complete circuit, the total power delivered equals the total power absorbed.** After a long mesh or nodal calculation, a quick power balance will tell you whether you have made an error.

## 4.9 Series and Parallel: A First Look

Two arrangements of components are so common that they deserve names straight away. Chapter 6 explores them in depth; here we just learn to recognise them from the topology.

**Series.** Two components are in series if they are connected end to end at a node that has *nothing else* connected to it. Because there is nowhere else for current to go at that node, **components in series carry the same current**. In Figure 4.3, the 10 V source and R~1~ are in series, joined at node N.

**Parallel.** Two components are in parallel if they are connected between the *same pair of nodes*. Because they share both nodes, **components in parallel have the same voltage across them**. In Figure 4.4, R~1~ and R~2~ are in parallel with each other and with the source. In Figure 4.3, R~4~ and the current source are in parallel, both connected between B and C.

Many pairs of components are in neither series nor parallel. In Figure 4.3, R~1~ and R~3~ are not in series, because node A also connects to R~2~. They are not in parallel either, because they do not share two nodes. Recognising this is just as important as recognising series and parallel connections, because it tells you that simple combination rules will not be enough and a more powerful method is needed.

::: example
**Worked Example 4.4 — Spotting series and parallel**

In Figure 4.3, decide whether each pair is in series, in parallel or neither: (a) R~2~ and R~4~; (b) R~3~ and R~4~; (c) the 10 V source and R~2~.

**(a)** R~2~ connects A to C; R~4~ connects B to C. They share only one node, and C has other things connected to it. **Neither.**

**(b)** R~3~ connects A to B; R~4~ connects B to C. They share node B, but B also connects to the current source. **Neither.**

**(c)** The source connects N to C; R~2~ connects A to C. They share only node C. **Neither.** (The source is in series with R~1~, and that series pair is in parallel with R~2~, but the source alone is not.)
:::

That last observation is a hint of what is to come in Chapter 7: by grouping components step by step, larger circuits can often be simplified.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Counting each dot as a separate node.** All points joined by plain wire are one node.
- **Assuming crossing lines connect.** Without a dot, they do not.
- **Shorting a voltage source or opening a current source** in a diagram or in the laboratory.
- **Reversing double subscripts.** V~AB~ = V~A~ − V~B~, not the other way round.
- **Worrying about current direction.** Choose one and carry on; a negative answer simply means the opposite direction.
- **Judging series and parallel by appearance** rather than by node connections.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A **schematic** shows electrical connections only; layout and wire length are irrelevant.
- An **ideal voltage source** fixes its voltage; the circuit decides its current. A real one has series internal resistance. Never short-circuit one.
- An **ideal current source** fixes its current; the circuit decides its voltage. A real one has parallel internal resistance. Never open-circuit one.
- A **branch** is a single element; a **node** is a connection point including all wire attached to it; a **mesh** is a loop with no other loop inside it. For planar circuits, m = b − n + 1.
- The same circuit can be drawn many ways. **Label the nodes and list connections** to compare or redraw.
- A **reference node** (ground) is defined as 0 V. Node voltages are measured from it, and V~AB~ = V~A~ − V~B~.
- **Reference directions** are chosen freely; a negative answer means the opposite direction.
- Under the **passive sign convention** (current entering at +), P = VI is the power absorbed. In a complete circuit, power delivered equals power absorbed.
- Components in **series** share a node with nothing else attached and carry the same current. Components in **parallel** share both nodes and have the same voltage.

# Designing Motors for Torque, Efficiency and Heat

::: chapter-intro
Chapter 7 explained how traction motors make torque. This chapter is about what limits them. We look at the operating envelope and the voltage and current limits that shape it, at where the losses come from and how they spread across an efficiency map, at windings, steel, magnets and insulation, at how motors are cooled, and at the mechanical problems of high speed: rotor stress, bearing damage and noise. Throughout, the theme is trade-off, because every improvement in one direction costs something in another.
:::

A motor designer once told me that motor design is easy until you add heat. Without thermal limits, you could push as much current as you liked through a small machine and get as much torque as you wanted. With them, every design decision becomes a negotiation: more copper for less resistance, but less room for iron; thinner laminations for lower losses, but higher cost; more magnet for more torque, but more back-EMF at top speed and more money spent on rare earths; higher speed for a smaller motor, but more iron loss, more mechanical stress and more noise.

Good traction motors are the result of hundreds of such negotiations, guided by simulation and settled by testing. You do not need to be a motor designer to benefit from understanding them. If you specify motors, integrate them or diagnose them, knowing *why* a motor behaves as it does is the difference between asking good questions and accepting whatever you are told.

## The Operating Envelope

### Torque, power and the two limits

Chapter 2 introduced the characteristic shape of an electric drive: constant torque up to a base speed, then roughly constant power. We can now say where that shape comes from. Two limits act at once.

The **current limit** is set by the inverter's switches and by winding heating. The magnitude of the phase current cannot exceed some maximum *I*~max~. In the d–q reference frame of Chapter 7, that means:

$$ i_d^2 + i_q^2 \leq I_{max}^2 $$ {8.1}

which is a circle on a graph of *i*~q~ against *i*~d~.

The **voltage limit** is set by the DC-link voltage. The inverter can apply a phase voltage of no more than about *V*~DC~/√3 in peak terms using the modulation methods described in Chapter 11. The motor's terminal voltage depends on its back-EMF and on the voltage across its inductances, both of which grow in proportion to electrical speed. Neglecting resistance, the voltage limit is:

$$ (\omega_e L_q i_q)^2 + (\omega_e L_d i_d + \omega_e \psi_m)^2 \leq V_{max}^2 $$ {8.2}

which is an ellipse centred at *i*~d~ = −*ψ*~m~/*L*~d~, shrinking as speed rises. At low speed the ellipse is huge and only the current circle matters: the machine can deliver its full torque. Above base speed, the ellipse has shrunk enough to cut into the circle, and the controller must move the current towards negative *i*~d~ to stay inside both. That is field weakening. Torque falls, power stays roughly constant, and eventually, at very high speed, the ellipse becomes so small that power falls too.

![**Figure 8.1** Current and voltage limits in the d–q current plane. The current limit is a fixed circle; the voltage limit is an ellipse that shrinks as speed rises. At low speed (ω~1~) the full current circle is usable; at higher speeds (ω~2~, ω~3~) the operating point must move left, towards negative *i*~d~, to stay inside the ellipse.](figures/fig8_1.png)

The point *i*~d~ = −*ψ*~m~/*L*~d~ is called the *characteristic current*. If it lies inside the current circle, the machine can, in principle, keep producing power at any speed: its constant-power range is very wide. If it lies outside, power eventually collapses. Designers aim to put the characteristic current close to the current limit, which is one reason IPM machines, with their large reluctance torque, have such good field-weakening performance.

### Peak and continuous envelopes

Every motor has at least two envelopes. The *peak* envelope is what it can deliver for a few seconds to perhaps thirty seconds, limited by the inverter's current rating and by how quickly the windings and magnets heat up. The *continuous* envelope is what it can sustain indefinitely with the cooling system working normally, limited by steady-state temperatures. As Chapter 2 noted, continuous power is often 40 to 60 % of peak for passenger cars. For commercial vehicles, which spend long periods at high load, designers aim for a much higher continuous-to-peak ratio, and motors are correspondingly larger.

## Where the Losses Come From

The efficiency of a traction motor is high, but at 150 kW even 4 % loss is 6 kW of heat, more than a domestic electric shower. Understanding where that heat is generated tells you where to look when efficiency or temperature disappoints. The losses fall into five groups.

### Copper losses

Current flowing through the stator windings produces resistive loss:

$$ P_{cu} = 3\, I_{rms}^2\, R_{ph} $$ {8.3}

Two features of copper loss catch people out. The first is temperature: copper resistance rises by about 0.39 % per kelvin, so a winding at 150 °C has half again the resistance it had at 20 °C. The second is frequency. At high electrical frequencies, *skin effect* and *proximity effect* (the influence of neighbouring conductors' fields) push current towards parts of each conductor, raising the effective resistance above its DC value. In large rectangular conductors at kilohertz frequencies, these *AC losses* can double or triple the copper loss at high speed. That is the price of the hairpin windings described below.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.1 — Copper loss when hot**

A motor's phase resistance is 10 mΩ at 20 °C. During a long motorway climb, the winding reaches 150 °C and the phase current is 300 A RMS. Find the copper loss, ignoring AC effects, and compare it with the loss at 20 °C.

*Solution.* *R*~150~ = 10 × [1 + 0.0039 × 130] = 15.07 mΩ.

*P*~cu~ = 3 × 300^2^ × 0.01507 = 4.07 kW.

At 20 °C: 3 × 300^2^ × 0.010 = 2.70 kW.

Heating the winding by 130 K raises the copper loss by half. That extra loss heats the winding further, which is the positive feedback loop that makes thermal design of motors, like that of busbars, a matter of finding a stable operating temperature.
:::

### Iron losses

The stator and rotor cores experience alternating and rotating magnetic fields, and, as Chapter 3 explained, they lose energy through hysteresis and eddy currents. A widely used approximation, based on the work of Charles Steinmetz, separates the two:

$$ p_{Fe} = k_h\, f\, B^{\alpha} + k_e\, f^2 B^2 \quad \mathrm{(W/kg)} $$ {8.4}

where *f* is the frequency, *B* the peak flux density, and *k*~h~, *k*~e~ and *α* (often close to 2) are fitted to measured data for a particular steel. Hysteresis loss rises with frequency; eddy loss rises with its square. At the high electrical frequencies of modern traction motors, eddy loss can become dominant, which is why thinner laminations pay off.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.2 — Iron loss at two operating points**

For a 0.27 mm lamination steel, take *k*~h~ = 0.02 W/(kg·Hz·T^2^), *k*~e~ = 2 × 10^−5^ W/(kg·Hz^2^·T^2^) and *α* = 2. The stator core has a mass of 20 kg. Compare iron losses at (a) 400 Hz with a peak flux density of 1.5 T, typical of high torque at moderate speed, and (b) 1 000 Hz at 1.2 T, typical of high-speed cruising with field weakening.

*Solution.* (a) Hysteresis: 0.02 × 400 × 1.5^2^ = 18.0 W/kg. Eddy: 2 × 10^−5^ × 400^2^ × 1.5^2^ = 7.2 W/kg. Total 25.2 W/kg, or 504 W.

(b) Hysteresis: 0.02 × 1 000 × 1.44 = 28.8 W/kg. Eddy: 2 × 10^−5^ × 10^6^ × 1.44 = 28.8 W/kg. Total 57.6 W/kg, or 1 152 W.

Although the flux density is lower at high speed, the iron loss more than doubles. At motorway speed and light torque, iron loss can be the largest single loss in the machine, and it does not fall to zero when torque is zero. Real cores also suffer extra losses from harmonics and from the damage done to the steel at cut edges, so measured figures are often 1.5 to 2 times what Equation 8.4 predicts from catalogue data.
:::

### Magnet losses

The magnets in a PMSM are electrically conductive, and the harmonics in the air-gap field, from slotting and from the inverter's switching, induce eddy currents in them. The losses are small in watts but generated inside the rotor, where cooling is poor and where the magnets' temperature must be kept low to protect their coercivity. Designers *segment* magnets, splitting each into several insulated pieces along its length or width, to break up the eddy-current paths.

### Mechanical losses

Bearings, seals and air friction (*windage*) add losses that grow with speed. At 18 000 rpm, windage from a rotor spinning in air or oil mist is significant, and a rotor running in an oil-filled housing can lose surprising amounts to churning. Mechanical losses are often the dominant loss at high speed and zero torque.

### Stray and inverter-induced losses

The current from an inverter is not a perfect sine wave. Its switching ripple, at the switching frequency and its multiples, causes extra copper, iron and magnet losses. A lower switching frequency means a cheaper, more efficient inverter but more harmonic loss in the motor; a higher one does the reverse. This is a classic system-level trade that neither the inverter designer nor the motor designer can settle alone.

## Efficiency Maps

Plotting efficiency across the whole torque-speed range gives an *efficiency map*, the single most useful picture of a traction motor. Figure 8.2 shows a typical example for an IPM machine.

![**Figure 8.2** Efficiency map of a 150 kW IPM traction motor, with the peak torque envelope. The highest efficiency island sits at moderate torque and moderate-to-high speed. Efficiency falls at very low speed, where copper loss dominates relative to output, and at very light load and high speed, where iron and mechanical losses dominate. Dots show where a typical mixed drive cycle spends its time.](figures/fig8_2.png)

Three features are characteristic. At low speed and high torque, efficiency is limited by copper loss, which is large relative to the modest output power. At high speed and light torque, efficiency is limited by iron and mechanical losses, which persist even when little power is being delivered. Between them lies the *sweet spot*, where efficiency can exceed 96 or 97 %.

The dots in Figure 8.2 matter as much as the colours. Most real driving happens at low torque: on a level motorway at 120 km/h, a car might need only 15 to 25 kW from a 150 kW motor. So the efficiency that matters for range is not the peak value but the efficiency weighted by where the vehicle actually operates. A motor with a peak efficiency of 97 % but a sweet spot in the wrong place can use more energy over a drive cycle than one with a peak of 95 % and a sweet spot that matches the duty. Modern motor design optimises a *drive-cycle-weighted* efficiency, and the vehicle engineer can help by choosing the gear ratio to place the most common operating points where the motor is happiest.

### Measuring an efficiency map

Efficiency maps are measured on a dynamometer: the motor drives a load machine, and torque, speed, and electrical input power are measured at hundreds of points. The challenge is accuracy. At 96 % efficiency, an error of 0.5 % in input power and 0.5 % in torque can change the computed losses by 25 %. Precision power analysers, careful torque transducers and attention to temperature stabilisation at each point are essential. A good test lab treats loss measurement as a discipline in its own right.

## Windings

### Distributed and concentrated windings

Traction motor stators carry their windings in slots. In a *distributed* winding, each coil spans several slots, and the coils of each phase overlap with those of the others. This gives a smooth, nearly sinusoidal air-gap field, low harmonics and low torque ripple, but long end windings that add resistance, mass and axial length. In a *concentrated* winding, each coil is wound around a single tooth. End windings are short and manufacturing is simple, but the air-gap field is rich in harmonics, which cause extra rotor and magnet losses and noise. Most passenger-car traction motors use distributed windings; concentrated windings appear in some compact and axial-flux designs.

### Stranded and hairpin windings

A classic distributed winding uses many strands of round, enamelled copper wire, inserted into the slots in bundles. Round wires pack poorly, so only 40 to 50 % of the slot area is copper; the rest is insulation and air.

The *hairpin* winding, shown in Figure 8.3, replaces the bundles with solid rectangular copper bars, bent into a U shape like a hairpin, pushed into the slots from one end, and twisted and welded together at the other. Rectangular bars stacked in a rectangular slot achieve copper fill factors of 65 to 75 %, cutting DC resistance substantially. The bars are also stiffer and conduct heat better, and the process is easy to automate.

![**Figure 8.3** Slot cross-sections. (a) Stranded round-wire winding with a fill factor around 45 %. (b) Hairpin winding with four rectangular conductors per slot and a fill factor around 70 %. The lower conductors near the slot opening carry more AC loss because of the leakage field.](figures/fig8_3.png)

The catch is the AC loss mentioned earlier. A large solid conductor at kilohertz frequencies suffers from skin and proximity effects, especially the conductors nearest the slot opening, where the slot leakage field is strongest. Designers counter this by using more, thinner layers of conductors per slot (six or eight rather than four), transposing conductors between positions, or using a mix of conductor sizes. Hairpin windings have nonetheless become the standard for high-volume traction motors because their manufacturing and DC-loss advantages outweigh the AC penalty for most duty cycles.

### Insulation

The winding insulation system, enamel on the conductors, liner paper in the slots, phase separators and impregnating varnish or resin, sets the maximum winding temperature. Traction motors typically use thermal class H (180 °C) or higher materials, and design for hot-spot temperatures around 160 to 180 °C at peak load.

Insulation faces a second, electrical threat from the inverter. Every switching edge applies a steep voltage step to the motor terminals, and the long cable and the winding's own inductance and capacitance can cause *overshoot*, so the peak voltage at the motor can approach twice the DC-link voltage. If the local electric field across a small air gap in the insulation exceeds the breakdown strength of air, *partial discharge* occurs: tiny sparks that slowly erode the enamel. With 800 V systems and fast-switching silicon carbide devices, the *partial discharge inception voltage* of the winding has become a critical design parameter. Thicker enamel, void-free impregnation and controlling the rate of voltage change (*dv*/*dt*) at the inverter are the main countermeasures.

## Materials

**Electrical steel.** Non-oriented silicon steel, with about 3 % silicon to raise resistivity, is used for stator and rotor laminations. Grades are specified by thickness and by losses at a reference flux density and frequency. Traction motors have moved from 0.35 mm to 0.27 mm, 0.25 mm and even 0.20 mm laminations to cut eddy losses at high frequency. Thinner steel is more expensive and slower to stamp and stack, and its mechanical strength matters too, because the rotor laminations must resist the stresses discussed below.

**Magnets.** Sintered NdFeB magnets are graded by their remanence (how strong they are) and coercivity (how resistant to demagnetisation). The temperature at which they must still resist demagnetisation, often 150 to 180 °C for traction motors, determines how much dysprosium or terbium they contain. Ferrite magnets are far cheaper and contain no rare earths, but they are about a third as strong, so ferrite-based designs need very different geometries.

**Copper and aluminium.** Copper dominates windings for its conductivity. Aluminium windings are lighter and cheaper but need about 60 % more cross-section for the same resistance; they appear in some low-cost designs. Induction rotor cages use die-cast aluminium or, for higher efficiency, fabricated or die-cast copper.

## Cooling

The continuous rating of a motor is set almost entirely by how well its heat can be removed. Heat must flow from where it is generated, mainly the windings and the stator core, to a coolant, and every boundary in that path adds thermal resistance.

The standard approach is a **water jacket**: a spiral or axial channel in the aluminium housing around the stator, carrying the same water-glycol coolant used for the inverter. Heat from the windings must cross the slot insulation, the stator teeth and yoke, and the interface between stator and housing before reaching the coolant. The slot liner is often the worst step, since insulating materials are poor heat conductors.

**Oil cooling** is increasingly common. Oil from the gearbox is sprayed or dripped directly onto the end windings, or pumped through channels in the stator, bypassing much of the thermal resistance. Because the oil is a dielectric, it can touch live conductors. Some designs go further with *direct slot cooling*, in which oil flows through channels inside the slots alongside the conductors. These approaches raise the achievable current density and therefore the shear stress, which is how the most compact modern motors achieve their power densities.

The **rotor** is harder to cool. Its heat must cross the air gap, which is a poor conductor, or travel down the shaft. Hollow shafts with coolant or oil flowing through them, and oil sprayed from inside the shaft onto the rotor ends, help. Rotor temperature matters greatly in a PMSM because of the magnets, yet it is difficult to measure on a spinning part, so most drives *estimate* it from a thermal model running in the inverter software.

### Thermal time constants

Different parts of the motor heat at different rates. The copper windings have a small thermal mass relative to the heat they generate at peak current, so they heat quickly, over tens of seconds. The stator core and housing heat more slowly, over minutes. The rotor and its magnets are in between but have the worst cooling. This structure explains why peak power can be held only briefly, and why a motor that has been working hard for twenty minutes has less peak capability than a cold one.

![**Figure 8.4** Winding hot-spot temperature during repeated peak-power accelerations on a track, compared with steady motorway driving. Each acceleration adds a rapid temperature rise; between them, the winding cools only partly. Once the hot spot approaches its limit, the controller must reduce available power.](figures/fig8_4.png)

::: example
**Worked Example 8.3 — How long can peak power last?**

At peak torque, a motor's copper loss is 6 kW. The copper in the winding has a mass of 8 kg and a specific heat of 385 J/(kg·K). The winding starts at 90 °C and its hot-spot limit is 180 °C. Assuming, pessimistically, that no heat escapes during the peak (an adiabatic approximation), how long can peak torque be held?

*Solution.* Thermal capacity = 8 × 385 = 3 080 J/K.

Rate of temperature rise = 6 000 / 3 080 = 1.95 K/s.

Time to rise by 90 K = 90 / 1.95 = 46 s.

In reality, some heat flows into the stator core and the coolant, and copper loss rises as the winding heats, so the two errors partly cancel. Detailed thermal models give peak durations of 20 to 60 seconds for typical passenger-car motors, which matches this rough estimate. Note how sensitive the result is to the starting temperature: a winding already at 140 °C after a long climb would allow only 20 seconds.
:::

## Mechanical Limits at High Speed

### Rotor stress

A spinning rotor experiences centrifugal stress that rises with the square of speed. For a thin rotating ring of density *ρ* and radius *r* at angular speed *ω*, the hoop stress is:

$$ \sigma_{hoop} = \rho\, \omega^2 r^2 = \rho\, v^2 $$ {8.5}

where *v* is the surface speed. A solid disc experiences somewhat lower stresses, but the thin steel *bridges* that hold the magnets in an IPM rotor experience severe stress concentration, often two or three times the nominal value.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.4 — Rotor stress at 18 000 rpm**

An IPM rotor has an outer radius of 80 mm and is made of electrical steel with a density of 7 650 kg/m^3^. Estimate the nominal hoop stress at its outer surface at 18 000 rpm. If the bridges around the magnet pockets carry a stress concentration factor of 2.5, what stress do they see? The yield strength of the lamination steel is around 450 MPa.

*Solution.* *ω* = 18 000 × 2π / 60 = 1 885 rad/s. Surface speed *v* = 1 885 × 0.08 = 150.8 m/s.

*σ*~hoop~ = 7 650 × 150.8^2^ = 174 MPa (nominal).

Bridge stress ≈ 2.5 × 174 = 435 MPa.

That is very close to the yield strength, with the magnets' own mass adding further load. This is why high-speed IPM rotors need high-strength lamination grades, carefully shaped bridges and sometimes a carbon-fibre sleeve, and why a motor's maximum speed is usually a mechanical limit rather than an electrical one. Note also that thicker bridges, which would be stronger, carry more leakage flux and reduce torque: another trade.
:::

### Bearing currents

Inverters produce a *common-mode voltage*, a voltage between the motor windings' star point and the motor housing that jumps with each switching event. Through the small capacitances inside the motor, this voltage couples onto the rotor shaft. The shaft's only route to the grounded housing is through the bearings, across the thin film of lubricant between balls and races. When the voltage builds up enough to break down that film, a tiny spark jumps across. Millions of sparks over thousands of hours pit the races, producing a characteristic washboard pattern called *fluting*, and eventually the bearing becomes noisy and fails. This is *electrical discharge machining* (EDM) damage.

Fast-switching silicon carbide inverters and 800 V systems make the problem worse. Countermeasures include ceramic (hybrid) bearings, insulated bearing housings, *shaft grounding rings* of conductive fibres that give the current a harmless path, and inverter modulation strategies that reduce common-mode voltage. Case File 8 describes the problem in the field.

### Noise and vibration

Electric drives are quiet compared with engines, but their noise is tonal and high-pitched, so it is easily noticed in a silent cabin. Sources include *torque ripple*, the small fluctuations in torque as rotor and stator slots pass each other and as the current waveform departs from perfect sinusoids; *cogging torque*, the tendency of a PM rotor to prefer certain positions even with no current; *radial forces* on the stator teeth that make the housing ring like a bell; and *gear whine* from the reduction gearbox. Each produces tones at specific multiples, or *orders*, of rotational speed.

Designers fight these with careful choice of the number of slots and poles, *skewing* the rotor or stator (twisting the laminations slightly along the stack, or stepping the magnet segments) to smear out the slot effects, shaping the rotor surface, injecting compensating current harmonics through the control software, and stiffening the housing to move its resonances away from the excitation. The control engineer, the motor designer and the acoustic engineer have to work together; noise problems are among the most stubborn in drive-unit development.

::: problem
**Field Problem — High-pitched whine at one speed**

*Symptom.* Drivers report a noticeable whine at around 70 km/h under light acceleration, which disappears at higher or lower speeds.

*Likely causes.* (1) An electromagnetic order (for example the 48th order from slot–pole interaction) exciting a structural resonance of the housing or mounts at that speed. (2) Gear mesh order exciting a resonance. (3) Inverter switching-frequency sidebands. (4) A mount or bracket with a resonance close to the excitation frequency.

*How to tell them apart.* Record sound and vibration while sweeping slowly through the speed range, and plot as a colour map of frequency against speed (a Campbell diagram). An order line that brightens where it crosses a fixed-frequency band indicates resonance. Calculate which component produces that order: gear tooth counts identify gear orders; slot and pole numbers identify electromagnetic orders. Changing the switching frequency temporarily separates inverter-related noise. Fixes range from software harmonic injection to stiffening a bracket.
:::

::: casefile
**Case File 8 — Bearings that failed too soon**

*The complaint.* A delivery van fleet reports noisy rear drive units at 60 000 to 90 000 km, far below the expected life. Teardowns find the motor bearings' outer races covered with a regular grey washboard pattern.

*State it in numbers.* 2.1 % of drive units affected by 90 000 km, against a design target of less than 0.1 % at 250 000 km. Affected vans are those with a new silicon carbide inverter introduced eighteen months earlier; earlier vans with IGBT inverters show no such failures.

*Draw the boundary.* Bearing lubrication and load (mechanical causes), and the electrical environment of the shaft (electrical causes).

*Simplest model.* The washboard pattern is the classic sign of fluting from electrical discharge machining. The new inverter switches about five times faster than the old one. Faster voltage edges couple more current through the motor's internal capacitances, and the common-mode voltage contains more high-frequency energy. If shaft voltage regularly exceeds the breakdown voltage of the lubricant film, discharges will occur every few switching cycles.

*Measure.* Shaft-to-housing voltage measured with a high-bandwidth probe shows discharges of 12 to 18 V peak occurring tens of thousands of times per second, against 3 to 5 V on the old inverter. Bearing grease from failed units contains darkened, burnt residue.

*Dominant term.* Electrical discharge through the bearings caused by the faster-switching inverter.

*Fix and cross-check.* Production units receive a shaft grounding ring at the non-drive end and a hybrid ceramic-ball bearing at the drive end. The inverter's gate drive is adjusted to slow the switching edges slightly, which costs about 0.2 % efficiency at high load but halves the common-mode noise. Shaft voltage drops below 2 V. Accelerated endurance testing confirms no fluting after the equivalent of 300 000 km. The team checks that the grounding ring's wear life exceeds the vehicle's design life and that the slower edges still meet electromagnetic compatibility limits.

*Lesson.* An improvement in one component can create a failure in another. A faster switch is good for the inverter's efficiency but changes the electrical environment of everything it is connected to.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The operating envelope is set by a current limit (a circle in the d–q plane) and a voltage limit (an ellipse that shrinks with speed). Field weakening keeps the operating point inside both above base speed.
- Losses include copper (rising with temperature and, through AC effects, with frequency), iron (hysteresis ∝ *f*, eddy ∝ *f*^2^), magnet eddy currents, mechanical losses and inverter-induced harmonic losses.
- Efficiency maps show copper-limited low-speed regions, iron-limited light-load high-speed regions and a central sweet spot; drive-cycle-weighted efficiency is what determines range.
- Hairpin windings raise fill factor and cut DC resistance but add AC losses at high frequency. Insulation must withstand temperature and the partial discharge risk from fast inverter edges.
- Cooling sets continuous rating; oil cooling of windings raises power density. Windings heat in seconds, the core in minutes.
- High speed brings rotor stress (∝ *v*^2^), bearing damage from common-mode currents and tonal noise from torque ripple and structural resonance.
:::

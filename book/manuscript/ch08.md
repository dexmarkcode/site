A few years ago a final-year student came to my office convinced that her circuit board was faulty. She had built a simple voltage divider from two 1 MΩ resistors across a 10 V supply. Theory said the midpoint should sit at 5 V. Her digital multimeter read 4.76 V. She had replaced both resistors twice and checked the supply three times. Every reading came out the same, and she was beginning to doubt Ohm's law itself.

There was nothing wrong with her board, her resistors or Ohm's law. The "fault" was the multimeter. Its input resistance of 10 MΩ was in parallel with the lower resistor, and the act of measuring had changed the very voltage she was trying to measure. Once we calculated the effect, her 4.76 V reading turned out to be exactly right.

This chapter is about that effect, which engineers call **loading**. It explains why a voltage divider's output sags when you connect something to it, why measuring instruments are never perfectly invisible, and how meters are built to have the right resistance for their job. It also introduces one of the oldest and most elegant circuits in electrical measurement, the **Wheatstone bridge**, which still sits inside digital scales, pressure sensors and countless industrial instruments today.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- calculate the output of a voltage divider when a load is connected;
- choose divider resistor values to keep loading errors acceptably small;
- calculate the error introduced by a voltmeter or ammeter;
- design a shunt to extend an ammeter's range and a multiplier to make a voltmeter;
- state and derive the balance condition of a Wheatstone bridge;
- use a bridge to measure an unknown resistance or a small change in resistance;
- design simple resistive sensor circuits using dividers and bridges.
:::

## 8.1 The Loaded Voltage Divider

In Chapter 6 we derived the voltage divider rule on the assumption that the same current flows through both resistors. That is true only if nothing is connected to the output, or if whatever is connected draws negligible current. In real life the output of a divider is there to *feed* something – a sensor input, a transistor, a meter – and that something draws current. Figure 8.1 shows the situation.

![Figure 8.1 — A voltage divider with a load R~L~ connected to its output. Some of the current through R~1~ now flows into the load instead of through R~2~.](fig/ch08_loaded.svg){width=60%}

The load R~L~ is connected across R~2~, so the two are in **parallel**. The lower part of the divider is no longer R~2~ but the parallel combination R~2~ ∥ R~L~, which is always smaller than R~2~. So the output voltage falls.

::: keyeq
**The loaded voltage divider**

V~out~ = V~S~ × {{frac|R~2~ ∥ R~L~|R~1~ + (R~2~ ∥ R~L~)}}

where R~2~ ∥ R~L~ = R~2~R~L~ / (R~2~ + R~L~).
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 8.1 — How much does the output sag?**

In Figure 8.1, V~S~ = 12 V and R~1~ = R~2~ = 10 kΩ. Find V~out~ (a) with no load, (b) with R~L~ = 10 kΩ and (c) with R~L~ = 100 kΩ.

**(a) No load.** V~out~ = 12 × 10/20 = 6 V.

**(b) R~L~ = 10 kΩ.** R~2~ ∥ R~L~ = 10 ∥ 10 = 5 kΩ.

V~out~ = 12 × {{frac|5|10 + 5}} = 4 V

A third of the output has disappeared.

**(c) R~L~ = 100 kΩ.** R~2~ ∥ R~L~ = 10 × 100 / 110 = 9.09 kΩ.

V~out~ = 12 × {{frac|9.09|10 + 9.09}} = 5.71 V

That is 4.8 % below the unloaded value.
:::

Figure 8.2 shows how the output varies with load resistance for this divider. The message is clear: the load must be much larger than the divider's resistors if the output is to stay close to its design value.

![Figure 8.2 — Output of the 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider against load resistance. A load equal to R~2~ pulls the output down by a third; a load ten times R~2~ costs about 5 %.](fig/ch08_load_curve.svg){width=86%}

::: tip
**The Ten-Times Rule**

A useful rule of thumb: if the load resistance is at least **ten times** the resistance of the lower divider resistor, the output will be within about 5 % of its unloaded value. For 1 % accuracy you need roughly a hundred times. For serious precision, do the calculation, or use a buffer amplifier, which you will meet in a later course.
:::

### The designer's trade-off

If loading is a problem, why not make the divider resistors very small? Then even a modest load would be "much larger". The trouble is power. A divider made of two 100 Ω resistors across 12 V draws 60 mA continuously and wastes 0.72 W, even with nothing connected. In a battery-powered device that is unacceptable. A 2 × 100 kΩ divider wastes just 0.72 mW, but it can only feed loads of several megohms.

So the choice of divider resistance is always a compromise between **stiffness** (resistance to loading) and **efficiency**. A sensible approach is to make the current through the divider about ten times the current the load needs. That keeps the output steady without wasting more power than necessary.

### A glimpse of Thévenin

There is a neater way of looking at the loaded divider, which previews one of the great ideas of Chapter 14. Seen from its output terminals, an unloaded divider behaves exactly like a voltage source equal to its unloaded output, in series with a resistance equal to R~1~ ∥ R~2~.

For our 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider on 12 V, that means a 6 V source in series with 5 kΩ. Now connect a 10 kΩ load: by the ordinary divider rule, V~out~ = 6 × 10 / (5 + 10) = 4 V – the same answer as Worked Example 8.1(b), with far less work. Remember this. When you meet Thévenin's theorem you will see that it is not a trick special to dividers but a property of every linear circuit.

## 8.2 Measuring Without Disturbing: Meter Loading

Every measuring instrument draws some energy from the circuit it measures. A voltmeter needs a little current to make its reading; an ammeter needs a little voltage across it. The art of good measurement is to make those disturbances small enough not to matter – and to know when they do.

### Voltmeter loading

An ideal voltmeter would have infinite resistance, so that connecting it in parallel with a component would change nothing. A real voltmeter has a large but finite **input resistance**, R~m~. Modern digital multimeters typically have 10 MΩ on their voltage ranges. Old-fashioned analogue meters have much less, quoted as a **sensitivity** in ohms per volt, such as 20 kΩ/V; on the 10 V range, that meter has 20 kΩ/V × 10 V = 200 kΩ.

When you connect a voltmeter across a resistor, the meter's resistance goes in parallel with it, and you have created a loaded divider.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.2 — The student's divider**

Two 1 MΩ resistors form a divider across 10 V. The midpoint is measured with (a) a digital meter of 10 MΩ input resistance and (b) an analogue meter of 20 kΩ/V on its 10 V range. Find each reading.

**True value.** With nothing connected, V = 10 × 1/2 = 5 V.

**(a) Digital meter.** The lower resistor in parallel with the meter: 1 MΩ ∥ 10 MΩ = 10/11 MΩ = 0.909 MΩ.

V = 10 × {{frac|0.909|1 + 0.909}} = 4.76 V

The reading is 4.8 % low. This is the student's mystery solved.

**(b) Analogue meter.** R~m~ = 20 kΩ/V × 10 V = 200 kΩ = 0.2 MΩ. 1 MΩ ∥ 0.2 MΩ = 0.167 MΩ.

V = 10 × {{frac|0.167|1 + 0.167}} = 1.43 V

The meter reads less than a third of the true value!

**Lesson.** On low-resistance circuits, both meters are fine. On high-resistance circuits, even a good digital meter can mislead you, and an analogue meter can be wildly wrong. Always compare the meter's resistance with the resistance of the circuit at the point you are measuring.
:::

### Ammeter loading

An ideal ammeter would have zero resistance, so that inserting it into a circuit would change nothing. A real one has a small **burden resistance**. Inserting it adds that resistance in series and reduces the current.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.3 — An ammeter in a low-resistance circuit**

A 1.5 V cell (treat as ideal) drives a 10 Ω resistor. The current is measured with an ammeter of 0.5 Ω resistance. Find the true current, the measured current and the percentage error.

**Solution.** True current: 1.5 / 10 = 150 mA. With the ammeter in series: 1.5 / 10.5 = 142.9 mA. Error: (150 − 142.9) / 150 = 4.8 % low.
:::

The pattern is the mirror image of voltmeter loading. **Voltmeters cause trouble in high-resistance circuits; ammeters cause trouble in low-resistance circuits.** Duality strikes again.

## 8.3 Building Meters: Shunts and Multipliers

Before digital meters became cheap, nearly every electrical measurement was made with a **moving-coil movement**: a small coil of fine wire, suspended in a magnetic field, carrying a pointer. Current through the coil makes it turn, and the pointer moves across a scale. Such movements are still used in panel meters, and the way they are adapted to measure different ranges is a lovely application of series and parallel circuits.

A typical movement might give full-scale deflection with **1 mA**, and its coil might have a resistance of **100 Ω**. On its own, it is an ammeter that reads up to 1 mA, and also, by Ohm's law, a voltmeter that reads up to 1 mA × 100 Ω = 0.1 V. Neither range is much use. Two simple additions fix that.

### The ammeter shunt

To measure larger currents, we connect a low resistance called a **shunt** in parallel with the movement, as in Figure 8.3. Most of the current goes through the shunt; only a precise fraction goes through the movement.

![Figure 8.3 — Extending an ammeter's range with a shunt. The shunt is in parallel with the movement, so it diverts most of the current while the movement carries a fixed fraction.](fig/ch08_shunt.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 8.4 — Designing a 1 A ammeter**

Using the 1 mA, 100 Ω movement, design a shunt so that full-scale deflection corresponds to 1 A.

**Step 1: Voltage at full scale.** At full scale the movement carries 1 mA, so the voltage across it is 1 mA × 100 Ω = 0.1 V. Because the shunt is in parallel, it has the same 0.1 V across it.

**Step 2: Shunt current.** By KCL, the shunt must carry the rest: 1 A − 0.001 A = 0.999 A.

**Step 3: Shunt resistance.** R~sh~ = 0.1 / 0.999 = 0.1001 Ω.

**Comment.** The shunt is about one ten-thousandth of an ohm more than 0.1 Ω. Such precise low resistances are made from special alloy strips, and the connections to them must be carefully designed so that the resistance of the terminals themselves does not spoil the accuracy.
:::

### The voltmeter multiplier

To measure voltage, we add a high resistance called a **multiplier** in *series* with the movement (Figure 8.4). The total resistance is chosen so that the full-scale voltage drives exactly the full-scale current.

![Figure 8.4 — Making a voltmeter. A multiplier resistor in series with the movement sets the voltage needed for full-scale deflection.](fig/ch08_multiplier.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 8.5 — Designing a 10 V voltmeter**

Using the same movement, design a multiplier for a 10 V range.

**Step 1: Total resistance.** At full scale, 10 V must drive 1 mA: R~total~ = 10 / 0.001 = 10 kΩ.

**Step 2: Multiplier.** The movement provides 100 Ω, so R~mult~ = 10 000 − 100 = 9900 Ω.

**Sensitivity.** This voltmeter has 10 kΩ for every 10 V, or **1000 Ω/V**. Sensitivity is simply 1 / I~fs~, where I~fs~ is the full-scale current: the more sensitive the movement, the higher the voltmeter's resistance and the less it loads the circuit.
:::

### Current sensing in modern electronics

The shunt has not disappeared with the analogue meter. In almost every modern power supply, battery charger, motor controller and electric vehicle, current is measured by passing it through a tiny **current-sense resistor** and measuring the small voltage across it with an amplifier or analogue-to-digital converter. A 10 mΩ sense resistor carrying 5 A develops 50 mV and dissipates 5² × 0.01 = 0.25 W. The design choice is the same as for the old ammeter shunt: small enough not to waste power or disturb the circuit, large enough to give a measurable voltage.

## 8.4 The Wheatstone Bridge

In 1843 the English physicist Charles Wheatstone popularised a circuit (invented a decade earlier by Samuel Hunter Christie) that could measure an unknown resistance with remarkable precision using only a sensitive current detector and some known resistors. The **Wheatstone bridge** is shown in Figure 8.5. You will recognise its shape from the bridge network of Figure 7.7, with the middle resistor replaced by a sensitive meter, traditionally a **galvanometer**, G.

![Figure 8.5 — The Wheatstone bridge. Two voltage dividers (R~1~–R~3~ and R~2~–R~4~) share the same supply. The galvanometer G compares the voltages at their midpoints, C and D.](fig/ch08_wheatstone.svg){width=72%}

The key to understanding the bridge is to see it as **two voltage dividers side by side**. The left divider, R~1~ and R~3~, sets the voltage at C. The right divider, R~2~ and R~4~, sets the voltage at D. The galvanometer between them shows whether those two voltages are equal.

### The balance condition

When the voltage at C equals the voltage at D, no current flows through the galvanometer and the bridge is said to be **balanced**. Using the divider rule on each side (with the galvanometer drawing no current, so the dividers are unloaded):

::: eq
V~C~ = V~S~ × {{frac|R~3~|R~1~ + R~3~}}     V~D~ = V~S~ × {{frac|R~4~|R~2~ + R~4~}}
:::

Setting V~C~ = V~D~ and cancelling V~S~:

::: eq
{{frac|R~3~|R~1~ + R~3~}} = {{frac|R~4~|R~2~ + R~4~}}
:::

Cross-multiplying: R~3~(R~2~ + R~4~) = R~4~(R~1~ + R~3~), so R~2~R~3~ + R~3~R~4~ = R~1~R~4~ + R~3~R~4~. The R~3~R~4~ terms cancel, leaving R~2~R~3~ = R~1~R~4~.

::: keyeq
**Wheatstone bridge balance condition**

{{frac|R~1~|R~3~}} = {{frac|R~2~|R~4~}}     or equivalently     R~1~R~4~ = R~2~R~3~

At balance, no current flows in the detector.
:::

A neat way to remember it: **the products of opposite arms are equal**. Notice something remarkable about the balance condition: the supply voltage V~S~ has cancelled out completely. The balance point does not depend on the supply voltage, on its stability or even on its internal resistance. Nor does it depend on the resistance of the galvanometer, because at balance no current flows through it. That is why the bridge is so accurate: it relies only on the ratios of resistors, which can be made very precise.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.6 — Measuring an unknown resistance**

In Figure 8.5, R~1~ = 1 kΩ and R~3~ = 2 kΩ are fixed. R~4~ is an unknown resistor, and R~2~ is a precision variable resistor. The bridge balances when R~2~ = 500 Ω. Find R~4~.

**Solution.** From R~1~R~4~ = R~2~R~3~:

R~4~ = {{frac|R~2~ R~3~|R~1~}} = {{frac|500 × 2000|1000}} = 1000 Ω
:::

In a laboratory bridge, R~1~ and R~3~ are often switchable in decade ratios (1:1, 10:1, 100:1 and so on), so that a single precision variable resistor can measure unknowns over a wide range.

### A balanced bridge in a network

The balance condition is also a gift in circuit analysis. In Chapter 7 we found that a bridge network cannot be reduced by series and parallel steps. But if the bridge is balanced, C and D are at the same potential, and by the equipotential principle of Section 7.6 the middle element carries no current and can be removed. What remains is two series pairs in parallel.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.7 — A balanced bridge network**

A bridge network has R~1~ = 2 Ω, R~2~ = 4 Ω, R~3~ = 3 Ω, R~4~ = 6 Ω and a 10 Ω resistor between C and D. Find the equivalent resistance between the top and bottom nodes.

**Step 1: Check for balance.** R~1~R~4~ = 2 × 6 = 12; R~2~R~3~ = 4 × 3 = 12. Balanced.

**Step 2: Remove the middle resistor.** It carries no current, whatever its value.

**Step 3: Reduce.** Left side: 2 + 3 = 5 Ω. Right side: 4 + 6 = 10 Ω. In parallel: 5 × 10 / 15 = 3.33 Ω.
:::

## 8.5 Bridges and Dividers as Sensors

Many sensors are simply resistors whose value depends on something in the outside world. We met thermistors in Chapter 2, whose resistance depends on temperature, and LDRs, which respond to light. A **strain gauge** is a thin zigzag of metal foil glued to a surface (Figure 8.6). When the surface stretches, the foil gets slightly longer and thinner, and from R = ρL/A its resistance rises a little.

![Figure 8.6 — A foil strain gauge. When the material it is bonded to is stretched, the foil track lengthens and narrows, and its resistance increases very slightly.](fig/ch08_strain.svg){width=78%}

To turn a change in resistance into a signal we can use, we place the sensor in a divider or a bridge.

### A thermistor in a divider

::: example
**Worked Example 8.8 — A simple temperature sensor**

A 10 kΩ NTC thermistor (10 kΩ at 25 °C) is placed in the upper position of a divider with a fixed 10 kΩ resistor in the lower position, across 5 V. The output is taken across the fixed resistor. At 50 °C the thermistor's resistance is 3.59 kΩ. Find the output at 25 °C and at 50 °C.

**At 25 °C.** V~out~ = 5 × 10 / (10 + 10) = 2.50 V.

**At 50 °C.** V~out~ = 5 × 10 / (10 + 3.59) = 3.68 V.

**Comment.** As the temperature rises, the thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share of the 5 V and the output rises. A microcontroller can read this voltage and, with a calibration table, convert it to temperature. Figure 8.7 shows the output over a wider range. Notice that the response is not a straight line; that is the price of using a cheap and sensitive sensor.
:::

![Figure 8.7 — Output of the thermistor divider of Worked Example 8.8 against temperature. The output rises as the thermistor warms, steeply around room temperature and more gently at the extremes.](fig/ch08_thermistor.svg){width=86%}

### A strain gauge in a bridge

A strain gauge's resistance changes by only a tiny fraction – perhaps a few parts in ten thousand. In a simple divider, that would be a minute change superimposed on a large steady voltage, like trying to hear a whisper in a busy station. A bridge solves the problem. Arranged so that it is balanced when there is no strain, its output is *zero* at rest, and any output at all is due to the strain.

::: example
**Worked Example 8.9 — The output of a strain-gauge bridge**

A bridge is supplied from 10 V. Three arms are fixed 120 Ω resistors; the fourth (R~4~) is a 120 Ω strain gauge. Under load the gauge's resistance rises to 120.24 Ω, a change of 0.2 %. Find the bridge output voltage V~DC~ (assuming the measuring amplifier draws no current).

**Step 1: V~C~.** R~1~ = R~3~ = 120 Ω, so V~C~ = 10 × 120/240 = 5.000 V.

**Step 2: V~D~.** V~D~ = 10 × 120.24 / (120 + 120.24) = 10 × 120.24 / 240.24 = 5.004 995 V.

**Step 3: Output.** V~DC~ = V~D~ − V~C~ ≈ 4.995 mV, or about 5 mV.

**Comment.** A 0.2 % change in resistance gives a clean 5 mV signal from a bridge that would otherwise read exactly zero. An amplifier then boosts this to a useful level. This is precisely how digital kitchen and bathroom scales work: a metal beam carries strain gauges in a bridge, and your weight bends the beam very slightly.
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Using the unloaded divider rule when a load is connected.** Combine the load with R~2~ first.
- **Forgetting that meters load circuits.** Compare the meter's resistance with the circuit resistance at the measuring point.
- **Getting shunts and multipliers the wrong way round.** Shunts go in parallel (ammeters); multipliers go in series (voltmeters).
- **Misremembering the bridge balance condition.** Products of *opposite* arms are equal: R~1~R~4~ = R~2~R~3~ for the labelling of Figure 8.5.
- **Assuming balance means zero voltage everywhere.** At balance only the detector voltage is zero; the arms still carry current.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A **load** connected to a divider is in parallel with R~2~, reducing the output: V~out~ = V~S~ (R~2~ ∥ R~L~) / [R~1~ + (R~2~ ∥ R~L~)].
- Keep R~L~ at least **ten times** R~2~ for about 5 % accuracy; choosing divider values is a trade-off between stiffness and power.
- From its output, a divider looks like a source of its unloaded voltage in series with **R~1~ ∥ R~2~** – a first look at Thévenin's theorem.
- **Voltmeters** load high-resistance circuits; **ammeters** load low-resistance circuits.
- A **shunt** in parallel extends an ammeter's range; a **multiplier** in series turns a movement into a voltmeter. Voltmeter sensitivity is 1/I~fs~ ohms per volt.
- The **Wheatstone bridge** is two dividers side by side. It balances when R~1~R~4~ = R~2~R~3~, independently of the supply voltage.
- In a **balanced bridge network**, the middle element carries no current and can be removed.
- Resistive **sensors** are read using dividers (large signals) or bridges (small changes).

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

V~out~ = V~S~ (R~2~ ∥ R~L~) / [R~1~ + (R~2~ ∥ R~L~)]     R~sh~ = I~fs~R~m~ / (I − I~fs~)     R~mult~ = V~fs~ / I~fs~ − R~m~     R~1~R~4~ = R~2~R~3~
:::

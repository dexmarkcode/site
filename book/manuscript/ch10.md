# Power Semiconductors: Silicon, Silicon Carbide and Gallium Nitride

::: chapter-intro
Every converter in an electric vehicle is built around semiconductor switches, and their losses, ratings and failure modes shape the whole power electronic system. This chapter explains how the main devices work, from silicon diodes, MOSFETs and IGBTs to wide-bandgap silicon carbide and gallium nitride. It shows how to calculate conduction and switching losses, how heat flows from the chip to the coolant, how devices are packaged into power modules, why they wear out, and how gate drivers turn them on and off safely.
:::

When I started working on power electronics, a colleague handed me a datasheet for an IGBT module and said, "Everything you need to know is in here, and none of it is on the first page." He was right. The headline numbers, the voltage rating and the current rating, tell you almost nothing about how a device will behave in a real inverter. The useful information is buried in the graphs: how the on-state voltage changes with temperature, how switching energy changes with current and gate resistance, how the thermal impedance grows with time. Learning to read those graphs, and to turn them into losses and temperatures, is the core skill of a power electronics engineer.

This chapter teaches that skill. It also explains the biggest change in automotive power electronics of the past decade: the move from silicon to silicon carbide. That change has made 800 V vehicles practical, raised inverter efficiency by several percentage points over a drive cycle, and created a set of new engineering problems that are still being worked through.

## The Ideal Switch and Its Real Imitators

The ideal switch, as Chapter 3 described, has zero resistance when on, infinite resistance when off, and changes state instantly. It would convert power with no loss at all.

Real semiconductor switches fall short in three ways. When *on*, they have a voltage drop or a resistance, so current flowing through them causes *conduction loss*. When *switching*, they take a finite time, typically tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, during which voltage and current overlap, causing *switching loss*. And when *off*, they can block only up to a rated voltage, beyond which they break down, and they leak a tiny current.

Device designers face a fundamental trade-off. To block a high voltage, a device needs a thick, lightly doped *drift region* to spread the electric field. But a thick, lightly doped region has a high resistance when the device is on. For a simple silicon MOSFET, the on-resistance rises roughly with the blocking voltage raised to the power 2.5. That relationship is called the *silicon limit*, and it explains most of the history of power devices: each new device type is a clever way of escaping it.

## Silicon Devices

### Diodes

A diode conducts in one direction and blocks in the other. In an inverter, diodes carry current during the intervals when the main switches are off, providing the path that inductive load current needs. Their critical property in a fast-switching circuit is *reverse recovery*. A silicon PN diode stores charge in its drift region while conducting. When it is suddenly reverse-biased, that charge must be swept out before the diode can block, so for a short time it conducts *backwards*. The reverse recovery current flows through the opposite switch as it turns on, adding to that switch's turn-on loss, and the abrupt end of recovery can cause voltage overshoot and electromagnetic noise. Fast-recovery and soft-recovery diodes are designed to reduce these effects. Silicon carbide Schottky diodes, which have essentially no stored charge, eliminate them.

### MOSFETs

The metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET) is switched by the voltage on its *gate*, which is insulated from the rest of the device by a thin oxide layer. Applying a positive gate voltage above a threshold creates a conducting channel, and current flows between *drain* and *source* through a resistance *R*~DS(on)~. With the gate voltage removed, the channel disappears and the device blocks.

MOSFETs are *majority-carrier* devices: current is carried by one type of charge carrier only, and there is no stored charge to remove at turn-off. They therefore switch very fast. Their weakness is the silicon limit: at high voltages, *R*~DS(on)~ becomes prohibitively large. Silicon MOSFETs dominate below about 200 V, so they are the devices of choice for 48 V systems, low-voltage DC-DC converters and the secondary side of high-voltage converters.

MOSFETs have one more useful property. Every MOSFET contains a *body diode*, and when the channel is turned on, current can flow backwards through the channel as well as forwards. In an inverter, this *synchronous rectification* means the MOSFET's channel can carry the freewheeling current with lower loss than a diode would.

### IGBTs

The insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) combines a MOSFET-style insulated gate with a bipolar structure underneath. When it is on, the bipolar part floods the drift region with both holes and electrons, a process called *conductivity modulation*, which drastically lowers its resistance. The result is a device that can block 650 V or 1 200 V and still conduct hundreds of amps with an on-state voltage of only 1.3 to 2 V.

The price is switching speed. At turn-off, the excess carriers in the drift region must recombine before the device stops conducting, producing a *tail current* that persists for a microsecond or so while the voltage is already high. That tail is the main contributor to IGBT turn-off loss. IGBTs need a separate antiparallel diode to carry reverse current, since they cannot conduct backwards.

The IGBT's on-state behaviour is well modelled as a fixed voltage in series with a resistance:

$$ v_{CE} = V_{CE0} + r_{CE}\, i_C $$ {10.1}

For two decades, from the first hybrid vehicles until the late 2010s, silicon IGBTs were the standard switch in traction inverters, and they remain common in cost-sensitive 400 V vehicles.

## Wide-Bandgap Semiconductors

The *bandgap* of a semiconductor is the energy needed to free an electron from its bond so that it can conduct. Silicon's bandgap is 1.12 electronvolts. *Wide-bandgap* materials, chiefly silicon carbide (SiC) at about 3.26 eV and gallium nitride (GaN) at about 3.4 eV, hold their electrons much more tightly, and that has a cascade of consequences, summarised in Table 10.1.

Table: **Table 10.1** Key material properties of silicon and wide-bandgap semiconductors (approximate values).

| Property | Silicon | 4H-SiC | GaN |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Bandgap (eV) | 1.12 | 3.26 | 3.4 |
| Critical electric field (MV/cm) | 0.3 | 2.5–3 | 3.3 |
| Electron mobility (cm^2^/V·s) | 1 400 | ~950 | ~2 000 (2DEG) |
| Thermal conductivity (W/m·K) | 150 | 370–490 | 130–250 |
| Maximum practical junction temperature | 150–175 °C | 175–200 °C | 150 °C |

The most important number is the *critical electric field*, the field at which the material breaks down. Silicon carbide's is around ten times silicon's. That means a SiC device can block the same voltage with a drift region roughly one-tenth as thick, and doped about a hundred times more heavily. The drift resistance falls by a factor of several hundred. As a result, a SiC *MOSFET*, a fast, majority-carrier device with no tail current, can block 1 200 V with an on-resistance competitive with a silicon IGBT. Silicon carbide escapes the silicon limit.

SiC's high thermal conductivity, two to three times that of silicon, helps move heat out of the chip, and its wide bandgap allows operation at high temperature. Its disadvantages are cost, since SiC wafers are much harder to grow and process than silicon, and some reliability concerns specific to its gate oxide, discussed below. Costs have fallen steeply as wafer sizes have grown from 100 mm to 150 mm and now 200 mm, and SiC MOSFETs are standard in 800 V traction inverters and increasingly common at 400 V.

### Gallium nitride

GaN devices are built differently. The most common type, the *high-electron-mobility transistor* (HEMT), uses a thin layer of GaN topped by aluminium gallium nitride. At the interface, a sheet of extremely mobile electrons forms naturally, the *two-dimensional electron gas* (2DEG). The device switches extraordinarily fast, with tiny switching losses and no reverse recovery.

GaN devices are usually built on silicon substrates and are *lateral*: current flows along the surface rather than through the wafer. That makes them cheap to manufacture but limits their voltage rating to around 650 V in mainstream products and makes them harder to scale to very high currents. In vehicles, GaN is therefore found in on-board chargers and DC-DC converters, where its high switching frequency shrinks magnetic components dramatically. Vertical GaN devices and higher-voltage GaN for traction are active areas of development.

## Conduction Losses

Conduction loss depends on the current waveform through the device. For an inverter producing sinusoidal phase currents with peak value *Î*, modulation index *m* and power factor cos *φ*, each switch carries a portion of each cycle, and the average and RMS currents through one IGBT are approximately:

$$ I_{avg} = \hat{I}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi} + \frac{m \cos\varphi}{8}\right) $$ {10.2}

$$ I_{rms}^2 = \hat{I}^2\left(\frac{1}{8} + \frac{m \cos\varphi}{3\pi}\right) $$ {10.3}

The conduction loss of an IGBT is then *V*~CE0~*I*~avg~ + *r*~CE~*I*~rms~^2^, and that of a MOSFET is simply *R*~DS(on)~*I*~rms~^2^. Similar expressions, with the sign of the second term reversed, give the currents through the diodes.

::: example
**Worked Example 10.1 — Conduction loss: IGBT versus SiC MOSFET**

A traction inverter supplies 300 A RMS per phase, with *m* = 0.9 and cos *φ* = 0.9. Compare the conduction loss of one switch for (a) an IGBT with *V*~CE0~ = 0.9 V and *r*~CE~ = 1.6 mΩ at 150 °C, and (b) a SiC MOSFET with *R*~DS(on)~ = 3.0 mΩ at 150 °C.

*Solution.* Peak current *Î* = 300√2 = 424 A.

*I*~avg~ = 424 × (0.159 + 0.101) = 110.5 A.

*I*~rms~^2^ = 424^2^ × (0.125 + 0.0859) = 37 970 A^2^ (so *I*~rms~ = 195 A).

(a) IGBT: *P* = 0.9 × 110.5 + 0.0016 × 37 970 = 99.4 + 60.8 = 160 W.

(b) SiC MOSFET: *P* = 0.003 × 37 970 = 114 W.

At this heavy load the SiC device has 29 % less conduction loss. The advantage grows at light load, where the IGBT's fixed *V*~CE0~ dominates: at 50 A RMS, the IGBT loses about 21 W while the MOSFET loses only about 3 W. Since cars spend most of their time at light load, that difference is worth a great deal of range. The MOSFET also conducts the freewheeling current through its channel, avoiding most diode loss.
:::

One caution: MOSFET on-resistance *rises* with temperature, often by 50 to 100 % between 25 °C and 175 °C, so always use the hot value. This positive temperature coefficient is actually helpful when devices are connected in parallel, as they almost always are in traction modules: a chip that runs hotter conducts less, so current sharing is self-stabilising.

## Switching Losses

Switching loss arises because, during each transition, the device carries substantial current while substantial voltage appears across it. Figure 10.2 shows idealised waveforms for turn-on and turn-off in a half-bridge with an inductive load.

![**Figure 10.2** Idealised switching waveforms with an inductive load. At turn-on, current rises first while the voltage is still high, then voltage falls; reverse recovery of the opposite diode adds a current overshoot. At turn-off, voltage rises first, then current falls; an IGBT adds a tail current. The shaded areas represent the energy lost in each transition.](figures/fig10_2.png)

Datasheets give measured switching energies, *E*~on~ and *E*~off~, at a reference voltage and current, along with graphs showing how they vary. As a first approximation, switching energy scales linearly with both voltage and current. Averaged over a sinusoidal output cycle, the switching loss per device is:

$$ P_{sw} = \frac{f_{sw}}{\pi}\,(E_{on} + E_{off})\, \frac{\hat{I}}{I_{ref}}\, \frac{V_{DC}}{V_{ref}} $$ {10.4}

with a similar expression for diode reverse-recovery energy *E*~rr~.

::: example
**Worked Example 10.2 — Switching loss and the choice of frequency**

Continue Worked Example 10.1 with *V*~DC~ = 400 V. The IGBT module has *E*~on~ + *E*~off~ = 45 mJ and its diode *E*~rr~ = 10 mJ, both at 400 V and 400 A. The SiC module has *E*~on~ + *E*~off~ = 12 mJ at the same conditions and negligible recovery. Find the switching losses per device at 10 kHz, and for the SiC module also at 20 kHz.

*Solution.* Scaling factor *Î*/*I*~ref~ = 424/400 = 1.06; voltage factor 1.

IGBT at 10 kHz: *P*~sw~ = (10 000 / π) × 0.045 × 1.06 = 152 W, plus diode recovery (10 000/π) × 0.010 × 1.06 = 34 W.

SiC at 10 kHz: (10 000 / π) × 0.012 × 1.06 = 41 W.

SiC at 20 kHz: 81 W.

Adding conduction losses, the IGBT switch and its diode lose roughly 160 + 152 + 34 + (diode conduction) ≈ 400 W at this operating point. The SiC switch loses about 114 + 41 = 155 W at 10 kHz, or 195 W at 20 kHz. Even at twice the switching frequency, SiC loses less than half as much. That headroom can be spent on higher frequency (smaller filters, lower motor harmonic losses), on a smaller and cheaper chip area, on higher power from the same package, or simply on efficiency.
:::

### The cost of speed

Fast switching brings its own problems. SiC MOSFETs can switch hundreds of amps in a few tens of nanoseconds, giving voltage slew rates of 20 to 50 V/ns and current slew rates of several A/ns. The current slew rate acting on even a few nanohenries of stray inductance in the busbars and module produces voltage spikes, *v* = *L di*/*dt*. The voltage slew rate drives currents through every parasitic capacitance in the system: motor winding insulation (Chapter 8), bearings (Case File 8), heatsinks and cables, generating electromagnetic interference. Power electronic designers spend enormous effort minimising the *commutation loop inductance*, the area of the loop through which current switches, and they often deliberately slow switching slightly, trading a little efficiency for manageable overshoot and noise.

## Heat: From Junction to Coolant

The losses calculated above appear as heat in the semiconductor chip, at the *junction*. That heat must flow through the chip, its solder or sinter layer, a ceramic insulating substrate, a baseplate and a thermal interface or direct bond, into a cold plate and finally the coolant. As in the battery (Chapter 5), each layer has a thermal resistance, and the junction temperature in steady state is:

$$ T_j = T_{coolant} + P_{loss}\, R_{th(j\text{-}f)} $$ {10.5}

where *R*~th(j-f)~ is the junction-to-fluid thermal resistance.

For short pulses, the full steady-state resistance does not apply, because the heat has not yet reached the outer layers. The *transient thermal impedance* *Z*~th~(*t*), plotted in every datasheet, rises from near zero for microsecond pulses to *R*~th~ for pulses lasting a second or more. It is usually modelled as a chain of RC elements, a *Foster* or *Cauer* network, which the inverter software can run in real time to estimate junction temperature.

::: example
**Worked Example 10.3 — Junction temperature**

The IGBT in Example 10.2 dissipates 312 W (conduction plus switching) and has *R*~th(j-f)~ = 0.12 K/W. The coolant is at 65 °C. Find the junction temperature. During a 10 s peak at 1.6 times the current, losses rise to about 600 W; if *Z*~th~ at 10 s is 0.11 K/W, what is the peak junction temperature?

*Solution.* Steady state: *T*~j~ = 65 + 312 × 0.12 = 102 °C.

Peak: *T*~j~ ≈ 65 + 600 × 0.11 = 131 °C.

Both are within a 150 °C or 175 °C limit, with margin for the hotter chips in a module and for coolant temperature rising on a hot day. A design rule of thumb is to keep at least 15 to 25 K of margin to the maximum rated junction temperature under worst-case conditions, because the lifetime of the module depends heavily on how hot and how variably hot the junctions run.
:::

### The stall problem

There is one operating condition where inverter thermal design is especially demanding: very low motor speed with high torque, such as holding a car on a steep hill with the motor or creeping up a kerb. At very low electrical frequency, the phase currents change slowly, and one switch may carry near-peak current for a long time without the relief that the other half-cycle normally brings. Its junction temperature then follows the instantaneous current rather than the average, and it can overheat while the others are cool. Inverter software therefore limits torque at standstill and low speed more tightly than at moderate speed.

## Power Modules and Packaging

Bare semiconductor chips are only a few millimetres across. To carry hundreds of amps and connect to busbars and cooling, they are assembled into *power modules*. Figure 10.3 shows a typical cross-section.

![**Figure 10.3** Cross-section of a power module. Semiconductor chips are sintered or soldered onto a direct-bonded copper (DBC) ceramic substrate, which insulates them from the cooled baseplate while conducting heat. Top-side connections use bond wires or, in advanced modules, copper clips or sintered foils. Pin-fin baseplates are cooled directly by the coolant.](figures/fig10_3.png)

The key layers are:

- **Chips**: several IGBTs and diodes, or several SiC MOSFETs, in parallel per switch position. A 1 200 V traction module might contain six to twelve SiC dies per switch.
- **Die attach**: traditionally solder; increasingly *silver sintering*, which has much better thermal conductivity and survives temperature cycling far longer.
- **Substrate**: a *direct-bonded copper* (DBC) or *active-metal-brazed* (AMB) ceramic of aluminium oxide, aluminium nitride or silicon nitride, providing electrical insulation with good thermal conductivity. Silicon nitride is preferred for its mechanical toughness.
- **Top-side interconnects**: aluminium or copper *bond wires*, or more robust copper clips and ribbons.
- **Baseplate and cooling**: a copper baseplate with *pin fins* that sit directly in the coolant flow, eliminating the thermal grease layer that older designs needed.

Traction-specific packages go further. *Double-sided cooling* sandwiches the chips between two cooled surfaces, roughly halving thermal resistance. *Transfer-moulded* modules encapsulate a half-bridge in epoxy like a large integrated circuit, which is compact, robust and suited to high-volume production. And modules are designed for low internal inductance, with the positive and negative terminals laid close together and parallel to cancel their magnetic fields.

## Reliability and Lifetime

A traction inverter must survive perhaps 15 years and several hundred thousand kilometres. The dominant wear-out mechanism is *thermo-mechanical fatigue*. Every acceleration heats the chips and every lift-off lets them cool. The layers of a module, silicon or SiC, solder, copper, ceramic and aluminium, expand at different rates with temperature, so each cycle strains the joints between them. Over hundreds of thousands of cycles, cracks grow in solder layers and bond-wire feet lift off the chips. The module's thermal resistance rises, junction temperatures climb, and eventually it fails.

The number of cycles to failure falls steeply with the size of the temperature swing Δ*T*~j~. Empirical models based on the Coffin-Manson relationship take the form:

$$ N_f = A\, (\Delta T_j)^{-n}\, \exp\!\left(\frac{E_a}{k\, T_{j,m}}\right) $$ {10.6}

where *n* is typically 4 to 6 and *T*~j,m~ is the mean junction temperature. With *n* = 5, increasing the temperature swing from 40 K to 60 K reduces life by a factor of (1.5)^5^ ≈ 7.6. That is why reducing temperature swings, through lower losses, better thermal paths and sintered joints, is so valuable, and why inverter designers use *mission profiles* derived from real driving data to predict lifetime.

### SiC-specific concerns

SiC MOSFETs have two reliability issues that silicon devices do not share to the same degree. First, their gate oxide interface contains more defects, which can trap charge and cause the *threshold voltage to drift* over time, especially under negative gate bias at high temperature. Gate driver voltages and qualification tests are chosen to keep this drift within limits. Second, SiC chips are small for their current rating, so they have little thermal mass, and their *short-circuit withstand time* is only about 2 to 3 microseconds, compared with around 10 microseconds for an IGBT. Short-circuit protection must therefore act much faster, as Case File 10 illustrates.

Cosmic radiation is a further, more exotic concern. High-energy neutrons from cosmic rays occasionally strike a device that is blocking high voltage and trigger a destructive breakdown. The failure rate is small but rises steeply with the applied voltage as a fraction of the rating, which is one reason designers keep a generous margin between the DC-link voltage and the device rating: 1 200 V devices for 800 V systems, 650 or 750 V devices for 400 V systems.

## Gate Drivers

The *gate driver* is the circuit that turns each power switch on and off. It sounds trivial, but it has a large influence on losses, noise, reliability and safety.

**Isolation.** The upper switch in each half-bridge has its source or emitter at a potential that jumps between the negative and positive DC rails at every switching event. Its gate driver must therefore be isolated from the low-voltage control electronics, using magnetic or capacitive isolation for the signals and an isolated DC-DC converter for power. The isolation must withstand voltage slew rates above 50 V/ns without passing spurious signals, a property called *common-mode transient immunity*.

**Gate voltages.** IGBTs are typically driven at +15 V on and −8 V off. SiC MOSFETs need a higher on-voltage, typically +15 to +18 V, to achieve their rated on-resistance, and a modest negative off-voltage, around −3 to −5 V, to prevent spurious turn-on while limiting threshold drift.

**Switching speed control.** The gate resistance sets how fast the device switches. A smaller resistance gives faster, lower-loss switching but more overshoot and noise. Some drivers use different resistances for turn-on and turn-off, and advanced *active gate drivers* change the drive strength during the transition itself, switching fast through the lossy part and slowing down where overshoot is created.

**Miller clamp.** When one switch in a half-bridge turns on, the rapid voltage rise across the other, which is off, couples through its gate-drain capacitance and can lift its gate voltage enough to turn it on briefly, causing a shoot-through current. An *active Miller clamp* holds the gate firmly low during this period.

**Protection.** The driver detects short circuits by watching the voltage across the switch while it is on. In normal conduction it is a volt or two; in a short circuit it rises rapidly towards the full DC-link voltage as the device leaves its normal operating region, which is called *desaturation*. On detection, the driver turns the device off, but *softly*, slowly, because turning off thousands of amps quickly would create a destructive voltage spike in the stray inductance. Fast, reliable desaturation detection is especially critical for SiC.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Inverter derates when holding on a steep hill**

*Symptom.* Drivers report that the car rolls back slightly or shows reduced power warnings when held stationary on a steep ramp using the accelerator, for example in multistorey car parks.

*Likely causes.* (1) Expected thermal protection: at zero speed one switch carries near-peak current continuously, and the inverter's junction temperature model reduces torque (see "The stall problem"). (2) A degraded thermal path, such as a pump fault or air in the coolant loop, making derating happen sooner. (3) An unusually high coolant temperature from a previous hard drive.

*How to tell them apart.* Check logged estimated junction temperatures and coolant temperature. If derating occurs at normal coolant temperatures within the expected time, behaviour is by design and the solution lies in vehicle functions, such as automatic hill hold using the friction brakes. If it happens much sooner than in other vehicles of the same type, inspect the cooling system and compare the module's estimated thermal resistance with its value at production, if logged.
:::

::: casefile
**Case File 10 — The short circuit the IGBT survived**

*The complaint.* A manufacturer migrates an inverter design from silicon IGBTs to SiC MOSFETs to improve efficiency, keeping the existing gate driver board with only gate-voltage changes. During validation, a deliberate output short-circuit test, which the IGBT inverter passed routinely, destroys the SiC module.

*State it in numbers.* The IGBT module was rated to withstand a short circuit for 10 μs at 400 V. The existing desaturation detection responds after a blanking time of 4.5 μs plus a filter delay of 1.5 μs, and then turns the device off softly over 2 μs, a total of 8 μs. The SiC module's datasheet specifies a short-circuit withstand time of 2.5 μs at 400 V and 175 °C.

*Draw the boundary.* The gate driver's detection and turn-off timing, the module's withstand capability, and the stray inductance that sets the turn-off overshoot.

*Simplest model.* With 8 μs total response against a 2.5 μs capability, failure is almost certain. The blanking time was originally chosen to ride through the IGBT's slow turn-on transient without false trips; SiC turns on in tens of nanoseconds and does not need it.

*Measure.* Double-pulse tests with the short circuit applied show the SiC device's current reaching about eight times rated within 1 μs, and the chip temperature, estimated from electrothermal simulation, exceeding 600 °C at the top metallisation by 3 μs. Failure analysis shows melted source metallisation.

*Dominant term.* Detection and turn-off timing designed for IGBTs.

*Fix and cross-check.* A new gate driver uses a faster desaturation circuit with a blanking time of 0.6 μs, plus a separate di/dt sensing method using the module's internal source inductance that detects hard short circuits within 300 ns. Turn-off uses a two-step soft shutdown. The total response falls to about 1.5 μs, within the 2.5 μs capability with margin. The team re-checks false-trip immunity under normal switching at maximum temperature and with worst-case noise from adjacent phases.

*Lesson.* Substituting a new device into an old design is never just a component change. Every assumption, including timing margins written years earlier, must be revisited.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Real switches have conduction loss, switching loss and limited blocking voltage. In silicon, on-resistance rises steeply with voltage rating (the silicon limit).
- Silicon MOSFETs are fast and dominate low-voltage applications; IGBTs use conductivity modulation to conduct well at high voltage but switch slowly because of tail current.
- Silicon carbide's tenfold higher critical field allows fast, low-loss MOSFETs at 1 200 V; GaN HEMTs switch even faster and suit chargers and DC-DC converters at up to about 650 V.
- Conduction loss is *V*~CE0~*I*~avg~ + *r*~CE~*I*~rms~^2^ for IGBTs and *R*~DS(on)~*I*~rms~^2^ for MOSFETs; switching loss scales with frequency, voltage and current.
- Junction temperature equals coolant temperature plus loss times thermal resistance; transient impedance governs short peaks. Low-speed, high-torque operation is a thermal worst case.
- Modules use sintered die attach, ceramic substrates, pin-fin or double-sided cooling and low-inductance layouts.
- Thermal cycling fatigue governs lifetime and depends strongly on temperature swing. SiC has short short-circuit withstand times and gate-oxide threshold drift concerns.
- Gate drivers provide isolation, correct gate voltages, controlled switching speed, Miller clamping and fast, soft short-circuit protection.
:::

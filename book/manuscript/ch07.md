Anyone who has pulled a pair of wired earphones out of a coat pocket knows the feeling. A tangle that looks hopeless at first glance turns out, with patience, to be a few simple loops wound round each other. You do not attack the whole knot at once. You find the loosest loop, free it, and work outwards.

Many circuits are like that. At first sight a network of resistors can look bewildering, with wires crossing and branches sprouting in every direction. But a large proportion of real circuits are built entirely from series and parallel combinations, nested inside one another. Find the innermost pair, combine it, redraw, and repeat. Each step leaves a simpler circuit, until only a single equivalent resistance remains. Then, if you need the individual currents and voltages, you work back out again.

This chapter turns that idea into a reliable method. We will also look at ladder networks, the surprisingly important question of *which terminals* you are looking into, some clever uses of symmetry, and the point at which series–parallel reduction runs out and more powerful tools are needed.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- reduce any series–parallel network to a single equivalent resistance;
- work back through a reduced network to find every current and voltage;
- use the divider rules to find a single quantity without solving the whole circuit;
- analyse ladder networks, including the "work backwards from the output" technique;
- explain why equivalent resistance depends on the pair of terminals chosen;
- deal with short-circuited and open-ended resistors within a network;
- use symmetry to simplify networks, and recognise networks that cannot be reduced by series and parallel steps alone.
:::

## 7.1 The Reduction Method

Here is the procedure. It is not complicated, but following it carefully avoids almost all mistakes.

1. **Label the nodes.** Give every node a letter, remembering (Chapter 4) that all points joined by plain wire are one node.
2. **Find a pair you can combine.** Look for two resistors that are definitely in series (joined at a node with nothing else attached) or definitely in parallel (connected between the same two nodes). Usually the best place to start is the part of the circuit furthest from the source.
3. **Combine and redraw.** Replace the pair by its equivalent, and redraw the circuit, keeping the node labels. Do not try to do several steps in your head: redraw.
4. **Repeat** until a single resistance remains between the source terminals.
5. **Find the source current**, I = V / R~T~.
6. **Work back out**, step by step through your drawings in reverse order, using Ohm's law, KVL, KCL and the divider rules to find each current and voltage you need.
7. **Check**, with KCL at a node, KVL round a loop, or a power balance.

Step 3 deserves emphasis. The most common mistake I see is students trying to "see" the whole reduction at once and combining resistors that are not actually in series or parallel. Redrawing after each step takes a little longer and saves a great deal of grief.

![Figure 7.1 — A simple series–parallel circuit. R~2~ and R~3~ are in parallel between node A and the bottom node; that combination is in series with R~1~.](fig/ch07_basic.svg){width=64%}

::: example
**Worked Example 7.1 — A first series–parallel circuit**

In Figure 7.1, find the total resistance, the source current, the voltage at node A and the current in each resistor.

**Step 1: Combine the parallel pair.** R~2~ and R~3~ both connect between A and the bottom node, so they are in parallel:

R~23~ = {{frac|6 × 12|6 + 12}} = {{frac|72|18}} = 4 Ω

**Step 2: Combine in series.** R~1~ and R~23~ are now in series: R~T~ = 4 + 4 = 8 Ω.

**Step 3: Source current.** I = 24 / 8 = 3 A. This flows through R~1~.

**Step 4: Work back.** The voltage across R~1~ is 3 × 4 = 12 V, so by KVL the voltage at A is V~A~ = 24 − 12 = 12 V. (Or, by the divider rule, V~A~ = 24 × 4/8 = 12 V.) This voltage is across both R~2~ and R~3~:

I~2~ = 12 / 6 = 2 A     I~3~ = 12 / 12 = 1 A

**Step 5: Check.** KCL at A: 3 = 2 + 1 ✓. Powers: R~1~ 36 W, R~2~ 24 W, R~3~ 12 W, total 72 W; source 24 × 3 = 72 W ✓.
:::

## 7.2 A Multi-Level Network

Let us take a network with three levels of nesting, which is closer to what you will meet in practice.

![Figure 7.2 — A three-level series–parallel network. R~4~ and R~5~ are in parallel; that pair is in series with R~3~; the result is in parallel with R~2~; and all of that is in series with R~1~.](fig/ch07_multi.svg){width=96%}

::: example
**Worked Example 7.2 — Reducing and expanding a three-level network**

Find every current and voltage in Figure 7.2, and verify the power balance.

**Reduction** (Figure 7.3 summarises the steps).

- R~4~ and R~5~ connect between B and the bottom node: parallel. R~45~ = 12 × 24 / 36 = 8 Ω.
- R~3~ and R~45~ meet at B, which now has nothing else attached: series. R~345~ = 12 + 8 = 20 Ω.
- R~2~ and R~345~ both connect between A and the bottom node: parallel. R~A~ = 20 × 20 / 40 = 10 Ω.
- R~1~ and R~A~ in series: R~T~ = 5 + 10 = 15 Ω.

![Figure 7.3 — The reduction of Figure 7.2 in four steps, working inwards from the far end.](fig/ch07_steps.svg){width=100%}

**Expansion.**

- Source current: I~1~ = 60 / 15 = 4 A, through R~1~.
- Voltage across R~1~: 4 × 5 = 20 V, so V~A~ = 60 − 20 = 40 V.
- R~2~ has 40 V across it: I~2~ = 40 / 20 = 2 A.
- By KCL at A, the current into R~3~ is I~3~ = 4 − 2 = 2 A. (Or: 40 V across the 20 Ω of R~345~ gives 2 A.)
- Voltage across R~3~: 2 × 12 = 24 V, so V~B~ = 40 − 24 = 16 V.
- I~4~ = 16 / 12 = 1.333 A; I~5~ = 16 / 24 = 0.667 A. KCL at B: 1.333 + 0.667 = 2 A ✓.

**Power balance.** R~1~: 4² × 5 = 80 W. R~2~: 2² × 20 = 80 W. R~3~: 2² × 12 = 48 W. R~4~: 16² / 12 = 21.33 W. R~5~: 16² / 24 = 10.67 W. Total 240 W. The source delivers 60 × 4 = 240 W ✓.
:::

### Going straight to one answer

If you only need *one* quantity, you do not have to find everything. The divider rules let you jump straight to it. Suppose in Figure 7.2 we only wanted V~B~.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.3 — Two dividers in a row**

Find V~B~ in Figure 7.2 using only the voltage divider rule.

**Step 1.** Seen from the source, R~1~ (5 Ω) is in series with the 10 Ω combination at A. So V~A~ = 60 × 10/15 = 40 V.

**Step 2.** Within the 40 V branch, R~3~ (12 Ω) is in series with R~45~ (8 Ω). So V~B~ = 40 × 8/20 = 16 V. ✓

Two lines instead of a page.
:::

Similarly, the current divider rule can take you straight to a branch current. For I~5~: the 2 A entering node B divides between 12 Ω and 24 Ω, so I~5~ = 2 × 12/36 = 0.667 A. Learning to spot these chains of dividers is one of the most useful skills you can acquire, because it is fast and it gives insight into how each part of the circuit affects the answer.

## 7.3 Ladder Networks

A **ladder network** is a chain of alternating series and shunt (parallel-to-ground) elements, like the rungs and rails of a ladder lying on its side. Ladders turn up in attenuators, in digital-to-analogue converters, in models of long cables and in filters. Figure 7.4 shows a two-section ladder.

![Figure 7.4 — A two-section ladder network. Series elements R~1~ and R~3~ alternate with shunt elements R~2~ and R~4~.](fig/ch07_ladder.svg){width=84%}

The standard approach is to reduce from the far end, exactly as before.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.4 — Reducing a ladder**

In Figure 7.4, find the source current and the voltages at A and B.

**Reduction from the far end.** R~3~ and R~4~ are in series: 10 + 30 = 40 Ω. This is in parallel with R~2~: 40 ∥ 40 = 20 Ω. Then in series with R~1~: R~T~ = 10 + 20 = 30 Ω.

**Expansion.** I = 30 / 30 = 1 A. V~A~ = 30 − 1 × 10 = 20 V. The current through R~3~ and R~4~ is 20 / 40 = 0.5 A, so V~B~ = 0.5 × 30 = 15 V.
:::

(Here and from now on I shall use the symbol ∥ as shorthand for "in parallel with". So 40 ∥ 40 means 40 Ω in parallel with 40 Ω.)

### Working backwards from the output

There is an elegant alternative for ladders, especially when you want the output voltage. **Assume** a convenient value at the far end – say 1 V across the last element – and work *towards* the source using only Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws. You will arrive at the source voltage that *would* produce your assumed output. Then scale everything by the ratio of the real source voltage to that value.

This works because the circuit is **linear**: if every source is multiplied by some factor, every voltage and current in the circuit is multiplied by the same factor. We will look at linearity properly in Chapter 13, but it is worth seeing it in action now.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.5 — The unit-output method**

Find V~B~ in Figure 7.4 by assuming V~B~ = 1 V and working back.

**Step 1.** Assume V~B~ = 1 V. Current through R~4~ = 1/30 A = 0.0333 A. This same current flows through R~3~.

**Step 2.** V~A~ = V~B~ + I~3~R~3~ = 1 + 0.0333 × 10 = 1.333 V.

**Step 3.** Current through R~2~ = 1.333 / 40 = 0.0333 A.

**Step 4.** KCL at A: current through R~1~ = 0.0333 + 0.0333 = 0.0667 A.

**Step 5.** Source voltage needed: V~S~ = V~A~ + I~1~R~1~ = 1.333 + 0.0667 × 10 = 2.0 V.

**Step 6: Scale.** A 2 V source would give 1 V at B. The real source is 30 V, which is 15 times larger, so V~B~ = 15 × 1 = **15 V**. ✓

**Bonus.** The ratio V~B~ / V~S~ = 1/2 is the ladder's **voltage gain** (here a loss, or attenuation). It is a property of the network alone, and the unit-output method finds it directly.
:::

The beauty of this method is that it never requires parallel-resistance calculations or division by awkward totals. Each step is a simple multiplication or addition. For long ladders it is much quicker than reduction.

## 7.4 It Depends Which Terminals You Look Into

Equivalent resistance is always measured *between two particular terminals*. Change the terminals and the answer changes, even for the same network. This sounds obvious once stated, but it catches people out, especially when a network is drawn without a source.

![Figure 7.5 — A triangle of three resistors. The resistance seen between A and B, between A and C, and between B and C are all different.](fig/ch07_terminals.svg){width=44%}

::: example
**Worked Example 7.6 — Three answers from one network**

For the network in Figure 7.5, find the equivalent resistance between (a) A and B, (b) A and C, (c) B and C.

**(a) Between A and B.** Imagine connecting an ohmmeter to A and B. There are two paths: directly through 9 Ω, or round through 6 Ω and 3 Ω in series (9 Ω). These two paths are in parallel: R~AB~ = 9 ∥ 9 = 4.5 Ω.

**(b) Between A and C.** Paths: directly through 6 Ω, or through 9 Ω and 3 Ω in series (12 Ω). R~AC~ = 6 ∥ 12 = 4 Ω.

**(c) Between B and C.** Paths: directly through 3 Ω, or through 9 Ω and 6 Ω in series (15 Ω). R~BC~ = 3 ∥ 15 = 2.5 Ω.

**Lesson.** Notice that the 9 Ω resistor is "in parallel" with the others in (a) but "in series" with one of them in (b) and (c). Series and parallel are not properties of resistors themselves; they describe how resistors relate to a chosen pair of terminals.
:::

This idea – the resistance "seen" looking into a pair of terminals – is the heart of Thévenin's theorem. When we find a Thévenin resistance in Chapter 14, we will be doing exactly what we did here.

## 7.5 Shorted and Dangling Resistors

Two special situations often appear in reduction problems, sometimes deliberately set as traps.

**A resistor with a plain wire across it** is short-circuited. Both its ends are the same node, so the voltage across it is zero and, by Ohm's law, it carries no current. It contributes nothing, and you can remove it from the diagram entirely (leaving the wire). A resistor in parallel with zero ohms is zero ohms.

**A resistor with one end connected to nothing** is said to be **dangling** or **open-ended**. No current can flow through it, because there is no complete path. It too can be removed. However, be careful: although it carries no current, its free end is at the same potential as its connected end, and a voltmeter connected there would read that voltage. The voltage drop across a resistor carrying no current is zero.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.7 — Spotting the traps**

A 12 V source is connected across a 4 Ω resistor in series with a 6 Ω resistor. A wire is connected directly across the 6 Ω resistor, and a 10 Ω resistor hangs from the junction between them with its other end unconnected. Find the source current.

**Solution.** The 6 Ω resistor is shorted: remove it. The 10 Ω resistor is dangling: remove it. All that remains is the 4 Ω resistor across 12 V, so I = 12 / 4 = 3 A. The current in the shorting wire is 3 A; the current in both the 6 Ω and the 10 Ω resistors is zero.
:::

## 7.6 Using Symmetry

Some networks look as though they cannot be reduced, yet yield instantly to a little thought about symmetry. The key principle is this:

::: keyeq
**Equipotential points**

If two points in a circuit are at the same potential, they can be joined by a wire, or any resistor between them can be removed, without changing anything else in the circuit.
:::

The reasoning is simple. If two points are at the same voltage, a wire between them would carry no current (there is no voltage to drive it), and a resistor between them carries no current either. Adding or removing something that carries no current changes nothing.

Symmetry often tells us that two points *must* be at the same potential, even before we calculate anything. A classic puzzle shows the power of this idea.

![Figure 7.6 — Twelve equal resistors along the edges of a cube. What is the resistance between opposite corners A and B?](fig/ch07_cube.svg){width=46%}

::: example
**Worked Example 7.8 — The resistor cube**

Twelve 1 Ω resistors form the edges of a cube (Figure 7.6). Find the resistance between diagonally opposite corners A and B.

**Step 1: Use symmetry.** Three resistors leave corner A, going to three corners. The cube looks exactly the same from each of these three edges, so a current entering at A must split equally between them, and the three corners next to A must all be at the same potential. Likewise, the three corners next to B are all at the same potential as one another.

**Step 2: Join equipotential corners.** We may join the three corners next to A into a single node, and the three corners next to B into another, without changing anything.

**Step 3: Reduce.** Now the cube is three groups in series:

- three 1 Ω resistors in parallel from A to the first node: 1/3 Ω;
- six 1 Ω resistors in parallel between the two middle nodes (each of the three corners near A connects to two corners near B): 1/6 Ω;
- three 1 Ω resistors in parallel from the second node to B: 1/3 Ω.

R~AB~ = 1/3 + 1/6 + 1/3 = 5/6 Ω ≈ 0.833 Ω.
:::

Puzzles like the cube are fun, but the principle has very practical uses. In Chapter 8 we will meet the **balanced bridge**, where two nodes are at the same potential and the resistor between them can be removed, turning an awkward network into a simple series–parallel one.

## 7.7 When Reduction Is Not Enough

Not every network can be reduced by series and parallel steps. Figure 7.7 shows the simplest example: the **bridge** network.

![Figure 7.7 — A bridge network. No two resistors are in series or in parallel, so series–parallel reduction cannot even begin.](fig/ch07_bridge.svg){width=72%}

Try to find a pair to combine. R~1~ and R~3~? They meet at C, but C also connects to R~5~, so they are not in series. R~1~ and R~2~? They share the top node, but their other ends go to different nodes, so they are not in parallel. Check every pair and you will find the same: R~5~, sitting across the middle, spoils every combination.

There are three ways out, all of which you will learn in this book:

- If the bridge happens to be **balanced**, C and D are at the same potential and R~5~ can be removed (Chapter 8).
- The **delta–star transformation** can convert three of the resistors into an equivalent arrangement that *is* series–parallel (Chapter 9).
- **Nodal** or **mesh analysis** can solve any such network directly, without any reduction at all (Chapters 10 and 11).

Recognising when reduction will not work is just as important as knowing how to do it. If you have spent five minutes hunting for a series or parallel pair and found none, stop: the circuit is telling you to use a different tool.

::: tip
**Thornton's Rule of Thumb**

If a circuit has a single source and you need the total current or the voltage at a particular point, try reduction first. It is quick and gives insight. If the circuit has several sources, or contains a bridge-like structure, or you need many currents at once, go straight to nodal or mesh analysis. Chapter 17 sets out this decision in full.
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Combining resistors that only look series or parallel.** Check the nodes every time.
- **Trying to do it all in your head.** Redraw after every step.
- **Forgetting that the "parallel" voltage changes** as you work back. Each level of the network has its own voltage.
- **Ignoring which terminals are specified.** R~AB~ and R~AC~ of the same network are generally different.
- **Leaving a shorted or dangling resistor in your calculation.** Neither carries current.
- **Persisting with reduction on a bridge.** Some networks cannot be reduced by series and parallel steps alone.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- Series–parallel networks are reduced by **repeatedly combining** series or parallel pairs and **redrawing**, usually starting from the end furthest from the source.
- After finding the source current, **work back out** through the drawings to find every current and voltage, and **check** with KCL, KVL or a power balance.
- **Chains of divider rules** often reach a single answer much faster than full analysis.
- **Ladder networks** can be reduced from the far end, or solved by assuming a unit output and scaling – a method that relies on linearity.
- **Equivalent resistance depends on the terminals** you look into.
- A **shorted** resistor and a **dangling** resistor both carry no current and can be removed.
- **Equipotential points** may be joined, or the resistor between them removed. Symmetry often identifies them.
- **Bridge networks** cannot be reduced by series and parallel steps; other methods are needed.

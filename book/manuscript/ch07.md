# Traction Motor Fundamentals

::: chapter-intro
Every traction motor turns electrical current into torque through the same basic physics, yet the machines in today's electric vehicles look remarkably different from one another. This chapter explains how motors make torque, how a three-phase winding creates a rotating magnetic field, and how the main families of traction motor work: induction, permanent-magnet synchronous, wound-rotor synchronous and reluctance machines. It finishes by comparing them and asking why manufacturers choose the machines they do.
:::

There is something deeply satisfying about an electric motor. It has one moving part. It has no valves, no pistons, no combustion and no exhaust. It can run at 98 % efficiency and deliver its maximum torque the instant you ask. It can run backwards as a generator without any change to its construction. And it has been understood in principle since the 1830s, when Faraday, Henry and others first showed that electricity and magnetism could make things move.

But simplicity of principle is not the same as ease of design. A modern traction motor must deliver enormous torque from a small, light package, spin at speeds above 15 000 rpm without flying apart, stay efficient across a vast range of speeds and loads, run quietly enough not to whine in a silent cabin, cost very little, and avoid scarce materials where possible. Meeting all those requirements at once has pushed motor design further in the past fifteen years than in the previous fifty.

## How Motors Make Torque

### The force on a conductor

Chapter 3 introduced the Lorentz force: a conductor of length *l*, carrying current *I* at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density *B*, experiences a force *F* = *BIl*. Put many such conductors around the edge of a cylinder of radius *r*, arrange for the currents and fields to push them all the same way round, and you have torque:

$$ T = N\, B\, I\, l\, r $$ {7.1}

for *N* conductors. Every rotating electric machine is, at heart, a way of arranging currents and fields so that this force acts consistently in one direction as the rotor turns.

### Shear stress and the size of a motor

There is a more useful way to look at Equation 7.1. The force per unit area acting across the *air gap* between the rotor and stator is a tangential magnetic *shear stress*, *σ*. If that stress acts over the rotor's cylindrical surface of diameter *D* and length *L*, the torque is the force times the radius:

$$ T = \sigma \cdot (\pi D L) \cdot \frac{D}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\, \sigma\, D^2 L = 2\, \sigma\, V_r $$ {7.2}

where *V*~r~ = π*D*^2^*L*/4 is the rotor volume. This is one of the most important results in motor design: *torque is proportional to rotor volume*, with the shear stress as the constant of proportionality.

The shear stress is limited by two things. The magnetic loading, roughly the air-gap flux density, is limited by saturation of the iron, to about 0.8 to 1.0 T on average. The electric loading, the current per metre of air-gap circumference, is limited by how much heat the cooling system can remove from the windings. Air-cooled industrial motors manage shear stresses of perhaps 10 to 20 kPa. Liquid-cooled traction motors running at their peak rating reach 40 to 60 kPa, and the best designs with direct oil cooling of the windings go higher still.

This tells you something fundamental about the industry's direction. Since torque depends on volume, the only ways to make a smaller, lighter motor for the same *power* are to raise the shear stress, through better cooling and magnetic materials, or to raise the *speed*, since power is torque times speed. A motor that delivers 150 kW at 15 000 rpm needs only half the torque, and roughly half the rotor volume, of one delivering 150 kW at 7 500 rpm. That is why traction motor speeds have risen relentlessly.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.1 — How big must the rotor be?**

A traction motor must produce 350 N·m of peak torque. The design team expects to achieve a peak air-gap shear stress of 40 kPa. They prefer a rotor whose length is 1.2 times its diameter. Estimate the rotor dimensions.

*Solution.* From Equation 7.2, the rotor volume is:

*V*~r~ = *T* / 2*σ* = 350 / (2 × 40 000) = 4.375 × 10^−3^ m^3^.

With *L* = 1.2*D*, *V*~r~ = π*D*^2^(1.2*D*)/4 = 0.942*D*^3^, so

*D* = (4.375 × 10^−3^ / 0.942)^1/3^ = 0.167 m, and *L* = 0.200 m.

A rotor about 167 mm in diameter and 200 mm long. The stator around it, including windings, end turns and housing, would make the complete motor roughly 250 mm in outer diameter and 300 mm long. If the shear stress could be raised to 55 kPa through better winding cooling, the rotor volume would fall by 27 %.
:::

## The Rotating Magnetic Field

DC motors, the type you may have met in school, use a mechanical *commutator* to reverse the current in the rotor windings as they turn, so that the torque always acts in the same direction. Early electric vehicles and many industrial drives used them, and they are easy to control. But commutators wear, spark and limit speed, and the rotor windings are hard to cool. With the arrival of cheap, powerful power electronics, AC machines took over completely.

AC machines rely on the trick shown in Figure 7.1. Three sets of coils are placed around the stator, displaced from each other by 120 electrical degrees, and fed with three-phase currents that are themselves displaced by 120° in time. Each coil set on its own produces a field that pulses back and forth along its own axis. Added together, the three pulsating fields produce a single field of constant strength that *rotates* around the air gap.

![**Figure 7.1** Creation of a rotating magnetic field. Three stator windings, displaced by 120° in space and fed with currents displaced by 120° in time, combine to produce a resultant field (bold arrow) of constant magnitude that rotates at the synchronous speed. Snapshots are shown at four instants a sixth of a cycle apart.](figures/fig7_1.png)

The field rotates once for every electrical cycle in a machine with one pair of poles. A machine with *p* pole pairs has its windings repeated *p* times around the circumference, and the field advances by only one pole pair per cycle. The speed of the rotating field, the *synchronous speed*, is therefore:

$$ n_s = \frac{60 f}{p} \quad \mathrm{rpm} $$ {7.3}

where *f* is the electrical frequency in hertz. Traction motors typically have between two and six pole pairs. More poles mean shorter magnetic paths, so less iron and a lighter machine, but a higher electrical frequency for a given speed, which raises iron losses and switching demands on the inverter.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.2 — Electrical frequency at top speed**

An eight-pole (four pole-pair) motor must run at 16 000 rpm. What electrical frequency must the inverter produce? If the inverter switches at 10 kHz, how many switching periods are there in each electrical cycle?

*Solution.* From Equation 7.3, *f* = *n*~s~*p*/60 = 16 000 × 4 / 60 = 1 067 Hz.

Switching periods per cycle = 10 000 / 1 067 ≈ 9.4.

With fewer than ten switching events per cycle, the inverter can no longer synthesise a smooth sine wave, and current harmonics, losses and control difficulties rise. This is one reason high-speed, high-pole-count machines push inverter switching frequencies upwards, and why silicon carbide, which switches with lower losses, suits them so well.
:::

The two main families of AC machine differ in how the rotor responds to this rotating field. In a *synchronous* machine, the rotor has its own magnetic poles, from permanent magnets or a DC field winding, and it locks onto the rotating field and turns at exactly the synchronous speed. In an *asynchronous* or *induction* machine, the rotor has no magnetic poles of its own; the rotating field induces currents in it, and those currents create the rotor field. The rotor must turn slightly slower than the field for this to happen.

## The Induction Motor

The induction motor, invented in the 1880s, is the workhorse of industry. Its rotor is extraordinarily simple: a stack of steel laminations with bars of aluminium or copper running through slots along its length, joined at each end by rings. The arrangement resembles a hamster wheel and is called a *squirrel cage*.

When the stator field rotates past the cage, it induces voltages in the bars by Faraday's law. The bars are short-circuited by the end rings, so currents flow, and those currents interact with the stator field to produce torque. The induced voltage depends on the *relative* speed between field and rotor. If the rotor ran at exactly synchronous speed, there would be no relative motion, no induced current and no torque. So the rotor always *slips* slightly behind. The slip is defined as:

$$ s = \frac{n_s - n}{n_s} $$ {7.4}

At full load, the slip of a traction induction motor is typically 1 to 5 %. The frequency of currents in the rotor is *s* times the stator frequency, a few hertz.

### Losses and the torque-slip curve

The power crossing the air gap, *P*~ag~, divides neatly between mechanical output and rotor copper loss:

$$ P_{rotor\,cu} = s\, P_{ag}, \qquad P_{mech} = (1 - s)\, P_{ag} $$ {7.5}

So slip is not just a speed difference; it is directly a loss. An induction motor running at 3 % slip loses at least 3 % of its air-gap power as heat in the rotor bars. That rotor heat is the induction motor's main weakness: it is generated in the spinning part of the machine, which is the hardest part to cool.

![**Figure 7.2** Torque-speed (torque-slip) characteristic of an induction motor at fixed supply frequency. Normal operation lies on the steep, nearly linear section near synchronous speed. An inverter-fed traction motor shifts this curve along the speed axis by changing the frequency.](figures/fig7_2.png)

Figure 7.2 shows the torque an induction motor produces at a fixed supply frequency. Near synchronous speed, torque rises almost linearly with slip. At larger slips, it reaches a maximum, the *breakdown torque*, and then falls. Mains-connected industrial motors live on this one curve. A traction motor fed from an inverter can change the supply frequency continuously, sliding the whole curve along the speed axis, so it always operates on the efficient, low-slip part.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.3 — Slip and rotor loss**

A four-pole (two pole-pair) induction motor is fed at 200 Hz and runs at 5 850 rpm, with 100 kW crossing the air gap. Find the slip, the rotor current frequency, the rotor copper loss and the mechanical power.

*Solution.* Synchronous speed: *n*~s~ = 60 × 200 / 2 = 6 000 rpm.

Slip: *s* = (6 000 − 5 850) / 6 000 = 0.025, or 2.5 %.

Rotor frequency: 0.025 × 200 = 5 Hz.

Rotor copper loss: 0.025 × 100 = 2.5 kW. Mechanical power: 97.5 kW.

That 2.5 kW is generated in a rotor spinning at nearly 6 000 rpm. Removing it requires either a hollow shaft with coolant flowing through it, oil spray, or accepting a high rotor temperature and limiting continuous power.
:::

### Strengths and weaknesses

The induction motor's great strengths are that it contains no magnets, so it has no rare-earth supply risk and cannot be demagnetised; its rotor is robust and can run at very high speed; it is relatively cheap; and, crucially for some vehicle applications, *it produces no field when it is not energised*. A permanent-magnet machine spinning with no current still has its magnets sweeping past the stator, causing iron losses and a back-EMF. An unpowered induction motor simply freewheels with only bearing and windage losses. Case File 7 shows why this matters.

Its weaknesses are lower efficiency than the best permanent-magnet machines, especially at light load and low speed, where the magnetising current is a large proportion of the total; rotor losses that are hard to cool; and a somewhat lower torque density. Copper rotor cages, which have lower resistance than aluminium, recover some of the efficiency deficit but are harder to manufacture.

## The Permanent-Magnet Synchronous Motor

The permanent-magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) puts the rotor's magnetic poles in the rotor itself, using high-strength magnets, usually sintered neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB). Because the rotor field comes free, without any magnetising current, a PMSM avoids the induction motor's rotor copper losses entirely and needs less stator current for the same torque. The result is higher efficiency, typically peaking at 96 to 97 % or above, and higher torque density. Most battery electric cars use PMSMs for at least one axle.

### Surface and interior magnets

There are two main ways to mount the magnets, shown in Figure 7.3.

![**Figure 7.3** Rotor cross-sections for four traction machine types. (a) Surface permanent magnet. (b) Interior permanent magnet with V-shaped magnet pockets, the most common traction arrangement. (c) Squirrel-cage induction rotor. (d) Synchronous reluctance rotor with flux barriers.](figures/fig7_3.png)

In a *surface permanent-magnet* (SPM) machine, magnets are glued to the outside of the rotor and held by a sleeve of carbon fibre or non-magnetic steel. The design is simple and gives smooth torque, but the sleeve must resist large centrifugal forces at high speed, and the magnets are exposed to the full effect of the stator field and to eddy-current heating.

In an *interior permanent-magnet* (IPM) machine, the magnets sit in slots, often in V or double-V shapes, buried inside the rotor laminations. The steel bridges around the slots hold the magnets against centrifugal force, protect them from demagnetising fields and, importantly, create a rotor whose magnetic properties differ in different directions. That difference produces a second source of torque, *reluctance torque*, explained below. Almost all modern PMSM traction motors are IPM machines.

### The torque equation

Motor engineers analyse synchronous machines in a reference frame that rotates with the rotor, using two axes: the *d-axis*, aligned with the magnet's north pole, and the *q-axis*, 90 electrical degrees ahead of it. Chapter 9 explains this transformation in detail. For now we need only one result. The torque of an IPM machine is:

$$ T = \frac{3}{2}\, p \left[\, \psi_m\, i_q + (L_d - L_q)\, i_d\, i_q \right] $$ {7.6}

Here *p* is the number of pole pairs, *ψ*~m~ is the flux linkage from the magnets, *i*~d~ and *i*~q~ are the components of stator current along the two axes (peak values), and *L*~d~ and *L*~q~ are the inductances along them.

The first term is the *magnet torque*: the force between the magnet field and the q-axis current, just as in Equation 7.1. The second is the *reluctance torque*. Because the magnets are poor magnetic conductors, the d-axis path through them has a lower inductance than the q-axis path through steel, so *L*~d~ < *L*~q~ in an IPM machine. Applying a *negative* d-axis current then produces additional positive torque, as the rotor tries to align its low-reluctance path with the stator field. In a well-designed IPM traction motor, reluctance torque can supply a third or more of the peak torque, which reduces the magnet mass needed.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.4 — Magnet and reluctance torque**

An IPM motor has four pole pairs, *ψ*~m~ = 0.08 Wb, *L*~d~ = 0.15 mH and *L*~q~ = 0.45 mH. It is supplied with peak currents *i*~q~ = 400 A and *i*~d~ = −200 A. Find the torque and its split between magnet and reluctance torque.

*Solution.* Magnet term: *ψ*~m~*i*~q~ = 0.08 × 400 = 32.

Reluctance term: (*L*~d~ − *L*~q~)*i*~d~*i*~q~ = (0.15 − 0.45) × 10^−3^ × (−200) × 400 = 24.

*T* = 1.5 × 4 × (32 + 24) = 336 N·m.

Magnet torque: 6 × 32 = 192 N·m (57 %). Reluctance torque: 6 × 24 = 144 N·m (43 %).

With no d-axis current, the same q-axis current would give only 192 N·m. Choosing the best split of current between the axes to get maximum torque per amp is a central part of motor control, covered in Chapter 9.
:::

### Back-EMF and the speed limit

A spinning PMSM is also a generator. Its magnets induce a voltage in the stator windings proportional to speed, the *back-EMF*:

$$ e_{pk} = \omega_e\, \psi_m $$ {7.7}

where *ω*~e~ = *p ω*~m~ is the electrical angular speed. The inverter must apply a voltage larger than this to push current into the motor. At high speed, the back-EMF exceeds what the battery can provide, and the drive must use negative d-axis current to *weaken* the effective field. This is the field-weakening region of Chapter 2's constant-power curve.

::: example
**Worked Example 7.5 — Back-EMF at top speed**

The motor in Example 7.4 runs at 16 000 rpm. What is its peak phase back-EMF, and how does the line-to-line peak compare with a 400 V DC link?

*Solution.* Electrical frequency is 1 067 Hz (Example 7.2), so *ω*~e~ = 2π × 1 067 = 6 702 rad/s.

*e*~pk~ = 6 702 × 0.08 = 536 V per phase (peak). The line-to-line peak is √3 × 536 = 929 V.

A 400 V battery can produce a peak line-to-line voltage of at most about 400 V from a standard inverter. The motor's own back-EMF at top speed is more than twice that. Without field weakening, the drive would lose control. Worse, if the inverter switches were all turned off at top speed, the back-EMF would drive current through the switches' internal diodes into the battery: an *uncontrolled generator* fault that the system must be designed to survive. We return to this in Chapter 11.
:::

### Magnets and the rare-earth question

Sintered NdFeB magnets have the highest energy product of any commercial magnet, which is why they dominate. But they have two problems. First, their *coercivity*, their resistance to demagnetisation, falls as temperature rises. Above about 150 °C, a strong opposing field from the stator, such as during a short circuit, can permanently weaken them. To raise high-temperature coercivity, manufacturers add heavy rare earths, dysprosium or terbium, which are scarcer and more expensive still. Grain-boundary diffusion techniques now place these elements only where they are needed, greatly reducing their use.

Second, rare-earth mining and, especially, processing are concentrated geographically, with a single country dominating global refining. Price spikes and export controls have made vehicle manufacturers nervous, and there is intense effort on three fronts: reducing heavy rare earths, reducing total magnet mass through better use of reluctance torque, and designing machines with no rare earths at all.

## Wound-Rotor Synchronous Motors

One way to keep the advantages of a synchronous machine without permanent magnets is to create the rotor field with a DC winding on the rotor. This is the *electrically excited synchronous motor* (EESM), also called the wound-rotor synchronous motor. It is how most power-station generators work.

The rotor current must be delivered to a spinning rotor. Traditionally this is done through *slip rings* and brushes, which wear slowly but need some maintenance. Newer designs use *contactless* power transfer: an inductive or capacitive coupler, or a small rotating transformer, feeding a rectifier on the rotor.

The EESM's special advantage is that its field is *adjustable*. At high speed, the controller simply reduces the rotor current instead of using stator current to fight a fixed magnet field. This gives excellent efficiency at high speed and light load, the motorway cruising condition where PMSMs lose out to their own magnets. At standstill and low speed, the rotor copper losses reduce efficiency somewhat. Several manufacturers use EESMs as their primary traction motor, and interest is growing as rare-earth concerns grow.

## Reluctance Machines

### Switched reluctance motors

The *switched reluctance motor* (SRM) has salient poles on both stator and rotor, no magnets and no windings on the rotor, which is just a shaped stack of laminations. Torque arises entirely from the rotor's tendency to align with whichever stator pole is energised. By switching current from pole to pole in sequence, the controller pulls the rotor round.

SRMs are cheap, rugged and tolerant of high temperatures and high speeds. Their great drawbacks are torque ripple and acoustic noise, because the force pulses as each pole is switched, and the radial forces excite the stator housing. They also need a non-standard inverter. Despite repeated efforts, they have not found wide use in passenger cars, though they appear in some commercial applications.

### Synchronous reluctance and PM-assisted reluctance

The *synchronous reluctance motor* (SynRel) uses a conventional three-phase stator with a rotor designed to have a very large difference between *L*~d~ and *L*~q~, achieved by cutting multiple *flux barriers*, curved air slots, through the laminations, as in Figure 7.3(d). It produces only reluctance torque, from the second term of Equation 7.6. Pure SynRels have a low power factor, which means larger inverter currents for a given power, but they need no magnets.

Adding a modest quantity of magnets in the flux barriers creates a *PM-assisted synchronous reluctance* machine. The magnets improve the power factor and torque, and they can be cheap ferrite magnets rather than rare earths, or a much smaller quantity of NdFeB. In practice, the dividing line between a PM-assisted SynRel and an IPM machine with strong reluctance torque is a matter of degree rather than kind, and many modern traction motors sit somewhere along that continuum.

## Other Configurations

**Axial flux motors.** In the machines discussed so far, the magnetic flux crosses the air gap radially. In an *axial flux* machine, the rotor and stator are discs, and flux crosses the gap parallel to the shaft. Axial flux machines are short and wide, with high torque density, because the torque-producing surface sits at a large radius. They suit applications where axial length is limited, such as hybrid transmissions or in-wheel installations, and several high-performance cars now use them. Manufacturing the stator and holding a precise air gap across a large disc are their main challenges.

**In-wheel and hub motors.** Placing motors inside the wheels eliminates the driveshafts and differential and frees up space, and it gives perfect torque control at each wheel. But it adds *unsprung mass*, which degrades ride and road holding, it exposes the motor to shock, water and road salt, and it requires the motor to produce wheel torque directly without reduction gearing, which makes it large and heavy. Hub motors are common on bicycles and scooters and in some buses and concept cars, but rare in production cars.

**Multi-motor layouts.** Many vehicles use two motors, one per axle, for all-wheel drive and higher performance; some use three or four, allowing torque vectoring between wheels on the same axle. Splitting the power between two machines also lets each be optimised for a different role, as the following Case File shows.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Gradual loss of peak torque after hard use**

*Symptom.* A performance car that has been used on a track shows reduced acceleration. The drive unit reports no fault, but logged data show that, for the same current, torque is about 8 % lower than when new, and the measured back-EMF constant is correspondingly lower.

*Likely causes.* (1) Partial irreversible demagnetisation of rotor magnets after operation at excessive rotor temperature combined with strong negative d-axis current. (2) An error in the rotor position sensor offset after service, which reduces torque for the same current magnitude. (3) Inverter current-sensor gain drift. (4) Software derating after a thermal event.

*How to tell them apart.* Measure the open-circuit back-EMF by spinning the motor with the inverter disabled; a lower value than the specification indicates demagnetisation. If the back-EMF is normal, check the position sensor calibration and the current sensors. Demagnetisation is permanent and requires rotor or motor replacement; it also suggests that the rotor temperature estimate in the control software should be reviewed.
:::

::: casefile
**Case File 7 — The front motor that cost range**

*The complaint.* A manufacturer develops an all-wheel-drive version of its rear-drive saloon by adding a second, identical IPM drive unit on the front axle. In testing, the all-wheel-drive car uses 9 % more energy on the motorway than the rear-drive car, against a target of no more than 4 %.

*State it in numbers.* At a steady 120 km/h, the rear-drive car uses 17.0 kWh per 100 km; the all-wheel-drive car uses 18.5. The extra 1.5 kWh per 100 km at 120 km/h corresponds to an additional continuous power of 1.5 × 1.2 = 1.8 kW. The extra 95 kg of the front unit accounts for at most 0.2 kW at that speed.

*Draw the boundary.* The front drive unit: motor, gearbox, inverter, and its control strategy.

*Simplest model.* At steady cruise, the rear motor can easily supply all the required torque, so the control strategy sends zero torque to the front. But the front IPM motor still spins at around 11 000 rpm with its magnets sweeping past the stator teeth, causing iron losses even with no current. In addition, the gearbox churns its oil, and bearings and seals add friction. A loss of 1 to 1.5 kW from these sources in an unpowered high-speed IPM machine is plausible.

*Measure.* Spin-down tests on a dynamometer confirm a drag of about 1.4 kW at the relevant speed, three-quarters of it from iron losses in the stator due to the magnets.

*Dominant term.* Magnet-induced iron losses in the idle front motor.

*Fix and cross-check.* Three options are evaluated: a mechanical disconnect clutch on the front axle, so the motor stops spinning when not needed; replacing the front IPM motor with an induction motor, which produces no field when unpowered; or a wound-rotor motor, whose field can be switched off. The clutch adds cost and complicates the sudden reconnection when the driver demands full power. The team chooses an induction motor for the front axle, accepting slightly lower peak efficiency there, since it works only during acceleration and on slippery roads. Motorway consumption penalty falls to 3 %. Cross-checks confirm that the induction motor's peak power and thermal capacity are sufficient for repeated launches.

*Lesson.* The most efficient motor when it is working is not necessarily the most efficient choice when it is mostly idle. Match the machine to its duty cycle, not to its peak efficiency figure.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Torque arises from the Lorentz force on current-carrying conductors in a magnetic field. Torque is proportional to rotor volume times air-gap shear stress, so compact motors need high shear stress or high speed.
- Three-phase windings fed with three-phase currents create a rotating magnetic field at synchronous speed *n*~s~ = 60*f*/*p*.
- Induction motors induce rotor currents through slip; rotor copper loss equals slip times air-gap power. They need no magnets and freewheel with little drag.
- Interior permanent-magnet machines combine magnet torque and reluctance torque, offering the highest efficiency and torque density. Back-EMF rises with speed and forces field weakening.
- NdFeB magnets lose coercivity when hot; heavy rare earths improve this but raise supply concerns.
- Wound-rotor synchronous machines provide an adjustable field and avoid rare earths. Reluctance machines rely on rotor saliency; PM-assisted SynRel designs bridge to IPM machines.
- Axial flux, in-wheel and multi-motor layouts suit particular packaging and performance needs.
- Choose the machine to suit the duty cycle: idle losses can matter as much as peak efficiency.
:::

In 1873 James Clerk Maxwell, the Scottish physicist whose equations unified electricity, magnetism and light, published his great *Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*. Tucked away among its pages of profound theory is a very practical suggestion. Instead of thinking about the current in every wire of a network, Maxwell proposed imagining a current circulating round each closed loop of the network, like water swirling in the cells of an ice-cube tray. The real current in any wire is then simply the combination of the circulating currents on either side of it.

Maxwell's "cyclic currents" are what we now call **mesh currents**, and the method built on them is **mesh analysis**. It is the natural partner of nodal analysis. Where nodal analysis uses node voltages as its unknowns and writes KCL, mesh analysis uses mesh currents and writes KVL. Each method is the mirror image of the other, and a well-equipped engineer is comfortable with both, choosing whichever gives fewer equations or a neater solution for the circuit in hand.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- explain what a mesh current is and why mesh currents satisfy KCL automatically;
- assign mesh currents and write KVL equations in terms of them;
- write mesh equations directly in matrix form "by inspection";
- handle current sources on the outside of a circuit;
- find branch currents, voltages and powers from mesh currents;
- compare mesh and nodal analysis and choose between them.
:::

## 11.1 The Big Idea: Currents That Go Round in Circles

Recall from Chapter 4 that a **mesh** is a loop with no other loop inside it – one "pane" of the window, when the circuit is drawn flat. To each mesh we assign a **mesh current**: an imaginary current that circulates round the boundary of that mesh. By convention we draw every mesh current **clockwise**.

![Figure 11.1 — Mesh currents I~1~ and I~2~ circulate clockwise in the two meshes. R~3~ lies on the boundary between them and carries both.](fig/ch11_two_mesh.svg){width=72%}

Look at Figure 11.1. A resistor that lies on the outside edge of the circuit, such as R~1~, belongs to only one mesh, so its current is simply that mesh current: I~1~. A resistor on the boundary between two meshes, such as R~3~, has both mesh currents flowing through it, in **opposite directions** (because both circulate clockwise). Its actual current is the difference:

::: keyeq
**Current in a shared branch**

I~branch~ = I~1~ − I~2~   (in the direction of I~1~)
:::

Why is this a good idea? Because **mesh currents satisfy KCL automatically**. Each mesh current enters a node and leaves it again as it circulates, so it contributes nothing to the net current at any node. KCL is built in, and we only need to write KVL – one equation per mesh. This is precisely the mirror of nodal analysis, where KVL was built in and we only wrote KCL.

The number of unknowns is the number of meshes, which from Chapter 4 is m = b − n + 1. For many circuits that is a small number.

## 11.2 The Method, Step by Step

1. **Check the circuit is planar**, that is, it can be drawn flat without crossing wires. (Almost all the circuits you will meet are.)
2. **Identify the meshes** and assign a clockwise mesh current to each: I~1~, I~2~, I~3~ and so on.
3. **Write KVL round each mesh**, walking clockwise in the direction of that mesh current. For each resistor:
   - if it belongs only to this mesh, its voltage drop is R × I~this~;
   - if it is shared with another mesh, its voltage drop is R × (I~this~ − I~other~).
   For each voltage source, add or subtract its voltage according to the polarity you meet as you walk round.
4. **Solve** the simultaneous equations for the mesh currents.
5. **Find branch currents**, voltages and powers, and **check**.

The rule in Step 3 is the key, and it is the exact counterpart of the "(V~here~ − V~there~)" rule of nodal analysis. When you are writing the equation for mesh k, you always take *your own* mesh current minus the neighbour's. When you move to the neighbouring mesh, you do the same from its point of view. The signs look after themselves.

For sources, use the rule from Chapter 5. Walking clockwise, if you enter a source at its − terminal and leave at + (a rise), it contributes −E to the "sum of drops = 0" equation; if you enter at + (a drop), it contributes +E. Equivalently, move all the sources to the right-hand side and write "sum of resistor drops = sum of source rises in the direction of the mesh current".

## 11.3 A First Example

::: example
**Worked Example 11.1 — The two batteries, once more**

Solve the circuit of Figure 11.1 by mesh analysis. (It is the circuit of Worked Examples 5.7 and 10.2.)

**Step 1: Mesh currents.** I~1~ clockwise in the left mesh, I~2~ clockwise in the right mesh.

**Step 2: KVL in mesh 1**, walking clockwise from the bottom-left corner:

- up through the 10 V source from − to +: a rise, −10;
- through R~1~ (only in mesh 1): +2I~1~;
- down through R~3~ (shared): +4(I~1~ − I~2~).

−10 + 2I~1~ + 4(I~1~ − I~2~) = 0,   so   6I~1~ − 4I~2~ = 10    (1)

**Step 3: KVL in mesh 2**, walking clockwise from the bottom of R~3~:

- up through R~3~ (shared), in the direction of I~2~: +4(I~2~ − I~1~);
- through R~2~ (only in mesh 2): +1I~2~;
- down through the 5 V source, entering at its + terminal: a drop, +5.

4(I~2~ − I~1~) + I~2~ + 5 = 0,   so   −4I~1~ + 5I~2~ = −5    (2)

**Step 4: Solve with Cramer's rule.** Δ = (6)(5) − (−4)(−4) = 30 − 16 = 14.

I~1~ = {{frac|(10)(5) − (−4)(−5)|14}} = {{frac|50 − 20|14}} = {{frac|30|14}} = 2.143 A

I~2~ = {{frac|(6)(−5) − (10)(−4)|14}} = {{frac|−30 + 40|14}} = {{frac|10|14}} = 0.714 A

**Step 5: Branch currents.**

- R~1~ carries I~1~ = 2.143 A.
- R~2~ carries I~2~ = 0.714 A clockwise, that is, from A towards the 5 V source, which is therefore being charged.
- R~3~ carries I~1~ − I~2~ = 1.429 A downwards.

All three agree with Chapters 5 and 10. Three methods, one answer.
:::

## 11.4 Mesh Equations by Inspection

Look at equations (1) and (2) again:

::: eq
(2 + 4) I~1~ − 4 I~2~ = 10

−4 I~1~ + (4 + 1) I~2~ = −5
:::

The pattern mirrors the one we found in nodal analysis:

- The coefficient of a mesh's **own** current is the **sum of all the resistances round that mesh**.
- The coefficient of a **neighbouring** mesh current is **minus the resistance shared** with that mesh (zero if they share nothing).
- The right-hand side is the **sum of the source voltages that push current in the clockwise direction** round that mesh. A source pushes clockwise if, walking clockwise, you pass through it from − to +.

::: keyeq
**Mesh equations by inspection**

[ R ] [ I ] = [ V ]

R~kk~ = total resistance round mesh k;   R~kj~ = −(resistance shared by meshes k and j);   V~k~ = sum of source voltages driving clockwise round mesh k.
:::

With all mesh currents clockwise, the resistance matrix is always symmetric, with positive diagonal entries and negative or zero off-diagonals. Again, a useful check.

![Figure 11.2 — A three-mesh circuit. R~2~ is shared by meshes 1 and 2; R~4~ by meshes 2 and 3.](fig/ch11_three_mesh.svg){width=100%}

::: example
**Worked Example 11.2 — Three meshes by inspection**

Find the mesh currents in Figure 11.2, every branch current, and the power in each source.

**Step 1: Resistance matrix.**

- Mesh 1: R~1~ + R~2~ = 1 + 4 = 5 Ω. Shares R~2~ = 4 Ω with mesh 2.
- Mesh 2: R~2~ + R~3~ + R~4~ = 4 + 1 + 6 = 11 Ω. Shares 4 Ω with mesh 1 and R~4~ = 6 Ω with mesh 3.
- Mesh 3: R~4~ + R~5~ = 6 + 2 = 8 Ω. Shares 6 Ω with mesh 2.

**Step 2: Source vector.**

- Mesh 1: walking clockwise, we go up through E~1~ from − to +, so it drives clockwise: +30.
- Mesh 2: no sources: 0.
- Mesh 3: walking clockwise, we go *down* through E~2~, entering at +. It opposes the clockwise direction: −10.

**Step 3: The equations.**

{{mat|5,−4,0;−4,11,−6;0,−6,8}} {{mat|I~1~;I~2~;I~3~}} = {{mat|30;0;−10}}

**Step 4: Solve.** From the third equation, I~3~ = (−10 + 6I~2~)/8. From the first, I~1~ = (30 + 4I~2~)/5. Substitute both into the second:

−4 × {{frac|30 + 4I~2~|5}} + 11I~2~ − 6 × {{frac|−10 + 6I~2~|8}} = 0

Multiply by 40 to clear the fractions:

−32(30 + 4I~2~) + 440I~2~ − 30(−10 + 6I~2~) = 0

−960 − 128I~2~ + 440I~2~ + 300 − 180I~2~ = 0

132I~2~ = 660,   so   **I~2~ = 5 A**

Then I~1~ = (30 + 20)/5 = **10 A** and I~3~ = (−10 + 30)/8 = **2.5 A**.

**Step 5: Branch currents.**

- R~1~: I~1~ = 10 A. R~3~: I~2~ = 5 A. R~5~: I~3~ = 2.5 A.
- R~2~: I~1~ − I~2~ = 5 A, downwards.
- R~4~: I~2~ − I~3~ = 2.5 A, downwards.

**Step 6: Source powers.** E~1~ carries I~1~ = 10 A out of its + terminal: it delivers 30 × 10 = 300 W. E~2~ carries I~3~ = 2.5 A *into* its + terminal (clockwise, downwards): it absorbs 10 × 2.5 = 25 W.

**Step 7: Power balance.** Resistors: 10² × 1 = 100 W; 5² × 4 = 100 W; 5² × 1 = 25 W; 2.5² × 6 = 37.5 W; 2.5² × 2 = 12.5 W; total 275 W. Add the 25 W absorbed by E~2~: 300 W, equal to the 300 W delivered by E~1~ ✓.
:::

## 11.5 Current Sources on the Outside

What if a mesh contains a current source? The easy case is when the current source lies on the **outer boundary** of the circuit, so that it belongs to only one mesh. Then that mesh current must equal the source current (with a sign depending on direction), and it is no longer an unknown. We simply do not write KVL for that mesh – which is just as well, because we would not know the voltage across the current source.

![Figure 11.3 — A current source on the outer edge of the circuit belongs only to mesh 2, so it fixes I~2~ directly.](fig/ch11_current_source.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 11.3 — A current source fixes a mesh current**

In Figure 11.3, find both mesh currents, the current in R~2~ and the voltage across the current source.

**Step 1: Mesh 2 is fixed.** The 3 A source points downwards on the right-hand edge, which is the clockwise direction for mesh 2. So **I~2~ = 3 A**.

**Step 2: KVL in mesh 1** (the only unknown):

−12 + 2I~1~ + 4(I~1~ − I~2~) = 0

−12 + 2I~1~ + 4I~1~ − 12 = 0,   so   6I~1~ = 24   and   **I~1~ = 4 A**

**Step 3: Current in R~2~.** I~1~ − I~2~ = 4 − 3 = 1 A, downwards.

**Step 4: Voltage across the current source.** Now that we know the currents, we can find the node voltages, taking the bottom rail as 0 V. Node A is at the top of R~2~: V~A~ = 4 × 1 = 4 V. Node B is reached from A through R~3~, which carries I~2~ = 3 A from A to B, so V~B~ = 4 − 2 × 3 = −2 V. The current source therefore has −2 V at its top and 0 V at its bottom.

**Comment.** The source pushes 3 A downwards, from −2 V up to 0 V – from lower to higher potential – so it is *delivering* power: 3 × 2 = 6 W. Check: the 12 V source delivers 12 × 4 = 48 W, making 54 W in total; the resistors absorb 2 × 4² + 4 × 1² + 2 × 3² = 32 + 4 + 18 = 54 W ✓.
:::

When a current source sits on a branch *shared* between two meshes, the situation is a little more involved, because neither mesh current is fixed by itself. The source fixes only the *difference* between them. That leads to the idea of a **supermesh**, which we take up in Chapter 12, together with its nodal counterpart, the supernode.

## 11.6 Mesh or Nodal?

You now have two systematic methods that will solve any planar linear circuit. Which should you use? The honest answer is: whichever gives fewer, simpler equations. Here are the main considerations.

**Count the unknowns.** Nodal analysis needs n − 1 equations (fewer if some node voltages are fixed by sources). Mesh analysis needs b − n + 1 equations (fewer if some mesh currents are fixed by sources). Count both and choose the smaller.

**Look at the sources.** Voltage sources connected to the reference node make nodal analysis easier, because they fix node voltages. Current sources on the outer edge make mesh analysis easier, because they fix mesh currents. Many voltage sources generally favour mesh analysis; many current sources generally favour nodal analysis – but always check the count.

**Consider what you want.** If you want voltages, nodal analysis gives them directly. If you want currents in particular loops – such as the current through a load – mesh analysis may get there faster.

**Planarity.** Mesh analysis only works for planar circuits. Nodal analysis works for any circuit, which is one reason simulators use it.

::: example
**Worked Example 11.4 — Choosing a method**

For the three-mesh circuit of Figure 11.2, how many equations would each method need?

**Nodes.** Taking the bottom rail as reference, the node at the top of E~1~ is fixed at 30 V and the node at the top of E~2~ is fixed at 10 V. The unknown nodes are the two junctions at the tops of R~2~ and R~4~. So nodal analysis needs **two** equations.

**Meshes.** There are three meshes and no current sources: **three** equations.

**Verdict.** Nodal analysis would have been quicker for this circuit. (Exercise 6 asks you to confirm this.) Mesh analysis was used here because this is the mesh chapter – but in an examination, a minute spent counting can save ten minutes of algebra.
:::

Chapter 17 brings all the methods together into a single decision strategy.

::: tip
**Duality Again**

Put the two methods side by side and the symmetry is striking. Node voltages ↔ mesh currents. KCL ↔ KVL. Conductance matrix ↔ resistance matrix. Voltage sources to reference fix nodes ↔ current sources on the outside fix meshes. Supernodes ↔ supermeshes. Learn one method properly and you have learned most of the other.
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Mixing clockwise and anticlockwise mesh currents.** Keep them all clockwise and the matrix stays symmetric.
- **Getting the shared-resistor term the wrong way round.** In mesh k's equation it is always R(I~k~ − I~neighbour~).
- **Source signs.** A source drives clockwise if you pass through it from − to + when walking clockwise.
- **Writing KVL round a mesh containing a current source.** You do not know its voltage; use the source to fix the mesh current instead.
- **Forgetting that branch currents are differences.** The current in a shared resistor is not I~1~ or I~2~ alone.
- **Applying mesh analysis to a non-planar circuit.** Use nodal analysis instead.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A **mesh current** circulates round a mesh. Branch currents are mesh currents (outer branches) or differences of mesh currents (shared branches).
- Mesh currents satisfy **KCL automatically**, so only KVL is needed: one equation per mesh.
- **Method:** assign clockwise mesh currents, write KVL round each mesh using R(I~this~ − I~other~) for shared resistors, solve, then find branch quantities and check.
- **By inspection**: [R][I] = [V], with total mesh resistance on the diagonal, minus shared resistances off it, and clockwise-driving source voltages on the right.
- A **current source on the outer edge** fixes its mesh current.
- Choose between mesh and nodal analysis by **counting equations**; mesh analysis needs a **planar** circuit.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

I~shared~ = I~1~ − I~2~     Σ V = 0 round each mesh     [R][I] = [V]     number of meshes m = b − n + 1
:::

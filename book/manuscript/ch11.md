# The Traction Inverter

::: chapter-intro
The traction inverter sits between the battery and the motor and handles every kilowatt that drives the wheels or comes back through regenerative braking. This chapter explains how a three-phase inverter synthesises AC from DC, compares the main modulation methods, deals with dead time and DC-link capacitor sizing, and looks at efficiency across real driving. It then turns to the hardware inside the box, the fault reactions that keep the vehicle safe when things go wrong, and the alternative topologies now appearing in 800 V vehicles.
:::

If the battery is the heart of an electric vehicle and the motor its muscle, the inverter is its nervous system and its hands combined. It decides, thousands of times a second, exactly which voltage to place on each motor terminal. It measures the currents that result, adjusts, measures again. It protects itself, the motor and the battery from overcurrent, overvoltage and overheating. And when something goes badly wrong, such as a short circuit or a loss of battery connection at motorway speed, it must choose a safe course of action within microseconds.

In this chapter I draw together the motor control of Chapter 9 and the devices of Chapter 10 and show how they become a working product. It is the longest chain of reasoning in the book so far, so it is worth keeping Figure 1.2 in mind: the inverter is one box among many, and nearly every decision about it affects its neighbours.

## The Three-Phase Voltage-Source Inverter

Almost every traction inverter uses the same basic circuit, shown in Figure 11.1: three *half-bridges*, or *legs*, connected across a DC-link capacitor. Each leg has an upper and a lower switch, and the midpoint of each leg connects to one motor phase. Because the input is a stiff voltage, held up by the battery and the capacitor, the circuit is called a *voltage-source inverter* (VSI).

![**Figure 11.1** The two-level three-phase voltage-source inverter. Each leg connects its motor phase either to the positive or to the negative DC rail. The DC-link capacitor supplies the pulsed currents drawn by the switches.](figures/fig11_1.png)

The two switches in a leg must never be on at the same time, because that would short-circuit the DC link through them, an event called *shoot-through*. So each leg has two useful states: upper switch on, connecting the phase to the positive rail, or lower switch on, connecting it to the negative rail. With three legs, there are 2^3^ = 8 possible switching states. Six of them apply a voltage across the motor; two, with all upper or all lower switches on, connect all three phases together and apply zero voltage. The inverter makes its output by choosing among these eight states, rapidly, in carefully timed proportions.

Because each phase terminal can only ever be at one of two voltages, this is called a *two-level* inverter. Its output voltage is a series of pulses, and the motor's inductance filters the pulses so that the *current* is nearly sinusoidal. What matters for control is the *average* voltage on each terminal over one switching period, and that is set by the duty cycle of each leg.

## Pulse-Width Modulation

### Sinusoidal PWM

The simplest modulation method compares a sinusoidal *reference* for each phase with a high-frequency triangular *carrier*. When the reference is above the carrier, the upper switch is on; when below, the lower switch is on. The result, shown in Figure 11.2, is a pulse train whose local average follows the reference.

![**Figure 11.2** Sinusoidal PWM. (a) A sinusoidal reference is compared with a triangular carrier. (b) The resulting leg output switches between the DC rails; its average over each carrier period follows the reference. The carrier frequency has been reduced here for clarity.](figures/fig11_2.png)

The *modulation index* *m* is the ratio of the reference amplitude to the carrier amplitude. With the leg voltage measured from the midpoint of the DC link, the peak fundamental phase voltage is *m V*~DC~/2. Sinusoidal PWM stays linear up to *m* = 1, so the maximum peak phase voltage is *V*~DC~/2.

### Space-vector PWM and third-harmonic injection

That limit wastes some of the available voltage. The motor's star point floats; it does not have to sit at the DC-link midpoint. If we add the same voltage to all three phase references, the line-to-line voltages, which are all the motor sees, are unchanged. So we can add a *common-mode* component, cleverly chosen, that flattens the tops of the phase references and lets their fundamental grow larger before any of them hits the rails.

Adding a third harmonic of one-sixth amplitude achieves this, as does the method engineers more commonly implement, *space-vector PWM* (SVPWM). SVPWM treats the eight switching states as eight space vectors, six of which form a hexagon in the α–β plane (Figure 11.3), with two zero vectors at the centre. Any reference voltage vector inside the hexagon can be produced, on average, by spending the right fractions of each switching period on the two adjacent active vectors and on the zero vectors. The largest circle that fits inside the hexagon has a radius of *V*~DC~/√3, and that is the maximum peak phase voltage in the linear range: 15.5 % more than sinusoidal PWM.

![**Figure 11.3** The space-vector hexagon. The six active switching states (labelled by the states of the three upper switches) form a hexagon; the two zero states sit at the centre. A reference vector in sector I is synthesised from the adjacent vectors V~1~ and V~2~ plus zero vectors. The inscribed circle is the linear modulation limit.](figures/fig11_3.png)

Beyond the inscribed circle, the inverter can enter *overmodulation*, where the reference is clipped by the hexagon and the output contains low-order harmonics, and eventually *six-step* operation, where each switch is on for exactly half a cycle. Six-step gives the largest possible fundamental, a peak phase voltage of 2*V*~DC~/π, but with significant harmonic distortion. Traction drives use overmodulation in the field-weakening region to squeeze out a few more per cent of voltage at high speed, which translates into more torque or lower current.

::: example
**Worked Example 11.1 — How much voltage can the motor have?**

For a 400 V DC link, find the maximum RMS line-to-line voltage available to the motor with sinusoidal PWM, SVPWM and six-step operation.

*Solution.* Peak phase voltages: SPWM *V*~DC~/2 = 200 V; SVPWM *V*~DC~/√3 = 231 V; six-step 2*V*~DC~/π = 255 V.

Line-to-line RMS = peak phase × √3 / √2:

SPWM: 200 × 1.225 = 245 V.
SVPWM: 231 × 1.225 = 283 V.
Six-step: 255 × 1.225 = 312 V.

The step from SPWM to SVPWM is free, apart from a little computation, which is why every traction drive uses SVPWM or an equivalent. The step to six-step adds another 10 %, but at the cost of current harmonics and therefore extra motor loss and torque ripple. Remember also that *V*~DC~ is not constant: at low state of charge under heavy load, a nominal 400 V pack may sag to 300 V, cutting all these figures by a quarter.
:::

### Discontinuous PWM

In SVPWM, the time spent in the zero states can be split between the "all upper on" and "all lower on" states in any proportion. If, during part of each cycle, we use only one of them, one leg can stay clamped to a rail without switching. *Discontinuous PWM* (DPWM) methods do this for 60° or 120° of each electrical cycle, reducing the number of switching events by up to a third. Clamping is best done while the phase current is near its peak, which removes the most switching loss. The price is somewhat higher current ripple at a given switching frequency. Many traction drives switch between continuous SVPWM at low speed and DPWM at higher modulation indices to cut losses where they matter most.

## Dead Time

Real switches do not turn off instantly, so the controller cannot turn one switch in a leg off and the other on at the same moment without risking shoot-through. It inserts a short *dead time*, typically 0.5 to 2 μs for IGBTs and 100 to 500 ns for SiC MOSFETs, during which both switches are off. During that interval, the phase current flows through one of the diodes, and which one depends on the current's direction, not on the controller's command.

The effect is a small voltage error on each phase, whose sign follows the sign of the phase current. Averaged over a switching period, its magnitude is approximately:

$$ \Delta V \approx t_d\, f_{sw}\, V_{DC} $$ {11.1}

::: example
**Worked Example 11.2 — Dead-time voltage error**

An IGBT inverter switches at 10 kHz with a dead time of 1.0 μs and a DC link of 400 V. Find the average voltage error per phase. Why does this matter more at low speed than at high speed?

*Solution.* Δ*V* = 1.0 × 10^−6^ × 10 000 × 400 = 4.0 V.

At high speed, the motor's terminal voltage is a couple of hundred volts, so a 4 V error is about 2 %. At very low speed, the required voltage may be only 5 to 10 V, so the same error is a large fraction of the total and distorts the current waveform near each zero crossing, causing torque ripple that the driver may feel as a vibration when creeping. Inverters therefore compensate by adding a correction voltage based on the measured current direction. With SiC devices, dead time can be much shorter, and the error shrinks accordingly.
:::

## The DC-Link Capacitor

The inverter draws current from the DC link in pulses. The battery and its cables have inductance and cannot supply those pulses directly, so the DC-link capacitor supplies the alternating part of the current while the battery supplies the average. The capacitor must be sized for three things: the *ripple current* it carries, which heats it; the *voltage ripple* it permits; and the energy needed to ride through transients and to damp resonances with the battery cable inductance.

In traction inverters, ripple current usually dominates. For SVPWM with peak phase voltage *M V*~DC~/2, the RMS capacitor current is well approximated by:

$$ I_{C,rms} = I_{rms}\sqrt{2M\left[\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4\pi} + \cos^2\varphi\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi} - \frac{9M}{16}\right)\right]} $$ {11.2}

where *I*~rms~ is the RMS phase current.

::: example
**Worked Example 11.3 — Capacitor ripple current**

An inverter supplies 300 A RMS per phase with *M* = 0.9 and cos *φ* = 0.9. Find the RMS current the DC-link capacitor must carry.

*Solution.* Inside the brackets: √3/(4π) = 0.1378; √3/π − 9 × 0.9/16 = 0.5513 − 0.5063 = 0.0451; multiplied by cos^2^ *φ* = 0.81 gives 0.0365. Sum = 0.1743.

Multiply by 2*M* = 1.8: 0.3137. Square root: 0.560.

*I*~C,rms~ = 0.560 × 300 = 168 A.

The capacitor must carry about 170 A RMS continuously at this operating point without overheating. Metallised polypropylene film capacitors can carry a few hundred milliamps of ripple per microfarad at high frequency, so this requirement typically leads to a capacitance of several hundred microfarads, regardless of the voltage ripple. Film capacitors also have a temperature limit of around 105 °C, and their life halves for roughly every 7 to 10 K rise, so their thermal path to the coolant matters.
:::

The capacitor must also be packaged with very low inductance, because it sits in the commutation loop of Chapter 10. Modern inverters integrate the capacitor and a *laminated busbar*, thin sheets of copper separated by insulation, so that the positive and negative paths are as close as possible and their magnetic fields cancel.

## Efficiency in Real Driving

Inverter efficiency is often quoted as a single peak figure, typically 98 to 99.5 %. As with the motor, what matters is efficiency across the drive cycle, and most driving happens at a small fraction of rated power. Figure 11.4 compares typical efficiency curves for IGBT and SiC inverters as a function of load.

![**Figure 11.4** Indicative traction inverter efficiency against output power at a fixed speed. The SiC inverter's advantage is largest at light load, where the IGBT's fixed on-state voltage and switching losses dominate; at full load, the two converge.](figures/fig11_4.png)

At light load, the IGBT's on-state voltage *V*~CE0~ and its relatively high switching energy mean that losses stay significant even when little power is delivered. The SiC MOSFET's resistive conduction loss falls with the square of current and its switching losses are low, so its efficiency stays high down to small loads. Over a WLTP cycle, moving from a silicon IGBT inverter to a SiC MOSFET inverter typically saves several per cent of the energy drawn from the battery, more than the difference in peak efficiencies would suggest. Combined with the benefits for 800 V systems, this is why SiC has spread so quickly.

## Inside the Box

A production traction inverter, often integrated into the drive unit housing, contains:

- **Power module(s)** with six switch positions, cooled by the vehicle's coolant loop.
- **Gate driver board** with isolated drivers, protection and isolated power supplies.
- **Control board** with a microcontroller running the motor control, safety monitoring and communication, plus the resolver interface.
- **Current sensors**, usually Hall-effect or magnetoresistive sensors around the phase busbars, often with a magnetic core.
- **DC-link capacitor** and laminated busbars.
- **EMI filter** at the DC input, with common-mode chokes and Y-capacitors to chassis, to keep switching noise off the high-voltage cables.
- **Active discharge circuit** to bring the DC link below 60 V within seconds of shutdown.
- **High-voltage connectors** for the DC input and, in non-integrated designs, the three-phase output, with interlock loop contacts.
- **Low-voltage connector** for power, CAN or Ethernet, the resolver and wake signals.

Integration of the inverter, motor and gearbox into a single *electric drive unit* removes the three-phase cables and their connectors, reduces cost and mass, and shortens the AC connections, which helps electromagnetic compatibility. It also creates challenges: the inverter must survive the vibration and temperature of the gearbox environment, and serviceability changes, since a failed inverter may mean replacing a larger assembly.

## Fault Reactions and Safety

An inverter must react to faults in ways that keep the vehicle and its occupants safe. Two families of safety concern dominate.

### Safe states for the motor

When a serious fault occurs, such as an overcurrent, a loss of position signal, an overvoltage or a controller failure, the inverter must stop controlling torque and put the motor into a *safe state*. There are two candidates, and choosing between them is one of the subtler parts of drive design.

**Freewheeling (all switches off).** With all six switches off, the motor's phases are connected to the DC link only through the diodes. At low speed, where the back-EMF is below the DC-link voltage, nothing flows and the motor simply coasts. But at high speed, where the back-EMF exceeds the DC-link voltage, as Worked Example 7.5 showed for our motor at 16 000 rpm, the diodes act as a rectifier and the motor drives current into the DC link. This is *uncontrolled generator mode*. It produces an uncontrolled braking torque and can push the DC-link voltage dangerously high, especially if the battery contactors have opened and nothing can absorb the current.

**Active short circuit (ASC).** Turning on all three lower switches (or all three upper switches) short-circuits the motor terminals. The back-EMF then drives current around the shorted windings, limited by their inductance. At high speed, the current settles to roughly the characteristic current, *ψ*~m~/*L*~d~, flowing almost entirely in the negative d-axis, and the braking torque is small. No energy flows to the DC link, so its voltage cannot rise. At low speed, however, ASC produces a substantial braking torque and higher currents relative to the back-EMF, which can feel like a sudden jolt.

::: example
**Worked Example 11.4 — Choosing the safe state**

For the motor of Chapter 7 (*ψ*~m~ = 0.08 Wb, *L*~d~ = 0.15 mH), estimate the steady short-circuit current in ASC at high speed. Should the inverter choose ASC or freewheeling at 16 000 rpm with a 400 V DC link? And at 2 000 rpm?

*Solution.* At high speed, ASC current approaches *ψ*~m~/*L*~d~ = 0.08 / 0.00015 = 533 A peak. If the inverter's switches are rated for, say, 600 A peak, they can carry this indefinitely, with cooling.

At 16 000 rpm the line-to-line back-EMF peak is 929 V (Example 7.5), more than twice the DC link. Freewheeling would cause uncontrolled generation into the DC link. ASC is the correct choice.

At 2 000 rpm the back-EMF peak is about 116 V line-to-line, well below 400 V. Freewheeling causes no current flow and no braking. ASC at this speed would produce a strong braking torque and, briefly, a large transient current. Freewheeling is the better choice.

Most drives therefore select the safe state according to speed: freewheeling below a threshold speed, ASC above it, with transitions designed to avoid large current transients. The fault-reaction logic must work even if the main microcontroller has failed, so it is usually implemented in hardware or in an independent safety circuit.
:::

### Torque safety

The second concern is *unintended torque*: the vehicle accelerating when the driver did not ask for it, or braking hard unexpectedly through regeneration. These hazards are typically rated at high integrity levels under ISO 26262 (ASIL C or D). The industry widely uses a three-level monitoring concept, originally developed for electronic throttles. *Level 1* is the normal control software. *Level 2* is an independent calculation, in a separate part of the processor or a separate processor, that estimates the actual torque from measured currents and compares it with the permitted torque; if they disagree beyond a threshold for longer than a defined time, it triggers the safe state. *Level 3* monitors the processor itself, through question-and-answer exchanges with an external watchdog, to make sure Level 2 is actually running. Chapter 14 returns to functional safety in a wider context.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Sudden jolt and loss of drive at speed**

*Symptom.* At about 110 km/h, the car suddenly loses drive with a short, firm deceleration, and the dashboard shows a powertrain fault. After stopping and restarting, it drives normally.

*Likely causes.* (1) The battery contactors opened unexpectedly (for example after an isolation or interlock fault), the DC link lost its load, and the inverter entered ASC to prevent overvoltage, producing the jolt. (2) A resolver signal fault triggered a safe-state reaction. (3) An overcurrent detection from a gate driver, perhaps caused by noise. (4) A torque-monitoring disagreement in Level 2.

*How to tell them apart.* Read the stored fault codes and freeze-frame data from both the BMS and the inverter, and check their time order: if the BMS reports contactor opening first, the inverter's reaction was a consequence. Look for an interlock loop interruption, often a connector issue, at the same moment. A resolver amplitude fault or a Level 2 torque disagreement points to the inverter side. Intermittent faults of this kind deserve careful attention because the safe-state reaction, though correct, is unpleasant at speed.
:::

## Beyond the Two-Level Inverter

The two-level VSI dominates, but other topologies are appearing, especially for 800 V systems.

**Three-level inverters** add a third output level, the DC-link midpoint, using either a *neutral-point-clamped* (NPC) or a *T-type* arrangement. Each switch then blocks only half the DC-link voltage, so 650 V devices can be used on an 800 V link, and the output waveform has smaller steps, which reduces motor harmonic losses, EMI and stress on winding insulation. The cost is more switches and more complex control, including balancing the midpoint voltage. With SiC MOSFETs readily available at 1 200 V, two-level designs remain more common, but three-level inverters appear in some high-performance and commercial applications.

**Dual inverters** feed an *open-end winding* motor, whose star point is opened, from both ends, with one inverter at each end. This can double the effective voltage across the windings, provide redundancy, or allow the two inverters to be fed from different sources.

**Integrated charging.** The motor windings and the inverter can double as components of the charger. A common example in 800 V vehicles is using the inverter and the motor's inductance as a *boost converter* so that a 400 V DC charging station can charge an 800 V battery, avoiding the need for a separate, heavy DC-DC converter. The motor must not produce torque during this process, which constrains how the windings are used.

**Multi-phase machines** with six or more phases, driven by correspondingly more inverter legs, spread the current across more switches, lower torque ripple and offer fault tolerance, since the motor can continue to run with a phase lost. They are attractive for high-power and safety-critical applications.

::: casefile
**Case File 11 — Noise on the radio**

*The complaint.* Pre-production vehicles fitted with a new SiC inverter fail the electromagnetic compatibility test for the AM broadcast band. Test drivers also report a buzzing on medium-wave radio stations when accelerating.

*State it in numbers.* Measured conducted emissions on the high-voltage DC cables exceed the vehicle's internal limit, derived from CISPR 25, by up to 14 dB between 0.5 and 1.7 MHz. The previous IGBT inverter met the limit with 6 dB margin.

*Draw the boundary.* Noise sources (switching edges, switching frequency harmonics), coupling paths (parasitic capacitances to chassis, the DC cables, the motor and its housing) and the existing filter.

*Simplest model.* The SiC devices switch about five times faster, so their switching edges have much more energy at high frequencies. Common-mode current flows through parasitic capacitances between the switching node and the heatsink, and from the motor windings to the motor housing, and returns through the chassis and the DC cable shields. The old EMI filter was designed for the slower IGBT edges. The fixed switching frequency of 12 kHz also concentrates its harmonics at specific frequencies, some of which fall inside the AM band.

*Measure.* A current probe around both DC cables together, which measures common-mode current, shows the excess is almost entirely common-mode. Inserting a temporary ferrite core around the cables cuts emissions by 10 dB, confirming the path.

*Dominant term.* Common-mode current driven by fast edges, with harmonic concentration from the fixed switching frequency.

*Fix and cross-check.* The team adds a nanocrystalline common-mode choke at the DC input, increases the Y-capacitance to chassis within the limit allowed by the touch-current and isolation-monitoring requirements, adds an insulating layer with a grounded shield between the power module and heatsink to reduce coupling capacitance, and introduces random variation of the switching frequency between 11 and 13 kHz to spread the harmonic energy. Emissions fall to 4 dB below the limit. Cross-checks confirm the isolation monitoring still measures correctly with the added Y-capacitance, and that switching-frequency variation does not create audible noise from the motor.

*Lesson.* Faster switching is not free. EMC must be designed in from the start of a SiC inverter project, not added at the end.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The two-level voltage-source inverter has three legs with eight switching states, six active and two zero; the motor's inductance filters the pulsed voltage into nearly sinusoidal current.
- Sinusoidal PWM gives a maximum peak phase voltage of *V*~DC~/2; SVPWM or third-harmonic injection raises this by 15.5 % to *V*~DC~/√3; overmodulation and six-step provide more at the cost of harmonics.
- Discontinuous PWM clamps legs for part of the cycle to cut switching losses.
- Dead time prevents shoot-through but creates a current-dependent voltage error that must be compensated, especially at low speed.
- The DC-link capacitor is usually sized by ripple current and must be packaged with very low inductance.
- SiC inverters gain most efficiency at light load, which dominates real driving.
- Safe states are chosen by speed: freewheeling at low speed, active short circuit at high speed where back-EMF exceeds the DC link. Torque safety uses independent multi-level monitoring.
- Three-level, dual and multi-phase inverters and integrated charging are growing alternatives, especially at 800 V.
:::

# Vehicle Integration, Thermal Systems and Functional Safety

::: chapter-intro
Good components do not automatically make a good vehicle. This chapter is about bringing them together: the integrated thermal system that heats and cools battery, drive unit and cabin from one set of hardware; the heat pump that has transformed winter range; the energy management software that arbitrates between them; electromagnetic compatibility; and the disciplines of functional safety, safety of the intended functionality and cyber security that make an electric vehicle trustworthy. We finish with crash safety and what happens after an accident.
:::

A friend who manages vehicle programmes once told me that the hardest phase of any project is the moment when all the parts first arrive in one car. Until then, each team has tested its component against its own specification and found it good. Then the car is assembled, and the problems that live *between* components appear: a coolant pump that cannot supply both the battery and the motor at the same time, a charger whose switching noise disturbs the radio, a software timing assumption in one controller that another controller does not meet. Integration is where an EV programme succeeds or fails.

This chapter covers the main integration disciplines. It draws on almost everything earlier in the book, and it is deliberately broad rather than deep, because the skill it describes is seeing connections.

## The Vehicle as a System of Systems

A modern electric car contains dozens of electronic control units and several hundred million lines of software. The traditional approach gave each function its own control unit: one for the BMS, one for the inverter, one for the charger, one for climate control, and so on, all connected by CAN buses. That approach is giving way to a more centralised architecture, with a small number of high-performance *central computers* and *zonal controllers* that collect sensors and drive actuators in each physical region of the car, connected by automotive Ethernet. Functions become software running on shared hardware.

This shift is often called the *software-defined vehicle*. Its practical consequences for EV engineering include *over-the-air (OTA) updates*, which allow manufacturers to improve charging curves, efficiency, range estimates and even power output after the car has been sold, and closer coordination between subsystems, because the energy management, thermal management and powertrain control can run on the same computer with the same data. It also brings new responsibilities: an update that changes how the battery is charged must be validated as carefully as the original software, and the update channel itself must be secure.

## Integrated Thermal Management

### The three thermal loads

An electric vehicle has three main thermal clients, each with different needs:

- **The battery** wants to stay between roughly 15 and 35 °C, with a small temperature spread. It needs cooling during fast charging and hot weather, and heating in winter.
- **The electric drive unit**, motor and inverter, produces waste heat whenever it works, and tolerates coolant temperatures up to 60 or 70 °C.
- **The cabin** wants to be at around 21 °C, with dry air for clear windows, and expects to be heated quickly on a cold morning.

Early EVs served these with separate systems: a battery cooling loop with its own radiator or chiller, a drive-unit loop with its own radiator, and a cabin with an air-conditioning system for cooling and a resistive heater for warmth. Modern designs integrate them into a single system of loops, pumps, valves and heat exchangers, shown schematically in Figure 14.1, so that heat can be moved wherever it is most useful.

![**Figure 14.1** An integrated thermal management architecture. A refrigerant circuit (heat pump) connects to coolant loops for the battery and drive unit through a chiller and a water-cooled condenser. Multi-way valves let the system move waste heat from the drive unit into the battery or cabin, cool the battery below ambient through the chiller, or reject heat at the front-end radiator.](figures/fig14_1.png)

With the right valves, the system can, for example, take waste heat from the inverter and motor and use it to warm the battery before fast charging; take heat from the outside air, the drive unit and even the battery itself and pump it into the cabin; or cool the battery with the chiller while the cabin is heated. A central controller decides the mode moment by moment, based on the needs of each client and the cost in energy of each option.

### The heat pump

The single most important component in winter is the *heat pump*. A heat pump is an air-conditioning system run in reverse: instead of moving heat from the cabin to the outside, it moves heat from the outside air, or from waste-heat sources, into the cabin. Because it moves heat rather than generating it, it can deliver more heat than the electrical energy it consumes. The ratio is the *coefficient of performance* (COP):

$$ \mathrm{COP} = \frac{\dot{Q}_{heat}}{P_{elec}} $$ {14.1}

A resistive PTC heater has a COP of exactly one. A heat pump in mild cold might achieve a COP of 2.5 to 3. As the outside temperature falls, there is less heat to extract and the compressor must work harder, so the COP falls, as Figure 14.2 shows. At around −15 to −20 °C, many heat pumps using the common refrigerant R1234yf approach a COP of 1 and the system supplements them with resistive heating. Heat pumps using carbon dioxide (R744) as the refrigerant work better at very low temperatures, though at higher pressures, and are used in some vehicles for cold climates. Using waste heat from the drive unit and battery as the source, rather than cold outside air, improves the COP considerably.

![**Figure 14.2** Indicative coefficient of performance of an automotive heat pump against ambient temperature, for outside-air and waste-heat-assisted operation. Below about −15 °C the air-source COP approaches that of a resistive heater.](figures/fig14_2.png)

::: example
**Worked Example 14.1 — What a heat pump is worth**

On a 0 °C day, a car needs an average of 3 kW of heat for the cabin. Its base consumption, excluding heating, is 15 kWh per 100 km, and the average speed of the journey is 50 km/h. The usable battery is 60 kWh. Compare range with a PTC heater (COP 1) and a heat pump (COP 2.5).

*Solution.* At 50 km/h, 100 km takes 2 hours.

PTC: heating energy = 3 × 2 = 6 kWh per 100 km. Total consumption 21 kWh per 100 km; range = 60 / 0.21 = 286 km.

Heat pump: electrical power = 3 / 2.5 = 1.2 kW; energy = 2.4 kWh per 100 km. Total 17.4 kWh per 100 km; range = 345 km.

The heat pump adds about 21 % to winter range in this case, and the benefit is largest in slow urban driving, where the heating energy per kilometre is greatest. At motorway speeds, the journey is shorter in time and the benefit shrinks. This is why heat pumps, once an option, have become standard on most mid- and upper-range EVs sold in cold climates.
:::

### Dehumidification and safety of vision

There is one thermal function that must never be compromised: keeping the windscreen clear. Warm, humid air from occupants' breath condenses on cold glass. Air-conditioning systems clear it by cooling the air below its dew point to remove moisture, then reheating it. An integrated system must be able to dehumidify and heat at the same time, even at low temperatures, which places demanding requirements on the heat pump modes and their control. Fogging that blocks the driver's view is a safety hazard, and it is treated as one in the design and validation of the thermal system.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Heat pump stops heating on cold, damp mornings**

*Symptom.* On mornings around 0 to 3 °C with high humidity, cabin heating weakens after about twenty minutes of driving, then recovers, while consumption rises.

*Likely causes.* (1) Ice forming on the outdoor heat exchanger, which in heat pump mode runs below the dew point and below freezing, blocking airflow and reducing heat extraction. The system then switches to resistive heating and runs a defrost cycle. (2) Low refrigerant charge. (3) A faulty expansion valve or sensor. (4) A blocked front grille or active shutter stuck closed.

*How to tell them apart.* Icing is common and expected in these conditions; logs will show falling evaporator pressure and temperature, followed by defrost events. If defrost cycles are frequent at milder conditions, or heating is weak even when dry, check the refrigerant charge and valve operation. Inspect the front end for blockages and confirm that the grille shutters open on command.
:::

## Energy Management

The thermal system, the powertrain and the auxiliaries all draw on the same battery, so something must decide how to allocate energy. That is the job of the *energy management* software, usually in the vehicle control unit or a central computer.

Its decisions include how much power to allow the cabin heater when the battery is low, when to precondition the battery, how to blend regenerative and friction braking, which drive unit to use in a two-motor car, and what range to display. Increasingly it is *predictive*: using the navigation route, its elevation profile, the traffic and the weather forecast to plan ahead. A car that knows it will arrive at a rapid charger in 25 minutes can begin warming its battery at the right moment, using drive-unit waste heat where possible. A car that knows a long descent is coming can avoid charging to full beforehand, so that it can accept regeneration on the way down.

Good energy management is invisible when it works. Its failures are very visible: a range estimate that drops suddenly, a car that arrives at a charger with a cold battery, a cabin that stays cold because the system has decided to protect range without telling the driver. Making the trade-offs transparent to the driver, through clear displays and settings, is as much a human-factors problem as an engineering one.

## Electromagnetic Compatibility

An electric vehicle is full of fast-switching power electronics carrying hundreds of amps a short distance from sensitive radio receivers, sensors and control electronics. *Electromagnetic compatibility* (EMC) is the discipline of making sure the vehicle neither disturbs other equipment nor is disturbed by it.

Two families of requirement apply. *Emissions* standards, such as CISPR 25 for components and CISPR 12 for complete vehicles, limit the conducted and radiated noise the vehicle produces. *Immunity* standards, such as the ISO 11452 series for components and ISO 11451 for vehicles, require the vehicle to keep working correctly when exposed to strong external fields, from broadcast transmitters to the radar of an airport. In Europe and the UK, UN Regulation No. 10 sets the type-approval requirements.

The main techniques were met in Case File 11: minimising the area of high-*di*/*dt* current loops, controlling switching edge rates, filtering at the boundaries of each high-voltage component, shielding high-voltage cables and bonding shields to the chassis at both ends, and keeping a clean, low-impedance chassis reference. A useful habit for any engineer designing a high-voltage component is to ask, for every fast-changing voltage and current in it, "where does the return current flow?" Most EMC problems are answered by that question.

## Functional Safety

### What ISO 26262 asks

Electric vehicles rely on electronic systems for functions whose failure could hurt people: delivering the requested torque and no more, braking by regeneration, preventing battery overcharge, keeping the high-voltage system isolated. *Functional safety* is the absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of electrical and electronic systems. The governing standard for road vehicles is ISO 26262.

ISO 26262 defines a safety lifecycle that runs alongside the development process of Chapter 1's V-model. It begins with a *hazard analysis and risk assessment* (HARA), which identifies how each function could malfunction, in which driving situations, and how bad the outcome could be.

Each hazardous event is rated on three scales:

- **Severity (S0–S3):** from no injuries (S0) to life-threatening or fatal injuries (S3).
- **Exposure (E0–E4):** how often the vehicle is in the situation where the hazard could occur, from incredible (E0) to high probability (E4).
- **Controllability (C0–C3):** whether the driver or others can avoid harm, from controllable in general (C0) to difficult or impossible to control (C3).

The combination determines the *Automotive Safety Integrity Level* (ASIL), from A (lowest) to D (highest), or QM (quality management only, meaning no special safety measures beyond normal good practice). A convenient property of the ISO 26262 table is that, for S1–S3, E1–E4 and C1–C3, the ASIL depends on the sum of the three indices: a sum of 10 gives ASIL D, 9 gives C, 8 gives B, 7 gives A, and 6 or less gives QM. Figure 14.3 shows the flow from hazard to safety requirement.

![**Figure 14.3** From hazard to safety requirements in ISO 26262. Each hazardous event is classified by severity, exposure and controllability; the resulting ASIL sets the rigour of the safety goal and of the functional, technical, hardware and software requirements derived from it.](figures/fig14_3.png)

::: example
**Worked Example 14.2 — Classifying two hazards**

Classify the following hazardous events.

(a) The drive delivers full unintended acceleration while the driver is manoeuvring slowly in a busy car park.

(b) The vehicle applies unexpectedly strong regenerative braking on the rear axle while driving at moderate speed on a road with patches of ice.

*Solution.* (a) Severity: pedestrians could be struck, so S3. Exposure: manoeuvring in car parks is a very common situation, E4. Controllability: a sudden, unexpected full-power surge at close quarters is very difficult for the driver to control, C3. Sum = 10: **ASIL D**.

(b) Severity: loss of control could lead to a collision at moderate speed, S3. Exposure: driving on icy patches is a situation many drivers meet a few times a year or more; a typical rating would be E2 or E3, say E2. Controllability: rear-axle lock-up on ice can cause a spin; with stability control a typical driver can often recover, so C2. Sum = 7: **ASIL A**. If the analysis considered a broader situation, such as any low-friction road (E3), the result would be ASIL B.

These examples show both the method and its subjectivity. The ratings require judgement, and good practice is to document the reasoning and have it reviewed by people who were not involved in the design. The resulting safety goals, "prevent unintended acceleration above a threshold" and "prevent excessive regenerative deceleration on a low-friction surface", then drive the design, as in the three-level torque monitoring of Chapter 11.
:::

### From safety goals to hardware and software

Each safety goal becomes a set of *functional safety requirements*, then *technical safety requirements* allocated to hardware and software. Important concepts include:

- **Fault tolerant time interval (FTTI):** the time between a fault occurring and a hazardous event resulting, if nothing is done. The safety mechanism must detect the fault and reach a safe state within it. For an inverter producing unintended torque, the FTTI may be a few hundred milliseconds; for an overcharge event in a battery, it may be seconds to minutes.
- **Safe state:** the condition the system moves to on a fault, such as zero torque, open contactors, or the active short circuit of Chapter 11.
- **Diagnostic coverage and hardware metrics:** for ASIL D, the standard sets targets such as at least 99 % *single-point fault metric*, at least 90 % *latent fault metric*, and a *probabilistic metric for random hardware failures* below 10 FIT, where one FIT is one failure per 10^9^ hours.
- **ASIL decomposition:** a high-ASIL requirement can sometimes be met by two sufficiently independent elements of lower ASIL, for example ASIL D split into two ASIL B(D) channels.
- **Software development rigour:** coding guidelines, static analysis, defined test coverage levels and independence of verification all scale with ASIL.

::: example
**Worked Example 14.3 — What 10 FIT means**

A vehicle is designed for a service life of 15 years with an average of 1.2 hours of operation per day. If a safety-related hardware function meets an ASIL D target of 10 FIT for violation of its safety goal, what is the probability of such a violation in one vehicle's life? In a fleet of 2 million vehicles?

*Solution.* Operating hours = 15 × 365 × 1.2 = 6 570 h.

Probability ≈ 10 × 10^−9^ × 6 570 = 6.6 × 10^−5^ per vehicle.

Across 2 million vehicles: about 130 expected events over their lives.

That is why ASIL D targets are so demanding, and why the analysis is not the end of the story: field monitoring, root-cause investigation and recalls are part of the safety lifecycle too.
:::

### SOTIF and cyber security

ISO 26262 addresses hazards from *malfunctions*. But a system can also behave hazardously when nothing has failed, because its intended functionality is insufficient for a situation its designers did not foresee: a range estimator that is badly wrong in an unusual climate, or a regenerative braking strategy that behaves poorly on a surface it was never tested on. ISO 21448, *Safety of the Intended Functionality* (SOTIF), provides a framework for identifying and reducing such risks, and it is especially important for automated driving functions.

*Cyber security* has become equally important. A connected EV with OTA updates, remote charging control and app access has an attack surface that a 1990s car never had. A compromised charger, a malicious update or an attack through the telematics unit could, in principle, interfere with safety-relevant functions. ISO/SAE 21434 defines an engineering process for automotive cyber security, and UN Regulations No. 155 and 156 require manufacturers to operate a certified cyber-security management system and a software update management system for vehicles to be type-approved in many markets. For the charging ecosystem, the public-key infrastructure of ISO 15118 Plug & Charge and the security profiles of OCPP extend the same thinking to the grid connection.

::: casefile
**Case File 14 — Lift-off regeneration on black ice**

*The complaint.* During winter testing in Scandinavia, a rear-wheel-drive EV with strong one-pedal regeneration spins on two occasions when the driver lifts off the accelerator on a bend with black ice. Stability control intervenes, but late. No such events occur on dry or wet roads.

*State it in numbers.* Logged lift-off regenerative torque at the time of the events corresponds to 0.15 g of deceleration, applied entirely to the rear axle. On black ice, the available friction coefficient is around 0.1, much of which is already used for cornering. The rear wheels began to slip within 80 ms of lift-off; the stability control system detected the yaw deviation and reduced regenerative torque after about 250 ms.

*Draw the boundary.* The pedal map and regenerative braking strategy, the drive unit's torque response, the stability control's detection and intervention, and the interface between them.

*Simplest model.* A rear-axle braking force of 0.15 g on a surface that can support perhaps 0.05 g of longitudinal force while cornering will saturate the rear tyres almost immediately. Once the rear tyres lose lateral grip, the car begins to rotate. The stability control system sees this only once yaw deviation builds, by which time the spin has started. The hazard analysis had classified "excessive regenerative deceleration on low friction" at ASIL A, and the safety mechanism relied on stability control alone.

*Measure.* Further tests confirm that wheel slip at the rear axle develops within 50 to 100 ms on ice, faster than the stability control's yaw-based detection.

*Dominant term.* The regenerative braking strategy did not adapt quickly enough to low friction; the safety mechanism acted on the consequence (yaw) rather than the cause (wheel slip).

*Fix and cross-check.* The drive unit's controller is given direct authority to limit regenerative torque when it detects rear wheel slip from wheel-speed and motor-speed signals, reacting within 20 ms. Lift-off regeneration is ramped in over 300 ms rather than applied as a step, and its level is reduced when the outside temperature is below 4 °C and the steering angle is significant. The hazard analysis is revisited: the team concludes the exposure rating should consider all low-friction surfaces (E3), raising the classification to ASIL B, and documents the new safety mechanism accordingly. Re-testing on ice produces no spins. Cross-checks confirm that energy recovery in normal winter driving falls by less than 2 %.

*Lesson.* A safety analysis is a living document. Real-world testing should feed back into it, and the best safety mechanisms act on the earliest available evidence of a problem.
:::

## Crash Safety and the Aftermath

Electric vehicles must meet the same crash standards as any other car, and they have done well in independent crash tests, helped by their low centre of gravity and the stiff battery structure under the floor. They must also meet additional requirements for high-voltage safety in and after a crash, set out in regulations such as UN Regulations No. 94, 95 and 137 for frontal and side impact and FMVSS 305 in the United States. After the test, the high-voltage system must remain protected against electric shock, by low voltage, isolation or physical protection; no more than a limited quantity of electrolyte may spill, and none into the cabin; the battery must stay attached to the vehicle; and there must be no fire or explosion within the observation period.

The vehicle's design supports this through crash-sensor-triggered contactor opening and pyrofuses (Chapter 5), active discharge of the DC link (Chapter 11), and the structural protection of the pack.

What happens after a real crash matters too. Emergency responders need to know how to make the vehicle safe and how to cut it open to free occupants without cutting high-voltage cables. Manufacturers publish standardised *rescue sheets* for every model, following ISO 17840, showing the location of the battery, high-voltage cables, airbags and cut zones, and the procedure to disable the high-voltage system. Damaged batteries can enter thermal runaway hours or even days after a crash, so recovery operators, garages and salvage yards need procedures for monitoring, isolating and storing damaged EVs. The engineering of a vehicle does not end at the factory gate.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Integration problems live between components; modern vehicles centralise computing in central and zonal controllers and rely on over-the-air updates.
- Integrated thermal management uses one refrigerant circuit and coupled coolant loops to serve battery, drive unit and cabin; waste heat can be moved where it is useful.
- Heat pumps deliver more heat than the electricity they use; COP falls with ambient temperature. They can add 15 to 25 % to winter range, especially in urban driving.
- Energy management arbitrates among consumers and increasingly uses predictive information from navigation.
- EMC requires attention to current loops, edge rates, filtering, shielding and bonding.
- ISO 26262 classifies hazards by severity, exposure and controllability into ASILs, which drive safety goals, mechanisms, hardware metrics and software rigour. SOTIF and cyber security (ISO/SAE 21434, UN R155/R156) cover hazards without faults and malicious attacks.
- Crash regulations add high-voltage protection, electrolyte and fire requirements; rescue sheets and post-crash handling complete the safety picture.
:::

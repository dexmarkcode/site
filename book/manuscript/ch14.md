In 1883 Léon Charles Thévenin, an engineer with the French telegraph service, published a short note in the proceedings of the French Academy of Sciences. He was concerned with a practical problem: telegraph lines were long, complicated networks of wires, batteries and instruments, and engineers needed to predict how much current would flow through a receiving instrument connected at some point along a line. Thévenin showed that, as far as that instrument was concerned, the entire network – however complicated – could be replaced by a single battery in series with a single resistance.

(The German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz had actually published the same result thirty years earlier, in a paper on animal electricity. Thévenin was unaware of it. In some countries the theorem is called the Helmholtz–Thévenin theorem, which is fairer, if a little long.)

This result is probably the most useful single idea in circuit analysis. It lets you take a vast, tangled circuit, focus on the one component you care about, and replace everything else by just two numbers. Once you have those two numbers, you can connect any load you like and find its current in a single line of arithmetic. It is how engineers think about batteries, sensors, amplifier outputs, power supplies and almost every interface between one circuit and another. We have glimpsed it twice already: in the internal resistance of a battery (Chapter 3) and in the loaded voltage divider (Chapter 8). Now we meet it properly.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- state Thévenin's theorem and explain what it means;
- find the Thévenin voltage by calculating the open-circuit voltage;
- find the Thévenin resistance by switching off independent sources;
- find the Thévenin resistance using the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current;
- find the Thévenin resistance of circuits with dependent sources using a test source;
- use a Thévenin equivalent to analyse a circuit with a varying load;
- determine a Thévenin equivalent by measurement.
:::

## 14.1 The Theorem

::: keyeq
**Thévenin's theorem**

Any linear two-terminal network of sources and resistors can be replaced, as seen from its terminals, by a single ideal voltage source V~Th~ in series with a single resistance R~Th~.

V~Th~ is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals. R~Th~ is the resistance seen looking into the terminals with all independent sources switched off.
:::

![Figure 14.1 — Thévenin's theorem. However complicated the linear network, it behaves at its terminals a and b exactly like a voltage source V~Th~ in series with a resistance R~Th~.](fig/ch14_concept.svg){width=94%}

The key words are "as seen from its terminals". The Thévenin equivalent tells you nothing about what is happening *inside* the original network – the currents in its internal resistors, or the power it wastes internally. It tells you everything about how the network behaves towards anything you connect to terminals a and b. For many problems, that is exactly what you need.

### Why it works

Here is a short argument for why the theorem is true, using the superposition theorem from Chapter 13. Suppose the network is connected to some external load, and a current I flows out of terminal a into that load. Replace the load by an ideal current source of the same value I: as far as the network is concerned, nothing has changed. Now use superposition to find the terminal voltage V~ab~.

- With the external current source switched off (open-circuited) and all the internal sources on, the terminal voltage is simply the open-circuit voltage. Call it V~Th~.
- With all the internal independent sources switched off and only the external current source on, the network is just a collection of resistors, which looks like a single resistance R~Th~. The current I flows out of terminal a, so it drops a voltage I × R~Th~ across that resistance, reducing V~ab~.

Adding the two: V~ab~ = V~Th~ − I × R~Th~. That is exactly the terminal equation of a voltage source V~Th~ in series with a resistance R~Th~. Whatever load is connected, whatever current flows, the network and its equivalent produce the same terminal voltage. They are indistinguishable from outside.

Notice that this argument used only linearity. That is the only condition the theorem needs.

## 14.2 Finding the Thévenin Equivalent

There are three steps.

1. **Identify the terminals** and remove the load (the component you are interested in) from the circuit.
2. **Find V~Th~**: calculate the voltage between the open terminals, using any method you like.
3. **Find R~Th~**: switch off every *independent* source (voltage sources → shorts, current sources → opens), then find the equivalent resistance between the terminals.

Finally, reconnect the load to the equivalent circuit and solve the simple series loop.

![Figure 14.2 — Finding a Thévenin equivalent. (a) The original circuit with its load. (b) With the load removed, the open-circuit voltage is V~Th~. (c) With the source switched off, the resistance looking into a–b is R~Th~.](fig/ch14_stages.svg){width=100%}

::: example
**Worked Example 14.1 — A first Thévenin equivalent**

For the circuit of Figure 14.2(a), find the Thévenin equivalent seen by the load R~L~, and use it to find the load current for R~L~ = 2 Ω, 6 Ω and 10 Ω.

**Step 1: Remove the load** (Figure 14.2(b)).

**Step 2: Open-circuit voltage.** With nothing connected at a–b, no current flows through the 2 Ω resistor, so it drops no voltage, and terminal a is at the same voltage as the junction above the 12 Ω resistor. That junction is the output of a 6 Ω / 12 Ω divider:

V~Th~ = 24 × {{frac|12|6 + 12}} = 16 V

**Step 3: Thévenin resistance** (Figure 14.2(c)). Short the 24 V source. Looking into terminal a, we see the 2 Ω resistor in series with the parallel combination of 6 Ω and 12 Ω (both now run from the junction to the bottom rail):

R~Th~ = 2 + (6 ∥ 12) = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω

**Step 4: The equivalent.** A 16 V source in series with 6 Ω.

**Step 5: Load currents.** I~L~ = V~Th~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~):

- R~L~ = 2 Ω: 16 / 8 = **2 A**
- R~L~ = 6 Ω: 16 / 12 = **1.33 A**
- R~L~ = 10 Ω: 16 / 16 = **1 A**

**Comment.** Without Thévenin, each load value would require a fresh series–parallel calculation. With it, each takes one line. If you need to try fifty load values, or plot a graph as in Figure 14.3, the saving is enormous.
:::

![Figure 14.3 — Load current and load voltage for the circuit of Worked Example 14.1 as the load resistance varies. Both curves follow directly from the Thévenin equivalent: I~L~ = 16/(6 + R~L~) and V~L~ = 16R~L~/(6 + R~L~).](fig/ch14_load.svg){width=100%}

Figure 14.3 shows two features of every Thévenin source. As R~L~ falls towards zero, the current rises towards its maximum, the **short-circuit current** V~Th~/R~Th~ (here 16/6 = 2.67 A). As R~L~ rises towards infinity, the voltage rises towards V~Th~. Between these extremes the source behaves exactly like a battery with internal resistance – which, of course, is what it is.

## 14.3 More Practice With the Basic Method

::: example
**Worked Example 14.2 — Thévenin on the two-battery circuit**

In the two-battery circuit we have used throughout Part Four (10 V through 2 Ω, 5 V through 1 Ω, both feeding node A, with a 4 Ω load from A to the reference), find the Thévenin equivalent seen by the 4 Ω resistor, and hence its current.

**Step 1: Remove the 4 Ω.** The node A now connects only to the two source branches.

**Step 2: V~Th~.** With the 4 Ω removed, a single current circulates from the 10 V source through 2 Ω, through 1 Ω, into the 5 V source. Nodal analysis at A is quickest:

{{frac|V~A~ − 10|2}} + {{frac|V~A~ − 5|1}} = 0,   so   1.5V~A~ = 10   and   V~Th~ = 6.667 V

**Step 3: R~Th~.** Short both sources. Looking into A, the 2 Ω and 1 Ω each run to the reference, in parallel: R~Th~ = 2 ∥ 1 = 0.667 Ω.

**Step 4: Load current.**

I~4Ω~ = {{frac|6.667|0.667 + 4}} = 1.429 A

which matches every earlier result. We have now solved this circuit in five different ways, and they all agree.
:::

Thévenin's theorem is particularly powerful for the problem that defeated simple reduction in Chapter 7: finding the current in the middle resistor of an unbalanced bridge.

![Figure 14.4 — The unbalanced bridge of Chapter 9 with its middle resistor R~5~ removed. The Thévenin equivalent between C and D gives the current in R~5~ directly.](fig/ch14_bridge.svg){width=66%}

::: example
**Worked Example 14.3 — The bridge, the Thévenin way**

For the bridge of Worked Example 9.3, find the current in the 9 Ω resistor R~5~ using Thévenin's theorem.

**Step 1: Remove R~5~** (Figure 14.4). The two sides of the bridge are now independent voltage dividers.

**Step 2: V~Th~.** Taking the bottom node as reference:

V~C~ = 20 × {{frac|5|6 + 5}} = 9.091 V     V~D~ = 20 × {{frac|6.5|3 + 6.5}} = 13.684 V

So D is higher than C by V~Th~ = 13.684 − 9.091 = **4.593 V**.

**Step 3: R~Th~.** Short the 20 V source. Now the top and bottom nodes are joined. Looking from C, the 6 Ω and 5 Ω both run to this joined node, in parallel: 6 ∥ 5 = 2.727 Ω. Likewise from D: 3 ∥ 6.5 = 2.053 Ω. From C to D the path goes through one combination and then the other, in series:

R~Th~ = 2.727 + 2.053 = **4.780 Ω**

**Step 4: Current in R~5~.**

I~5~ = {{frac|4.593|4.780 + 9}} = 0.333 A, flowing from D to C

This agrees exactly with the delta–star solution in Chapter 9, and it took a fraction of the effort.

**Bonus.** We can now see at a glance how the current in the middle would change if R~5~ were replaced by a sensitive meter of, say, 100 Ω: I = 4.593 / 104.78 = 43.8 mA. That is precisely the calculation needed to choose a detector for a Wheatstone bridge.
:::

::: tip
**Finding R~Th~: Look In From the Terminals**

When finding R~Th~, imagine you are standing at the terminals with an ohmmeter, looking into the circuit with all the independent sources switched off. Redraw the circuit from that viewpoint, with the terminals at the top of the page. Resistors that seemed to be in series from the source's point of view are often in parallel from yours. This is the "it depends which terminals you look into" lesson of Chapter 7.
:::

## 14.4 The Short-Circuit Method

There is a second way of finding R~Th~ that does not require switching sources off. Short-circuit the terminals and calculate the current that flows through the short, I~sc~. In the Thévenin equivalent, a short across the terminals carries V~Th~/R~Th~. So:

::: keyeq
**Thévenin resistance from open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current**

R~Th~ = {{frac|V~oc~|I~sc~}}
:::

This method is useful when the circuit is easier to analyse with the terminals shorted, and it is indispensable when dependent sources are present (Section 14.5). It also mirrors exactly what you can do in a laboratory.

::: example
**Worked Example 14.4 — Checking Example 14.1 by short circuit**

In Figure 14.2(b), short terminals a and b and find I~sc~. Hence confirm R~Th~.

**Solution.** With a–b shorted, the 2 Ω resistor runs from the junction to the bottom rail, in parallel with the 12 Ω: 2 ∥ 12 = 1.714 Ω. The source sees 6 + 1.714 = 7.714 Ω, so it supplies 24 / 7.714 = 3.111 A. This divides between 12 Ω and 2 Ω; the share through the 2 Ω (and so through the short) is

I~sc~ = 3.111 × {{frac|12|12 + 2}} = 2.667 A

Then R~Th~ = V~oc~ / I~sc~ = 16 / 2.667 = 6 Ω ✓.
:::

## 14.5 Thévenin With Dependent Sources

When a network contains **dependent** sources, the "switch off the sources and look in" method needs care. You may switch off the *independent* sources, but the dependent ones must stay (Chapter 13), and a network containing dependent sources does not generally reduce to a simple series–parallel combination. There are two reliable approaches.

**Method 1: V~oc~ / I~sc~.** Find the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current with all sources (dependent and independent) active, and divide.

**Method 2: Test source.** Switch off the independent sources only. Apply a test voltage V~t~ (or test current I~t~) at the terminals, calculate the current I~t~ (or voltage V~t~) that results, and take R~Th~ = V~t~ / I~t~. Any convenient value will do, such as 1 V or 1 A, because the network is linear.

![Figure 14.5 — A network containing a voltage-controlled current source whose value depends on the terminal voltage itself.](fig/ch14_dependent.svg){width=60%}

::: example
**Worked Example 14.5 — A network with a dependent source**

Find the Thévenin equivalent of Figure 14.5 at terminals a–b. The dependent source draws 0.25V~ab~ amps downwards from node a.

**V~oc~.** With a–b open, KCL at a:

{{frac|V~ab~ − 6|2}} + 0.25V~ab~ = 0,   so   0.75V~ab~ = 3   and   **V~oc~ = 4 V**

**Method 1: I~sc~.** Short a–b, so V~ab~ = 0 and the dependent source delivers nothing. All the current from the 6 V source flows through the short: I~sc~ = 6/2 = 3 A. Then R~Th~ = 4/3 = **1.33 Ω**.

**Method 2: test source.** Switch off the 6 V source (short it) and apply a test voltage V~t~ at a–b. The current drawn from the test source flows into node a and out through the 2 Ω (V~t~/2) and the dependent source (0.25V~t~):

I~t~ = 0.5V~t~ + 0.25V~t~ = 0.75V~t~,   so   R~Th~ = V~t~ / I~t~ = 1/0.75 = **1.33 Ω** ✓

**The trap.** If you had wrongly switched off the dependent source as well, you would have found R~Th~ = 2 Ω – a 50 % error. Dependent sources must stay.
:::

In fact the dependent source in this example behaves exactly like a 4 Ω resistor (it draws 0.25 A per volt), which is why R~Th~ = 2 ∥ 4 = 1.33 Ω. Dependent sources can even produce *negative* Thévenin resistances in some amplifier circuits – a sign of positive feedback and potential instability, which you will meet in later courses.

## 14.6 Finding a Thévenin Equivalent by Measurement

One of the greatest strengths of Thévenin's theorem is that it applies to real equipment whose insides you cannot see. A sealed sensor module, a solar panel, a battery, the output of an unknown circuit board – each can be characterised by two measurements.

**Measurement 1: open-circuit voltage.** Measure V~oc~ with a high-resistance voltmeter and nothing else connected. This is V~Th~.

**Measurement 2: a known load.** Ideally you would measure the short-circuit current, but shorting a source is often unsafe or damaging. Instead, connect a known load resistor R~L~ and measure the terminal voltage V~L~. The load current is I~L~ = V~L~/R~L~, and the voltage lost inside the source is V~Th~ − V~L~, so

::: eq
R~Th~ = {{frac|V~Th~ − V~L~|I~L~}}
:::

This is exactly the method we used to measure a battery's internal resistance in Worked Example 3.13. Now you can see that it was Thévenin's theorem all along.

::: example
**Worked Example 14.6 — Characterising a car battery**

A car battery reads 12.6 V with no load. With a 10 Ω load connected, it reads 12.0 V. Find its Thévenin equivalent and predict its terminal voltage when supplying a 150 A starter motor.

**Step 1: V~Th~** = 12.6 V.

**Step 2: Load current** = 12.0 / 10 = 1.2 A.

**Step 3: R~Th~** = (12.6 − 12.0) / 1.2 = 0.5 Ω.

**Step 4: Prediction.** At 150 A the internal drop would be 150 × 0.5 = 75 V – more than the battery's entire EMF!

**Interpretation.** This battery cannot possibly supply 150 A; its internal resistance is far too high. A healthy car battery has an internal resistance of a few milliohms (compare the 0.02 Ω in Worked Example 3.12). A reading of 0.5 Ω tells the mechanic the battery is failing, even though its open-circuit voltage looks perfectly healthy. Garages use exactly this principle in their battery testers.
:::

## 14.7 Why Engineers Love Thévenin

Before we leave this theorem, it is worth reflecting on why it is so widely used.

**It isolates the part you care about.** When you are designing the load – a sensor input, a loudspeaker, a battery charger's output stage – you do not need to keep re-analysing the source circuit. Two numbers describe it completely.

**It makes "what if" questions trivial.** What if the load doubles? What if it is short-circuited? What if we add a 1 kΩ resistor in series? Each answer is a one-line calculation.

**It describes real equipment.** Data sheets for signal generators, sensors and amplifiers quote "output resistance" or "source impedance". That is the Thévenin resistance. When an engineer says "this amplifier has a 50 Ω output", they are giving you R~Th~.

**It underpins the concept of loading.** In Chapter 8 we found that a voltage divider's output sags under load. Thévenin explains why: a divider is a source with a non-zero R~Th~ (equal to R~1~ ∥ R~2~), and every source with non-zero R~Th~ loses voltage when current is drawn.

**It leads to maximum power transfer.** Given a source with a fixed R~Th~, what load extracts the most power? That question, with its answer and its practical consequences, is the subject of Chapter 16.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Forgetting to remove the load** before finding V~oc~. V~Th~ is the voltage with nothing connected.
- **Switching off sources incorrectly.** Voltage sources become shorts; current sources become opens.
- **Switching off dependent sources.** Never. Use V~oc~/I~sc~ or a test source.
- **Getting the polarity of V~Th~ wrong.** Decide which terminal is + and be consistent when you reconnect the load.
- **Looking into the wrong terminals** when finding R~Th~. Draw the circuit from the terminals' point of view.
- **Expecting the equivalent to tell you internal powers.** It only describes behaviour at the terminals.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **Thévenin's theorem**: any linear two-terminal network is equivalent, at its terminals, to a voltage source V~Th~ in series with a resistance R~Th~.
- **V~Th~** is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals.
- **R~Th~** is the resistance seen at the terminals with independent sources switched off; or **V~oc~/I~sc~**; or, with dependent sources, found using a **test source**.
- The load current is **I~L~ = V~Th~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~)**, which makes varying-load problems trivial.
- A Thévenin equivalent can be found **by measurement**: V~oc~ plus one known load.
- Real-world **output resistance** and **internal resistance** are Thévenin resistances.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

V~Th~ = V~oc~     R~Th~ = V~oc~ / I~sc~     I~L~ = V~Th~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~)     R~Th~ = (V~oc~ − V~L~) / I~L~
:::

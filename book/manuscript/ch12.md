# DC-DC Converters, On-Board Chargers and Auxiliary Power

::: chapter-intro
Around the traction inverter sit several smaller power converters that the car cannot live without. One supplies the 12 V or 48 V network that powers every control unit, light and safety system. Another charges the battery from an ordinary AC socket. Others boost voltage between 400 V and 800 V, drive the air-conditioning compressor or feed power back to a house or the grid. This chapter explains how these converters work, from simple buck and boost stages to isolated resonant and dual-active-bridge designs, and how they are combined into compact, efficient units.
:::

Ask most people where the power electronics in an electric car is, and they will point to the inverter. It is the biggest and most powerful converter, so that is fair. But if you counted the switching converters in a modern EV, you might find ten or twenty: the on-board charger, the high-voltage to low-voltage DC-DC converter, the compressor inverter, the heater controller, the 48 V converters for chassis systems, the dozens of small point-of-load converters on every circuit board. Together they handle much less power than the traction inverter, but they run for much longer hours, they are often what wakes the car, and when one of them fails the vehicle can be just as stranded.

This chapter focuses on the two that matter most to an EV engineer, the on-board charger and the high-voltage to low-voltage converter, and on the circuit families from which they are built.

## The Family of Converters

Figure 12.1 shows the converters connected to a typical EV's high-voltage bus.

![**Figure 12.1** Converters around the high-voltage DC bus. The traction inverter and the on-board charger handle the largest powers; the DC-DC converter supplies the low-voltage network; the compressor inverter and heater serve the thermal system. Some 800 V vehicles add a boost converter or use the drive unit to accept 400 V charging.](figures/fig12_1.png)

They can be grouped by what they must do:

- **Non-isolated DC-DC conversion**, where input and output share a common reference: the boost converter that lets an 800 V car charge at 400 V, or a buck stage in a heater controller.
- **Isolated DC-DC conversion**, where a transformer separates input from output: the high-voltage to 12 V converter and the second stage of the on-board charger.
- **AC-DC conversion with power factor correction**: the first stage of the on-board charger, drawing a clean sinusoidal current from the mains.
- **DC-AC conversion**: the traction inverter and the compressor inverter, and the reverse direction of a bidirectional charger supplying a house.

## Non-Isolated Converters

### Buck, boost and their bidirectional form

Chapter 3 introduced the buck converter, which steps voltage down with an output of *D V*~in~. Rearranging the same components gives the *boost* converter, which steps voltage up:

$$ V_{out} = \frac{V_{in}}{1 - D} $$ {12.1}

When the switch is on, the inductor connects across the input and its current rises, storing energy. When the switch turns off, the inductor's current flows through a diode into the higher-voltage output, releasing that energy. The higher the duty cycle, the more energy is stored and the higher the output.

Replace the diode with a second active switch and the circuit becomes a *bidirectional* half-bridge converter: it boosts in one direction and bucks in the other. This is the circuit that sits between 400 V charging stations and 800 V batteries in some vehicles, and it is, physically, exactly one leg of an inverter plus an inductor. That is why it is possible to borrow an inverter leg and the motor's windings for the job, as Chapter 11 mentioned.

The inductor current ripples at the switching frequency. For a boost converter, the peak-to-peak ripple is:

$$ \Delta I_L = \frac{V_{in}\, D}{L\, f_{sw}} $$ {12.2}

and choosing the inductor is a trade between ripple, which raises losses and filter requirements, and size.

::: example
**Worked Example 12.1 — Boosting 400 V to 800 V for charging**

An 800 V car must accept 150 kW from a 400 V DC charger through an on-board boost converter. The converter switches at 20 kHz. Find the duty cycle, the input current and the inductor ripple for an inductance of 200 μH. Why do designers often use several interleaved phases instead of one?

*Solution.* *D* = 1 − *V*~in~/*V*~out~ = 1 − 400/800 = 0.5.

Input current = 150 000 / 400 = 375 A.

Ripple: Δ*I* = 400 × 0.5 / (200 × 10^−6^ × 20 000) = 50 A peak-to-peak, about 13 % of the average.

A single 375 A inductor with 50 A of ripple at 20 kHz is large and heavy. Splitting the converter into, say, three phases switched 120° apart, each carrying 125 A, means each inductor is smaller, and the ripples largely cancel at the input and output, reducing filter capacitors. Interleaving is standard practice in high-power converters for exactly this reason.
:::

## Isolated Converters

Many converters in an EV must provide *galvanic isolation*: no direct electrical path between input and output. The 12 V network must be isolated from the floating high-voltage system so that a fault cannot put traction voltage onto the chassis-referenced low-voltage wiring. The on-board charger must isolate the battery from the mains, whose neutral is connected to earth. Isolation requires a transformer, and a transformer requires alternating current, so isolated DC-DC converters work by chopping DC into high-frequency AC, transforming it, and rectifying it again.

The higher the switching frequency, the smaller the transformer, because a transformer's size is set by the volt-seconds it must support in each half-cycle. Operating at 100 kHz rather than 20 kHz shrinks the magnetic core dramatically. But hard switching at 100 kHz would make switching losses unacceptable. The solution is *soft switching*: arranging the circuit so that each switch turns on when the voltage across it is already zero (*zero-voltage switching*, ZVS) or turns off when the current through it is already zero (*zero-current switching*, ZCS). The two isolated topologies that dominate EV converters both achieve this.

![**Figure 12.2** Isolated converter topologies. (a) LLC resonant converter, regulated by switching frequency. (b) Dual active bridge, regulated by the phase shift between two active bridges and inherently bidirectional.](figures/fig12_2.png)

### The LLC resonant converter

The LLC converter, shown in Figure 12.2(a), places a resonant tank, a series inductor *L*~r~, a series capacitor *C*~r~ and the transformer's magnetising inductance *L*~m~, between a half- or full-bridge and the transformer. The bridge produces a square wave, and the tank responds with nearly sinusoidal current. By operating near the tank's resonant frequency, the converter achieves ZVS on its primary switches and ZCS on its secondary rectifiers across a wide load range. Efficiencies of 97 to 98 % are routine.

The output voltage is regulated by varying the switching *frequency*, since the tank's voltage gain changes with frequency, as Figure 12.3 shows. Below resonance, gain rises; above, it falls. The LLC is superb when the input and output voltages are fairly constant, but less comfortable when they must vary widely, because the frequency range then becomes large and efficiency suffers at the extremes. A battery's voltage varies by 30 % or more between empty and full, which is a challenge for an LLC charger stage.

![**Figure 12.3** Voltage gain of an LLC resonant tank against normalised switching frequency for several load levels. At the resonant frequency (normalised value 1), gain is close to unity for all loads, which is the most efficient operating point. Regulation is achieved by moving the frequency away from resonance.](figures/fig12_3.png)

### The dual active bridge

The dual active bridge (DAB), shown in Figure 12.2(b), places an active full bridge on *both* sides of the transformer, with a series inductance *L* between them. Each bridge produces a square wave at the same frequency. Power flows from the leading bridge to the lagging bridge, and its magnitude depends on the *phase shift* *φ* between the two square waves:

$$ P = \frac{V_1\, V_2'\, \varphi\, (\pi - |\varphi|)}{2\pi^2 f_{sw}\, L} $$ {12.3}

where *V*~2~′ is the secondary voltage referred to the primary. Reversing the sign of the phase shift reverses the power flow, so the DAB is naturally *bidirectional*, which makes it ideal for chargers that must also supply power back to the grid or a house. It achieves ZVS over a wide range, though it loses soft switching at light load or when the voltage ratio departs far from the transformer turns ratio, and designers add extra phase-shift variables to extend its range.


::: example
**Worked Example 12.2 — Power through a dual active bridge**

A DAB operates at 100 kHz with *V*~1~ = 400 V and a secondary voltage of 400 V referred to the primary. The series inductance is 20 μH. What power flows with a phase shift of 45° (π/4 rad)? What phase shift gives maximum power, and what is that power?

*Solution.* From Equation 12.3:

*P* = 400 × 400 × (π/4)(π − π/4) / (2π^2^ × 100 000 × 20 × 10^−6^)
= 160 000 × 0.785 × 2.356 / 39.48 = 7.50 kW.

Power is maximum when *φ*(π − *φ*) is maximum, at *φ* = π/2 (90°), giving *P*~max~ = 160 000 × (π/2)^2^ / 39.48 = 10.0 kW.

In practice, designers keep the phase shift well below 90°, since circulating current and conduction losses rise steeply near the maximum. A design point of 7.4 kW at around 45° is a sensible choice for a domestic single-phase bidirectional charger.
:::

## The Low-Voltage Supply

Every EV still has a low-voltage network, and something must keep it charged now that there is no alternator. That job falls to the high-voltage to low-voltage DC-DC converter, sometimes called the auxiliary power module or LDC (low-voltage DC-DC converter).

Typical ratings are 2 to 3.5 kW at around 14 V, so output currents of 150 to 250 A. The converter is usually a phase-shifted full bridge or LLC on the high-voltage side, a transformer, and synchronous rectification with low-voltage silicon MOSFETs on the output. Efficiency is typically 93 to 96 %. Because the output current is so high, the secondary-side rectifiers, transformer windings and output busbars dominate the losses, and their layout is critical.

::: example
**Worked Example 12.3 — The cost of the 12 V network**

On a winter night drive, the low-voltage network draws 150 A at 14 V: headlights, heated seats and mirrors, wipers, fans, infotainment and dozens of control units. The DC-DC converter is 94 % efficient. What power is drawn from the 400 V battery, and what current? Over a 90-minute drive, how much range does this cost a car using 18 kWh per 100 km?

*Solution.* Output power = 14 × 150 = 2.10 kW. Input power = 2.10 / 0.94 = 2.23 kW, a current of 5.6 A at 400 V.

Energy over 1.5 h = 3.35 kWh, equivalent to about 18.6 km of driving.

That is not trivial. Low-voltage loads in a modern car have grown with every generation of electronics, and they now matter for range. LED lighting, efficient heated surfaces in place of high-power air heating, and putting control units to sleep when not needed all help.
:::

### Standby and the sleeping car

When the car is parked, the DC-DC converter normally switches off and the 12 V battery alone supplies any remaining loads: the telematics unit listening for a phone app, the alarm, the key-fob receiver, perhaps the BMS checking cell voltages. These *quiescent* currents are tiny, perhaps 20 to 40 mA, but they run all the time. Over a month, 30 mA drains 22 Ah, a large fraction of a small 12 V battery. Most EVs therefore wake the high-voltage system periodically to top up the 12 V battery from the traction pack, which itself loses a little energy each time. A car whose 12 V battery is weak, or whose control units fail to go to sleep properly, will refuse to start, even with a full traction battery: the contactors cannot close without low-voltage power. This is one of the commonest roadside call-outs for electric vehicles, as the Field Problem below explains.

### Towards 48 V and zonal architectures

Many manufacturers are moving the low-voltage network from 12 V to 48 V, at least for high-power loads such as electric power steering, active suspension, brake-by-wire actuators and heaters. At four times the voltage, the same power needs a quarter of the current and a sixteenth of the resistive loss in the same wire, so wiring harnesses can be much lighter. At the same time, vehicle electrical architectures are moving from dozens of function-specific control units towards a few powerful central computers and *zonal controllers* that distribute power and data to each region of the car. The DC-DC converter then may feed a 48 V backbone, with local converters stepping down to 12 V or 5 V where needed. Safety-critical by-wire functions add a requirement for *redundant* low-voltage supplies, so some designs have two DC-DC converters or two low-voltage batteries.

::: problem
**Field Problem — Car will not wake despite a full traction battery**

*Symptom.* After two weeks parked at an airport, a car shows no response to the key or app. The traction battery was at 80 % when parked.

*Likely causes.* (1) The 12 V battery is discharged, by normal quiescent current over a long period, by a control unit that failed to sleep, or by an aftermarket accessory such as a dashcam wired to permanent power. (2) The periodic top-up from the traction battery was disabled or failed. (3) The 12 V battery itself is aged and has lost capacity.

*How to tell them apart.* Jump-start or charge the 12 V battery and check its health. Then measure the quiescent current after the car has had time to fall asleep, typically 10 to 30 minutes; a reading of hundreds of milliamps instead of tens identifies a fault, which can be located by removing fuses one at a time while watching the current. Review logs for missed top-up events. Aftermarket devices are a frequent culprit.
:::

## The On-Board Charger

The on-board charger (OBC) lets a vehicle charge from an ordinary AC supply, at home, at work, or from a public AC post. In Europe, typical ratings are 7.4 kW single-phase (32 A at 230 V) or 11 kW three-phase (16 A per phase at 400 V line-to-line), with 22 kW available in some models. In markets with split-phase supplies, such as North America, ratings around 7 to 11.5 kW are common.

### Two-stage architecture

Almost every OBC has two stages, shown in Figure 12.4.

![**Figure 12.4** Architecture of a two-stage on-board charger. The power factor correction (PFC) stage rectifies the mains and draws a sinusoidal current in phase with the voltage, regulating an intermediate DC link. The isolated DC-DC stage converts that link to the battery voltage and provides galvanic isolation.](figures/fig12_4.png)

**The power factor correction (PFC) stage** converts the AC mains to an intermediate DC voltage, typically 400 V for single-phase input or 650 to 800 V for three-phase. Crucially, it draws current that is sinusoidal and in phase with the mains voltage, giving a power factor close to one and low harmonic distortion. A simple diode rectifier followed by a capacitor would draw current only in short spikes near the voltage peaks, polluting the grid with harmonics and wasting the capacity of the supply wiring. International standards such as IEC 61000-3-2 and 61000-3-12 limit the harmonic currents a device may draw. The PFC is a boost converter whose duty cycle is continuously adjusted so that its input current follows the shape of the input voltage.

The classic PFC uses a diode bridge followed by a boost converter. Modern high-efficiency designs use the *bridgeless totem-pole PFC*, which eliminates the lossy diode bridge by using a fast half-bridge (SiC or GaN) switching at high frequency and a slow half-bridge switching at mains frequency. It was impractical with silicon MOSFETs, whose body diodes recover too slowly, and became practical with wide-bandgap devices. For three-phase input, active rectifiers similar to a traction inverter running in reverse, or Vienna rectifiers, are common.

**The isolated DC-DC stage** converts the intermediate DC voltage to the battery voltage, provides galvanic isolation and regulates the charging current as the BMS commands. LLC and DAB converters are the usual choices.

### Single-phase power pulsation

A single-phase supply delivers power that pulsates at twice the mains frequency: instantaneous power is *V I* sin^2^(*ωt*), which swings between zero and twice the average, 100 times a second on a 50 Hz supply. The battery wants a steady current, so something must store energy during the peaks and release it during the troughs. Usually that is a large capacitor on the intermediate DC link, sized by:

$$ C = \frac{P}{2\pi f_{line}\, V_{DC}\, \Delta V_{pp}} $$ {12.4}

::: example
**Worked Example 12.4 — The bulk capacitor of a single-phase charger**

A 7.4 kW single-phase OBC has a 400 V intermediate DC link and allows a peak-to-peak 100 Hz ripple of 40 V. What capacitance is needed?

*Solution.* *C* = 7 400 / (2π × 50 × 400 × 40) = 7 400 / 5 026 548 = 1.47 mF.

That is a large capacitor bank. At 450 V rating it is usually made from several aluminium electrolytic capacitors, which are bulky, temperature-sensitive and often the life-limiting component of the charger. Three-phase chargers do not need such large capacitors, because the total power of a balanced three-phase supply is constant. Some designs use *active power decoupling* circuits to store the ripple energy in a smaller capacitor at higher voltage swing, and others allow the ripple to pass through to the battery, which tolerates a modest 100 Hz current component.
:::

### Efficiency and integration

OBC efficiency at full power is typically 93 to 96 %, and wide-bandgap devices push the best designs higher. Because a car spends many hours a week charging at home, these few per cent matter to running costs. Losses at partial power and in standby matter too: a charger that consumes 30 W while the battery is being balanced at the end of a charge, or while waiting for an off-peak tariff to start, can waste a surprising amount over a year.

To save cost, mass and space, manufacturers increasingly integrate the OBC, the high-voltage to low-voltage DC-DC converter and the high-voltage junction box into one unit, sometimes called a *power distribution and conversion unit* or simply a "3-in-1". Integrated designs can share a housing, cooling, control electronics and, in clever designs, magnetic components: a single transformer with three windings can serve both the charger and the 12 V converter.

## Bidirectional Charging

A charger built with active switches on both sides, such as a totem-pole PFC and a DAB, can run backwards. The vehicle can then supply power as well as accept it. The industry distinguishes:

- **Vehicle-to-load (V2L):** the car provides AC sockets, powering tools, camping equipment or appliances.
- **Vehicle-to-home (V2H):** the car supplies a house, usually through a dedicated installation that isolates the house from the grid during an outage or offsets peak-time consumption.
- **Vehicle-to-grid (V2G):** the car exports power to the grid under the control of an aggregator or system operator, earning money for grid services.

The power electronics for V2L and V2H are straightforward extensions of a bidirectional OBC. V2G involves more: grid-connection rules (in the UK, the G98 and G99 requirements for generators connected to distribution networks), protection against islanding, communication protocols such as ISO 15118-20, and agreements about battery warranty and ageing. DC bidirectional charging, in which the power conversion sits in the wall unit rather than in the car, is the other route, and both are discussed in Chapter 13.

::: casefile
**Case File 12 — The charger that kept tripping the house**

*The complaint.* Several owners of a new model report that charging at home on their 7 kW wallboxes trips the residual-current protection a few seconds after the charge starts. The same wallboxes charge other vehicles without trouble.

*State it in numbers.* The wallboxes contain residual-current devices that trip at 30 mA AC residual current and include a 6 mA DC residual-current detection function, as required for EV charging installations. Logs from one wallbox show the DC detector tripping, with measured DC residual current of 6.5 to 8 mA, about 3 seconds after the charger begins drawing power.

*Draw the boundary.* The vehicle's on-board charger, especially its earth (protective conductor) connections, EMI filter Y-capacitors and any path by which DC can reach earth; the wallbox's detection circuitry.

*Simplest model.* Residual current is any difference between the currents in the line and neutral conductors, which must therefore be flowing to earth. AC leakage through Y-capacitors is normal and predictable: a few milliamps at 50 Hz. DC leakage is unusual. It suggests that the floating high-voltage DC side of the vehicle has a high-resistance path to the chassis, and so to earth through the charging cable, while the charger's switching action connects the mains to the high-voltage side. During operation of a non-isolated part of the charger, a DC current can then flow through that path.

*Measure.* Insulation monitoring data from the affected cars show high-voltage isolation resistances around 70 kΩ on the negative side, close to the alarm threshold but not below it. Unaffected cars read above 2 MΩ. Further investigation finds that the cars share a batch of coolant heaters with a manufacturing defect that lets moisture reduce their internal insulation over time. During charging, the OBC's PFC stage connects the mains neutral potential to its intermediate DC link, and through the isolation transformer's interwinding capacitance and the degraded heater insulation, a DC component of a few milliamps reaches earth.

*Dominant term.* Degraded insulation in a high-voltage component, revealed by the wallbox's sensitive DC detection.

*Fix and cross-check.* The affected heaters are replaced, restoring isolation above 2 MΩ, and the tripping stops. The manufacturer also tightens the threshold at which the vehicle warns of degrading isolation during charging, so that future cases are caught by the car before the wallbox trips. The team confirms that the OBC design itself, with its isolated DC-DC stage, meets leakage requirements with healthy components.

*Lesson.* An external protection device tripping is often a symptom of something inside the vehicle. The trip is information, not just a nuisance.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- An EV contains many converters besides the traction inverter: OBC, HV-LV DC-DC, compressor and heater drives, boost stages and numerous small converters.
- Boost converters give *V*~in~/(1 − *D*); a half-bridge with an inductor is bidirectional. Interleaving reduces ripple and component size.
- Isolated converters use high-frequency transformers; soft switching (ZVS, ZCS) keeps losses low. LLC converters regulate by frequency; dual active bridges regulate by phase shift and are inherently bidirectional.
- The HV-LV DC-DC converter supplies the 12 V or 48 V network, often 2 to 3.5 kW; low-voltage loads matter for range, and quiescent current determines how long the car can sleep.
- On-board chargers use a PFC stage for sinusoidal input current and an isolated DC-DC stage; single-phase chargers need large energy storage for the 100 Hz power pulsation.
- Integration of charger, DC-DC and junction box saves cost and space. Bidirectional chargers enable V2L, V2H and V2G, subject to grid rules.
:::

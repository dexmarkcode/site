The first time I tried to analyse a transistor amplifier as a student, I was stuck for an entire evening. The textbook replaced the transistor with something called a "dependent current source", drew it as a diamond, and then carried on as though nothing unusual had happened. To me, something very unusual *had* happened. Every source I had met until then had a fixed value. This one's value depended on a current somewhere else in the circuit, which I did not yet know. How could I write an equation for a source whose size I could not state?

The answer, when it came, was almost disappointing in its simplicity: you write the equations exactly as before, treating the dependent source as if its value were known, and then you add one more equation that says what it depends on. That is the whole trick.

This chapter deals with that idea and with two awkward situations that we postponed in Chapters 10 and 11: a voltage source connected between two nodes, neither of which is the reference, and a current source shared between two meshes. The tools for these – the **supernode** and the **supermesh** – are small extensions of what you already know. With them, together with dependent sources, nodal and mesh analysis become completely general methods that can handle any linear circuit you are likely to meet.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- recognise when a supernode is needed and apply KCL to it;
- write the constraint equation for a voltage source between two non-reference nodes;
- recognise when a supermesh is needed and apply KVL to it;
- write the constraint equation for a current source shared by two meshes;
- identify the four types of dependent source and explain where they are used;
- include dependent sources in nodal and mesh analysis.
:::

## 12.1 The Trouble With a Floating Voltage Source

In nodal analysis we write KCL at each node, expressing every current in terms of node voltages. For a resistor that is easy: (V~x~ − V~y~)/R. For a current source it is easy: its value. But what about a **voltage source**?

If the voltage source has one end at the reference node, we saw in Chapter 10 that it simply fixes the voltage of the other node. The difficulty arises when it is connected between two *non-reference* nodes – a so-called **floating** voltage source. The current through an ideal voltage source is not determined by the source itself (Chapter 4); it is whatever the rest of the circuit demands. So we cannot write the current through it in terms of node voltages, and the usual KCL equations at its two nodes contain an extra unknown.

There are two ways round this. One is to introduce the unknown current through the source as an extra variable, which works but makes the equations larger. The other, much neater, is the **supernode**.

### The supernode

Draw a closed boundary around the voltage source and the two nodes it connects, as in Figure 12.1. This region is called a **supernode**. In Chapter 5 we saw that KCL applies to any closed boundary: the total current leaving the region must be zero. The awkward current through the voltage source flows entirely *inside* the boundary, so it never appears in the equation.

![Figure 12.1 — A voltage source floating between nodes 1 and 2. The dashed boundary encloses both nodes and the source to form a supernode.](fig/ch12_supernode.svg){width=72%}

That gives us one equation for two unknown node voltages. The second comes from the voltage source itself, which fixes the *difference* between the two node voltages. This is called the **constraint equation**.

::: keyeq
**The supernode**

1. KCL for the supernode: sum of currents leaving the boundary through all other branches = 0.
2. Constraint equation: V~+~ − V~−~ = E, where E is the source voltage and V~+~, V~−~ are the node voltages at its + and − terminals.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 12.1 — A simple supernode**

A 9 A current source pushes current into node 1, which is connected to the reference through 2 Ω. Node 2 is connected to the reference through 4 Ω. A 6 V source connects the two nodes, with its + terminal at node 1. Find V~1~ and V~2~.

**Step 1: Constraint.** V~1~ − V~2~ = 6.

**Step 2: KCL for the supernode** (currents leaving the boundary): through 2 Ω, V~1~/2; through 4 Ω, V~2~/4; through the current source, −9 (it pushes current in).

{{frac|V~1~|2}} + {{frac|V~2~|4}} − 9 = 0

**Step 3: Solve.** Substitute V~1~ = V~2~ + 6 and multiply by 4: 2(V~2~ + 6) + V~2~ = 36, so 3V~2~ = 24 and **V~2~ = 8 V**, **V~1~ = 14 V**.

**Step 4: The current through the voltage source.** Now that the node voltages are known, apply ordinary KCL at node 1 alone. In: 9 A from the current source. Out: 14/2 = 7 A through 2 Ω. The remaining 2 A must flow from node 1 through the voltage source to node 2. Check at node 2: 2 A in, 8/4 = 2 A out ✓.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 12.2 — A supernode with a fixed node**

In Figure 12.1, find V~1~, V~2~ and the current through the 5 V source.

**Step 1: Known node.** The top of the 20 V source is at 20 V.

**Step 2: Constraint.** The + terminal of the 5 V source is at node 1, so V~1~ − V~2~ = 5.

**Step 3: KCL for the supernode** (currents leaving through the three resistors):

{{frac|V~1~ − 20|4}} + {{frac|V~1~|8}} + {{frac|V~2~|4}} = 0

**Step 4: Solve.** Substitute V~1~ = V~2~ + 5 and multiply through by 8:

2(V~2~ + 5 − 20) + (V~2~ + 5) + 2V~2~ = 0

2V~2~ − 30 + V~2~ + 5 + 2V~2~ = 0,   so   5V~2~ = 25

**V~2~ = 5 V** and **V~1~ = 10 V**.

**Step 5: Current through the source.** At node 1 alone: in from the 4 Ω, (20 − 10)/4 = 2.5 A; out through the 8 Ω, 10/8 = 1.25 A. So 1.25 A flows from node 1 through the 5 V source to node 2. Check: 5/4 = 1.25 A leaves node 2 through its 4 Ω resistor ✓.
:::

Notice what the supernode has done. Two nodes, joined by a voltage source, have effectively become one unknown: once V~2~ is known, V~1~ follows from the constraint. **A voltage source reduces the number of unknowns by one**, whether it is connected to the reference (fixing a node) or floating (linking two nodes). That is worth remembering when you count equations to choose a method.

## 12.2 The Trouble With a Shared Current Source

Mesh analysis has the mirror-image problem. If a current source lies on the outer edge of a circuit, it fixes one mesh current (Chapter 11). But if it lies on a branch **shared** between two meshes, it fixes only the *difference* between their mesh currents. And we cannot write KVL round either mesh on its own, because we do not know the voltage across the current source.

### The supermesh

The solution is the dual of the supernode. Imagine removing the shared current source branch altogether, merging the two meshes into one larger loop – the **supermesh**. Write KVL round this larger loop, which does not pass through the current source and so avoids its unknown voltage. Then add a constraint equation saying that the difference between the two mesh currents equals the source current.

::: keyeq
**The supermesh**

1. KVL round the supermesh: the loop formed by the two meshes with the shared current-source branch excluded.
2. Constraint equation: the difference of the two mesh currents in the shared branch equals the source current, with the sign set by the source's direction.
:::

![Figure 12.2 — A current source on the branch shared by meshes 1 and 2. The supermesh is the outer loop formed by the two meshes together, avoiding the current source.](fig/ch12_supermesh.svg){width=72%}

::: example
**Worked Example 12.3 — A supermesh**

In Figure 12.2, find I~1~ and I~2~, the voltage across the current source and the power in each source.

**Step 1: Constraint.** In the shared branch, I~1~ (clockwise in mesh 1) flows down and I~2~ (clockwise in mesh 2) flows up. The current source pushes 2 A upwards, so the net upward current is I~2~ − I~1~ = 2.

**Step 2: KVL round the supermesh**, the outer loop through the 32 V source, 2 Ω, 4 Ω and 6 Ω, walking clockwise:

−32 + 2I~1~ + 4I~2~ + 6I~2~ = 0,   so   2I~1~ + 10I~2~ = 32

**Step 3: Solve.** Substitute I~2~ = I~1~ + 2: 2I~1~ + 10I~1~ + 20 = 32, so 12I~1~ = 12 and **I~1~ = 1 A**, **I~2~ = 3 A**.

**Step 4: Voltage across the current source.** Now write KVL round mesh 1 alone, letting V~cs~ be the voltage at the top of the current source relative to the bottom:

−32 + 2 × 1 + V~cs~ = 0,   so   V~cs~ = 30 V

(Walking clockwise round mesh 1, we go down through the current source from its top to its bottom, a drop of V~cs~.)

**Step 5: Powers.** The 32 V source carries 1 A out of its + terminal: it delivers 32 W. The current source pushes 2 A upwards, from 0 V at its bottom to 30 V at its top: it delivers 2 × 30 = 60 W. Total delivered: 92 W. Absorbed: 2 × 1² + 4 × 3² + 6 × 3² = 2 + 36 + 54 = 92 W ✓.
:::

Just as a voltage source removes one unknown from nodal analysis, **a current source removes one unknown from mesh analysis**, whether on the edge (fixing a mesh current) or shared (linking two).

## 12.3 Dependent Sources

So far every source in this book has had a fixed value: a 12 V battery, a 2 A current source. These are called **independent sources**. A **dependent source** (also called a **controlled source**) is one whose value is set by a voltage or current somewhere else in the circuit.

Dependent sources are not physical components you can buy. They are **models**. When engineers want to analyse a circuit containing a transistor or an amplifier, they replace that device by a simple network of resistors and dependent sources that behaves the same way, at least for small signals. This is how amplifier gain, input resistance and output resistance are calculated, and it is the bridge between the circuit theory of this book and the electronics you will study next.

There are four types, according to whether the source produces a voltage or a current and whether it is controlled by a voltage or a current. Figure 12.3 shows their symbols; the diamond shape distinguishes them from independent sources.

![Figure 12.3 — The four types of dependent source. The controlling quantity (v~x~ or i~x~) is a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit.](fig/ch12_dependent.svg){width=100%}

::: {.table-wrap}
| Type | Output | Controlled by | Constant | Units of constant | Typical model of |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| VCVS | voltage μv~x~ | voltage v~x~ | μ (voltage gain) | none | operational amplifier |
| CCVS | voltage r i~x~ | current i~x~ | r (transresistance) | ohms | current-sensing amplifier |
| VCCS | current g v~x~ | voltage v~x~ | g (transconductance) | siemens | field-effect transistor |
| CCCS | current β i~x~ | current i~x~ | β (current gain) | none | bipolar transistor |

: Table 12.1 — The four dependent sources.
:::

### How to handle them

The rule is the one I wish someone had told me clearly on that frustrating evening:

1. **Write the nodal or mesh equations exactly as usual**, treating the dependent source as an ordinary source whose value is the expression μv~x~, ri~x~, gv~x~ or βi~x~.
2. **Express the controlling quantity** (v~x~ or i~x~) in terms of the unknowns of your method – node voltages or mesh currents.
3. **Substitute and solve.**

All the earlier rules still apply. A dependent voltage source floating between two nodes still needs a supernode; a dependent current source shared between meshes still needs a supermesh.

There is one important difference you must remember for later chapters. When we use superposition (Chapter 13) or find Thévenin resistances (Chapter 14), we will "switch off" independent sources. **Dependent sources must never be switched off.** They are not really sources of energy in their own right; they are part of the model of a device, and they stay in the circuit as long as their controlling variable exists.

::: example
**Worked Example 12.4 — Nodal analysis with a VCCS**

Figure 12.4 is a simplified model of a single-transistor amplifier with feedback. The transistor is represented by a VCCS that draws a current g·v~x~ downwards out of node 2, where v~x~ is the voltage across R~1~ (that is, V~1~) and g = 4 mS. Find V~1~, V~2~ and the voltage gain V~2~/2 V.

![Figure 12.4 — A simple amplifier model. The VCCS draws a current proportional to the voltage at node 1.](fig/ch12_amp.svg){width=100%}

**Step 1: Units.** Working in kilohms, milliamps and millisiemens keeps the numbers tidy: a voltage in volts divided by a resistance in kΩ gives a current in mA.

**Step 2: KCL at node 1** (currents leaving, in mA):

(V~1~ − 2)/1 + V~1~/1 + (V~1~ − V~2~)/10 = 0,   so   2.1V~1~ − 0.1V~2~ = 2    (1)

**Step 3: KCL at node 2.** The VCCS draws g·v~x~ = 4V~1~ mA out of node 2, downwards, which counts as a current *leaving*:

(V~2~ − V~1~)/10 + V~2~/2 + 4V~1~ = 0,   so   3.9V~1~ + 0.6V~2~ = 0    (2)

**Step 4: Solve.** From (2), V~2~ = −6.5V~1~. Substituting into (1): 2.1V~1~ + 0.65V~1~ = 2, so 2.75V~1~ = 2 and **V~1~ = 0.727 V**. Then **V~2~ = −6.5 × 0.727 = −4.73 V**.

**Step 5: Gain.** V~2~ / V~S~ = −4.73 / 2 = −2.36. The negative sign means the output is inverted: as the input rises, the output falls. This is typical of a single-transistor amplifier.

**Comment.** Notice that the coefficient matrix is no longer symmetric (−0.1 in one place, 3.9 in the other). Dependent sources break the symmetry that we relied on as a check in Chapters 10 and 11. Do not be alarmed by this; just check your working by other means.
:::

::: example
**Worked Example 12.5 — Mesh analysis with a CCVS**

![Figure 12.5 — A two-mesh circuit containing a current-controlled voltage source whose value is 3i~x~, where i~x~ is the current down through the 4 Ω resistor.](fig/ch12_ccvs.svg){width=70%}

In Figure 12.5, find the mesh currents, i~x~ and the voltage of the dependent source.

**Step 1: Controlling variable.** i~x~ flows down through the 4 Ω resistor. In terms of mesh currents, i~x~ = I~1~ − I~2~.

**Step 2: KVL in mesh 1** (clockwise):

−10 + 2I~1~ + 4(I~1~ − I~2~) = 0,   so   6I~1~ − 4I~2~ = 10    (1)

**Step 3: KVL in mesh 2** (clockwise). Walking down through the CCVS, we enter at its + terminal, a drop of 3i~x~:

4(I~2~ − I~1~) + 2I~2~ + 3i~x~ = 0

Substitute i~x~ = I~1~ − I~2~:

4I~2~ − 4I~1~ + 2I~2~ + 3I~1~ − 3I~2~ = 0,   so   −I~1~ + 3I~2~ = 0    (2)

**Step 4: Solve.** From (2), I~1~ = 3I~2~. Into (1): 18I~2~ − 4I~2~ = 10, so **I~2~ = 0.714 A** and **I~1~ = 2.143 A**.

**Step 5: Results.** i~x~ = 2.143 − 0.714 = 1.429 A. The dependent source voltage is 3 × 1.429 = 4.29 V.

**Power check.** The 10 V source delivers 10 × 2.143 = 21.43 W. The CCVS carries I~2~ = 0.714 A into its + terminal, so it absorbs 4.29 × 0.714 = 3.06 W. The resistors absorb 2 × 2.143² + 4 × 1.429² + 2 × 0.714² = 9.18 + 8.16 + 1.02 = 18.37 W. Total absorbed 21.43 W ✓.
:::

Interestingly, the mesh currents here are identical to those of Worked Example 11.1, because the dependent source happens to produce 4.29 V, which with these resistances has the same effect as the 5 V battery and 1 Ω there. Coincidences like this are rare; do not count on them.

## 12.4 A General Procedure

We can now state nodal and mesh analysis in their completely general forms.

**General nodal analysis**

1. Choose a reference node and label the other nodes.
2. For each voltage source (independent or dependent): if it is connected to the reference, it fixes a node voltage; if it is floating, form a supernode and write its constraint equation.
3. Write KCL at every remaining node and supernode.
4. For each dependent source, express its controlling variable in terms of node voltages.
5. Solve, then check.

**General mesh analysis**

1. Check the circuit is planar; assign clockwise mesh currents.
2. For each current source (independent or dependent): if it is on the outer edge, it fixes a mesh current; if it is shared, form a supermesh and write its constraint equation.
3. Write KVL round every remaining mesh and supermesh.
4. For each dependent source, express its controlling variable in terms of mesh currents.
5. Solve, then check.

Look at how closely the two lists mirror each other. They are the same procedure seen through the lens of duality.

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Writing KCL at each node of a supernode separately** and forgetting the unknown source current. Use the boundary.
- **Forgetting the constraint equation.** A supernode or supermesh always needs one.
- **Writing KVL round a mesh containing a current source.** Its voltage is unknown; go round the supermesh instead.
- **Getting the constraint sign wrong.** Check carefully which mesh current flows in the same direction as the source.
- **Switching off dependent sources** when using superposition or finding Thévenin resistance. Never do this.
- **Leaving the controlling variable undefined.** Every dependent source needs an extra relation linking its control to your unknowns.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A voltage source between two non-reference nodes requires a **supernode**: KCL round a boundary enclosing both nodes, plus the **constraint** V~+~ − V~−~ = E.
- A current source shared by two meshes requires a **supermesh**: KVL round the combined loop avoiding the source, plus the **constraint** relating the two mesh currents.
- Each voltage source reduces the number of nodal unknowns by one; each current source reduces the number of mesh unknowns by one.
- **Dependent sources** (VCVS, CCVS, VCCS, CCCS) model amplifiers and transistors. Their values depend on a voltage or current elsewhere.
- Include them by writing equations as usual and adding an equation for the **controlling variable**.
- Dependent sources break the **symmetry** of the coefficient matrix and must **never be switched off**.

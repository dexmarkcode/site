Years ago, a friend who repaired hi-fi equipment showed me an old valve amplifier with a small switch on the back marked "4 Ω – 8 Ω – 16 Ω". "Set that wrong," he said, "and you either lose half your volume or cook the output transformer." His customers rarely understood why the setting mattered. It mattered because of one of the most quoted and most misunderstood results in electrical engineering: the **maximum power transfer theorem**.

The theorem answers a simple question. You have a source – a battery, an amplifier, an antenna, a sensor – whose behaviour at its terminals is described by a Thévenin equivalent. You can choose the load. Which load resistance will draw the most power from it? The answer is wonderfully neat, and it is used every day in the design of radio receivers, audio equipment, sensors and solar power systems.

But the theorem comes with a catch that catches many people out. Maximum power is not the same as maximum *efficiency*, and in some of the most important applications – including the national electricity grid – maximising power transfer would be a disastrous idea. Understanding when to match and when not to is a mark of a thoughtful engineer.

::: {.outcomes}
**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- derive the condition for maximum power transfer from a Thévenin source;
- calculate the maximum power available from any linear network;
- explain why efficiency is only 50 % at maximum power transfer;
- decide when matching is desirable and when it is not;
- combine Thévenin's theorem with maximum power transfer to solve design problems;
- explain the principle of maximum power point tracking in solar systems.
:::

## 16.1 Setting Up the Problem

Thanks to Thévenin's theorem, every linear source, however complicated, looks like a voltage source V~Th~ in series with a resistance R~Th~. So the general problem reduces to the simple circuit of Figure 16.1: a Thévenin source driving a variable load R~L~.

![Figure 16.1 — A Thévenin source driving a variable load. Which value of R~L~ receives the greatest power?](fig/ch16_circuit.svg){width=56%}

The load current is I~L~ = V~Th~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~), so the power in the load is

::: eq
P~L~ = I~L~² R~L~ = {{frac|V~Th~² R~L~|(R~Th~ + R~L~)²}}
:::

Think about the two extremes before doing any mathematics.

- If R~L~ = 0 (a short circuit), the current is as large as it can be, but the voltage across the load is zero, so P~L~ = V × I = 0.
- If R~L~ is infinite (an open circuit), the voltage across the load is as large as it can be (V~Th~), but the current is zero, so again P~L~ = 0.

Somewhere between these extremes, the power must reach a maximum. Our job is to find where.

## 16.2 Finding the Maximum

### A numerical exploration

Let us use the source from Worked Example 14.1: V~Th~ = 16 V and R~Th~ = 6 Ω. Table 16.1 shows the load power for a range of load resistances.

::: {.table-wrap}
| R~L~ (Ω) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | **6** | 8 | 10 | 12 | 18 | 30 |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| P~L~ (W) | 5.22 | 8.00 | 9.48 | 10.24 | **10.67** | 10.45 | 10.00 | 9.48 | 8.00 | 5.93 |
| η (%) | 14.3 | 25.0 | 33.3 | 40.0 | **50.0** | 57.1 | 62.5 | 66.7 | 75.0 | 83.3 |

: Table 16.1 — Load power and efficiency for a 16 V, 6 Ω source.
:::

![Figure 16.2 — Load power against load resistance for the source of Table 16.1. The peak occurs where R~L~ equals R~Th~.](fig/ch16_power.svg){width=86%}

The power peaks at 10.67 W when R~L~ = 6 Ω – exactly equal to R~Th~. Notice also a curious symmetry in the table: R~L~ = 3 Ω and 12 Ω give the same power, as do 2 Ω and 18 Ω. Each pair of resistances multiplies to 36 = R~Th~². The power curve is symmetrical about R~Th~ on a logarithmic scale, which is clearer in Figure 16.3.

### A proof

For readers who have met differentiation, here is the formal argument. (If you have not, skip to the result; the numerical exploration has already shown you that it is true.)

We want the value of R~L~ that makes dP~L~/dR~L~ = 0. Using the quotient rule on P~L~ = V~Th~² R~L~ (R~Th~ + R~L~)^−2^:

::: eq
{{frac|dP~L~|dR~L~}} = V~Th~² {{frac|(R~Th~ + R~L~)² − 2R~L~(R~Th~ + R~L~)|(R~Th~ + R~L~)^4^}} = V~Th~² {{frac|R~Th~ − R~L~|(R~Th~ + R~L~)³}}
:::

This is zero when R~Th~ − R~L~ = 0, that is, when R~L~ = R~Th~. It is positive for smaller R~L~ and negative for larger R~L~, so the stationary point is indeed a maximum.

::: keyeq
**The maximum power transfer theorem**

A linear source delivers maximum power to a load when the load resistance equals the source's Thévenin resistance:

R~L~ = R~Th~     and then     P~max~ = {{frac|V~Th~²|4R~Th~}}
:::

The expression for P~max~ follows by substituting R~L~ = R~Th~: the current is V~Th~/(2R~Th~), and the power is that squared times R~Th~. When the load is chosen in this way, it is said to be **matched** to the source.

::: example
**Worked Example 16.1 — The maximum power available**

For the circuit of Worked Example 14.1, what load gives maximum power, and how much power is that?

**Solution.** From the Thévenin equivalent, V~Th~ = 16 V and R~Th~ = 6 Ω. The matched load is R~L~ = 6 Ω, and

P~max~ = {{frac|16²|4 × 6}} = {{frac|256|24}} = 10.67 W

This agrees with Table 16.1. Notice that we did not need to know anything about the inside of the original circuit beyond its Thévenin equivalent.
:::

### The procedure for any network

1. Remove the load and find the Thévenin equivalent (V~Th~, R~Th~) seen from the load's terminals.
2. Set R~L~ = R~Th~ for maximum power.
3. Calculate P~max~ = V~Th~² / (4R~Th~).

::: example
**Worked Example 16.2 — Maximum power from a bridge**

In the unbalanced bridge of Worked Example 14.3, the middle resistor R~5~ is to be replaced by a variable resistor. What value draws the most power, and how much?

**Solution.** We found V~Th~ = 4.593 V and R~Th~ = 4.780 Ω between C and D. So the best choice is R~5~ = 4.78 Ω, and

P~max~ = {{frac|4.593²|4 × 4.780}} = {{frac|21.10|19.12}} = 1.10 W

With the original 9 Ω resistor, the power was only 0.333² × 9 = 1.00 W, so matching gains about 10 %. Close to the peak the curve is quite flat, which is useful in practice: a load within a factor of two of the matched value gets at least 89 % of the maximum power.
:::

That last remark is worth checking. With R~L~ = 2R~Th~, P~L~ = V~Th~² × 2R~Th~ / (3R~Th~)² = (8/9) × V~Th~²/(4R~Th~), which is 89 % of P~max~. The same is true for R~L~ = R~Th~/2. Matching does not need to be precise to be worthwhile.

## 16.3 The Price of Maximum Power: Efficiency

Now for the catch. When the load is matched, R~L~ = R~Th~, so the same current flows through two equal resistances. Exactly as much power is dissipated inside the source (in R~Th~) as is delivered to the load. The efficiency is

::: eq
η = {{frac|P~L~|P~L~ + P~internal~}} = {{frac|R~L~|R~Th~ + R~L~}} = 50 % at matching
:::

At maximum power transfer, **half the power is wasted inside the source**. Figure 16.3 shows load power and efficiency together. Efficiency keeps rising as R~L~ increases, approaching 100 % when the load is much larger than the source resistance – but by then the load is receiving only a small fraction of the maximum available power.

![Figure 16.3 — Load power (as a percentage of the maximum) and efficiency against R~L~/R~Th~. Power peaks at matching, where efficiency is only 50 %; high efficiency requires R~L~ much greater than R~Th~.](fig/ch16_efficiency.svg){width=86%}

So there is a fundamental trade-off, and the right choice depends entirely on what you are trying to achieve.

## 16.4 When to Match and When Not To

### Do not match: power systems

Imagine a power station feeding the National Grid. If the grid were "matched" to the generators, half of all the electricity generated would be burned up inside the generators and transmission network. That would be absurd, and also extremely dangerous, since the generators would overheat. Power systems are designed for the opposite extreme: the source resistance is made as small as possible compared with the load, so that efficiency is very high. The aim is to deliver a fixed *voltage* efficiently, not to extract the maximum possible power.

::: example
**Worked Example 16.3 — The car battery that would melt**

A car battery has an EMF of 12.6 V and an internal resistance of 0.02 Ω. What is the maximum power it could theoretically deliver, and at what current? Why is this never done?

**Solution.** The matched load would be 0.02 Ω. P~max~ = 12.6² / (4 × 0.02) = 158.76 / 0.08 = 1984 W, at a current of 12.6 / 0.04 = 315 A.

**Why not?** At matching, another 1984 W would be dissipated *inside* the battery. Its internal temperature would climb rapidly, its electrolyte would boil and it could split open or catch fire. A starter motor draws a large current for a few seconds, which the battery survives; continuous matched operation it would not. Batteries, like power stations, are designed to work with loads much larger than their internal resistance.
:::

The same principle applies to the amplifier from the start of this chapter. A modern transistor audio amplifier has an output resistance of a small fraction of an ohm, far less than its 8 Ω loudspeaker. It is deliberately *not* matched; it behaves as a voltage source, which gives better control of the loudspeaker cone and much higher efficiency. (Old valve amplifiers used an output transformer to make the loudspeaker look like the right load for the valves, which is why that switch on the back mattered so much.)

### Do match: signals, sensors and communication

In other applications, the power available is tiny and every bit of it matters, while efficiency is irrelevant because the energy comes free from the outside world. Here matching is essential.

- **Radio receivers.** The power arriving at an antenna from a distant transmitter may be a few billionths of a watt. Receivers are designed with an input resistance matched to the antenna (typically 50 Ω or 75 Ω) to capture as much of it as possible.
- **Sensors and energy harvesting.** Devices that scavenge energy from vibration, heat or radio waves are matched to their sources to extract the maximum power.
- **Transmission lines.** In high-frequency systems, matching also prevents signals from reflecting back along cables, which is why television aerial leads, laboratory coaxial cables and network cables all have specified characteristic impedances.

::: example
**Worked Example 16.4 — Power from an antenna**

A radio antenna behaves as a source with an open-circuit voltage of 2 mV and a resistance of 50 Ω. What is the maximum power a receiver can extract, and what input resistance should it have?

**Solution.** Match the receiver: R~in~ = 50 Ω. Then

P~max~ = {{frac|(0.002)²|4 × 50}} = {{frac|4 × 10^−6^|200}} = 2 × 10^−8^ W = 20 nW

**Comment.** Twenty billionths of a watt is the most the receiver could ever obtain from this antenna. Any mismatch reduces it further. In this world, a factor of two matters a great deal, and engineers work hard to match antennas, cables and receivers.
:::

### The opposite question

Students sometimes turn the theorem round and conclude that, for a *fixed* load, the source resistance should be made equal to the load. That is wrong. If R~L~ is fixed and you can choose R~Th~, the load power V~Th~²R~L~/(R~Th~ + R~L~)² is greatest when R~Th~ is as *small* as possible – ideally zero. The theorem applies only when the source is given and the load is being chosen.

::: example
**Worked Example 16.5 — Choosing the source, not the load**

A 10 Ω heater is to be powered from a 20 V supply through a series resistance that can be chosen. What series resistance gives maximum heater power?

**Solution.** Heater power = 20² × 10 / (R~s~ + 10)². This is largest when R~s~ = 0, giving 40 W. Making R~s~ = 10 Ω ("matching") would give only 20² × 10 / 400 = 10 W. Any series resistance only reduces the heater power.
:::

## 16.5 Maximum Power Point Tracking in Solar Systems

Solar panels are an important modern application of these ideas, with a twist. A solar cell is not a linear source; its current–voltage characteristic is curved (Figure 16.4). At low voltages it behaves almost like a current source; near its open-circuit voltage, the current falls away steeply. Its power output, V × I, rises with voltage, peaks and then falls sharply.

![Figure 16.4 — Current and power against voltage for a typical solar cell. The power peaks at the maximum power point (MPP), just before the current collapses.](fig/ch16_solar.svg){width=86%}

The peak is called the **maximum power point** (MPP). Unlike the linear case, there is no simple fixed rule like R~L~ = R~Th~; and worse, the MPP moves as the sunlight and temperature change. So solar inverters and charge controllers contain a circuit called a **maximum power point tracker** (MPPT). Every fraction of a second, it nudges the panel's operating voltage up or down a little, measures whether the power has increased, and keeps moving in whichever direction makes the power rise. In effect, it continuously adjusts the load the panel "sees" so that it sits at the top of the power curve.

The principle is exactly that of this chapter – adjust the load for maximum power – applied to a source that is not linear and does not stay still. For a solar panel, the energy from the sun is free and capturing as much of it as possible is the whole point, so here maximising power, rather than efficiency of the electrical transfer, is exactly the right goal.

::: tip
**Matching in One Sentence**

Match when the source's energy is precious and its internal losses do not matter (signals, antennas, solar panels); do not match when efficiency, heat or voltage regulation matter more (power systems, batteries, amplifier outputs).
:::

::: pitfall
**Common Pitfalls in This Chapter**

- **Thinking maximum power means maximum efficiency.** At maximum power, efficiency is only 50 %.
- **Applying the theorem with a fixed load.** If the load is fixed, make the source resistance as small as possible.
- **Forgetting to find the Thévenin equivalent first.** R~Th~ must be the resistance seen by the load, with everything else included.
- **Using P~max~ = V~Th~²/(4R~Th~) when the load is not matched.** For any other load, use P~L~ = V~Th~²R~L~/(R~Th~ + R~L~)².
- **Applying the simple rule to non-linear sources** such as solar cells, where the maximum power point must be found from the actual curve.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- A linear source delivers **maximum power** to a load when **R~L~ = R~Th~**; then P~max~ = V~Th~²/(4R~Th~).
- At matching, the **efficiency is 50 %**: half the power is lost inside the source.
- The power curve is **flat near its peak**: a load within a factor of two of R~Th~ receives at least 89 % of P~max~.
- **Power systems, batteries and modern amplifiers** are deliberately not matched; they aim for high efficiency with R~L~ ≫ R~Th~.
- **Antennas, sensors and signal systems** are matched to capture as much of a small available power as possible.
- If the load is fixed and the source resistance can be chosen, make R~Th~ as **small** as possible.
- **Solar MPPT** applies the maximum-power idea to a non-linear, changing source.

::: keyeq
**Key Equations**

P~L~ = V~Th~²R~L~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~)²     R~L~ = R~Th~ for maximum power     P~max~ = V~Th~² / (4R~Th~)     η = R~L~ / (R~Th~ + R~L~)
:::

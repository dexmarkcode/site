# How to Use This Book

Each chapter is built the same way, so that you always know where to look for what you need.

**Chapter introduction.** A short paragraph in italics at the start of every chapter tells you what the chapter covers and why it matters.

**Main text.** The body of each chapter explains the physics and engineering, building from first principles to current practice. Numbered sections (for example, Section 4.3) make cross-references easy to follow.

**Worked Examples.** Shaded boxes with a teal border take you through a calculation step by step. Read them with a pencil. Try to predict the answer before you read the solution; the moments when your prediction is wrong are the moments you learn the most.

**Case Files.** Each chapter contains at least one Case File, framed in navy. These are realistic engineering problems, the kind that land on an engineer's desk as a complaint, a failed test or a field report. They are worked through using the seven-step method set out in Chapter 1: state the problem in numbers, draw the boundary, build a simple model, estimate and measure, find the dominant term, cross-check the fix, and write it down. The details are composites of the sort of problems the industry meets, not accounts of any particular company or product.

**Engineer's Notes.** Short boxes between ruled lines give practical advice, rules of thumb and warnings that do not fit neatly into the main argument.

**Field Problems.** Amber-bordered boxes describe a symptom, its likely causes and how to tell them apart. They are written as quick diagnostic guides.

**Chapter Summary.** A dark panel at the end of each chapter lists the key points. If you are revising, start here and go back to any point you cannot explain in your own words.

**Problems.** End-of-chapter problems range from short conceptual questions to multi-step design calculations. Numerical answers to most of them are given in Appendix A.

### Suggested routes through the book

If you are studying a full course on electric vehicles, read the book in order. Each part builds on the previous one.

If you are an engineer joining a battery team, read Chapters 1 to 6 and then Chapter 14 for integration and safety.

If you are joining an electric drive or power electronics team, read Chapters 1 to 3, then 7 to 12, and dip into Chapter 5 for thermal management.

If you work on charging or energy systems, read Chapters 1 to 4, 6, 12 and 13.

If you are a manager or analyst who wants the overall picture, read Chapter 1, the introductions and summaries of every other chapter, and then Chapter 15.

### Notation

Symbols are defined where they first appear and the most common ones are listed below. Vectors and space vectors are printed in bold where needed. Root-mean-square values are written without a subscript unless confusion is possible; peak values carry a circumflex or the subscript *pk*.

Table: **Table 0.1** Frequently used symbols.

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|:---|:---|:---|
| *m* | Vehicle mass | kg |
| *v* | Vehicle speed | m/s |
| *F*, *T* | Force, torque | N, N·m |
| *P*, *E* | Power, energy | W, J or Wh |
| *V*, *I*, *R* | Voltage, current, resistance | V, A, Ω |
| *C*~rr~ | Rolling resistance coefficient | – |
| *C*~d~*A* | Drag coefficient × frontal area | m^2^ |
| SoC, SoH | State of charge, state of health | % |
| *Q* | Cell capacity | Ah |
| *ω*, *n* | Angular speed, rotational speed | rad/s, rpm |
| *p* | Number of pole pairs | – |
| *ψ*, *λ* | Flux linkage | Wb |
| *L*~d~, *L*~q~ | d- and q-axis inductances | H |
| *f*~sw~ | Switching frequency | Hz |
| *η* | Efficiency | – |
| *θ* | Road gradient angle or rotor angle | rad, ° |

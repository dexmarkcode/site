## The Continuous-Time Fourier Transform

::: objectives
- Compute Fourier transforms from the definition for the standard signals.
- Build a working table of transform pairs and use properties to extend it without integrating.
- Invert rational transforms by partial fractions.
- Use the convolution and modulation properties to analyse filters and communication systems.
- Relate duration and bandwidth, and energy in time and frequency.
:::

::: dropcap
The Fourier series handles periodic signals beautifully, but most signals are not periodic. A spoken word, a radar pulse and the impulse response of a filter all happen once. The way forward is a limiting argument that is worth seeing once, because it explains the shape of every formula that follows. Take a single pulse and imagine it repeated with period $T$. As $T$ grows, the harmonics $k\omega_0$ crowd closer together, the coefficients shrink in proportion to $1/T$, and in the limit the sum over harmonics becomes an integral over a continuous range of frequencies. The coefficients, rescaled by $T$, become a continuous function: the Fourier transform.
:::

### The Transform Pair

The Fourier transform and its inverse are
$$X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-j\omega t}\,dt, \qquad x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(j\omega)\,e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega.$$
We write $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$. The notation $X(j\omega)$ rather than $X(\omega)$ is deliberate: it reminds us that the Fourier transform is the Laplace transform evaluated on the imaginary axis, a connection that becomes important in Part III.

The transform exists for any signal with finite energy, and, with impulses allowed, for periodic signals and steps too. For the purposes of this book, if a signal is absolutely integrable, its transform exists and is continuous.

### The Essential Pairs

A small table carries a great deal of weight. Derive each of these once, by integration, and from then on use them as building blocks.

| $x(t)$ | $X(j\omega)$ |
|:--|:--|
| $\delta(t)$ | $1$ |
| $e^{-at}u(t)$, $a > 0$ | $\dfrac{1}{a + j\omega}$ |
| $t\,e^{-at}u(t)$ | $\dfrac{1}{(a + j\omega)^2}$ |
| $e^{-a|t|}$ | $\dfrac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2}$ |
| rectangle, height 1, width $T$ | $\dfrac{2\sin(\omega T/2)}{\omega}$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin Wt}{\pi t}$ | $1$ for $|\omega| < W$, else $0$ |
| $1$ | $2\pi\delta(\omega)$ |
| $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ | $2\pi\delta(\omega - \omega_0)$ |
| $\cos\omega_0 t$ | $\pi[\delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \delta(\omega + \omega_0)]$ |

The rectangle and the sinc deserve a closer look. A rectangular pulse in time has a sinc-shaped spectrum; an ideal rectangular spectrum corresponds to a sinc in time. This is *duality*: the transform and inverse transform have almost the same form, so every pair can be read in both directions.

### Properties That Do the Work

The properties are where the real power lies. Learn them as a set, because a typical problem chains two or three together.

- **Linearity.** Transforms of sums are sums of transforms.
- **Time shift.** $x(t - t_0) \leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)$. Delay adds a phase that is linear in frequency, and leaves the magnitude untouched.
- **Frequency shift.** $x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow X(j(\omega - \omega_0))$.
- **Scaling.** $x(at) \leftrightarrow \dfrac{1}{|a|}X\!\left(\dfrac{j\omega}{a}\right)$. Compress in time, expand in frequency.
- **Conjugation and symmetry.** For real $x$, $|X|$ is even and $\angle X$ is odd.
- **Differentiation.** $dx/dt \leftrightarrow j\omega X(j\omega)$.
- **Multiplication by $t$.** $t\,x(t) \leftrightarrow j\,dX/d\omega$.
- **Convolution.** $x * h \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$.
- **Multiplication.** $x(t)p(t) \leftrightarrow \dfrac{1}{2\pi}X * P$.
- **Parseval.** $\displaystyle\int|x(t)|^2dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int|X(j\omega)|^2d\omega$.

::: keyidea
The convolution property is the reason we study transforms at all. A convolution integral, often the hardest calculation in a problem, becomes a multiplication.
:::

The scaling property expresses something fundamental. A signal cannot be both short in time and narrow in frequency. The product of duration and bandwidth has a lower bound, a fact that sets the resolution of radar, the data rate of a channel, and, in a different guise, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

### Frequency Response

For an LTI system with impulse response $h(t)$, the Fourier transform $H(j\omega)$ is the *frequency response*. It tells us, frequency by frequency, how much the system amplifies and how much it delays. For a system described by a differential equation, transforming term by term turns derivatives into factors of $j\omega$:
$$\frac{dy}{dt} + ay = bx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H(j\omega) = \frac{b}{a + j\omega}.$$
The magnitude and phase of this first-order response are shown below. At $\omega = a$ the magnitude has fallen to $1/\sqrt2$ of its low-frequency value, which is 3 dB down, and the phase is $-45^\circ$. This half-power frequency is the usual measure of the system's bandwidth.

### Inverting Rational Transforms

Most transforms met in practice are ratios of polynomials in $j\omega$. To invert one, treat $j\omega$ as a single variable $v$, expand in partial fractions, and recognise each term from the table. For example,
$$X(j\omega) = \frac{j\omega + 3}{(j\omega + 1)(j\omega + 2)} = \frac{2}{j\omega + 1} - \frac{1}{j\omega + 2}\ \Longrightarrow\ x(t) = (2e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t).$$
The *cover-up rule* finds each coefficient quickly: to find the coefficient of $1/(v + 1)$, cover that factor in the denominator and set $v = -1$ in what remains. We shall develop partial fractions more fully in Chapter 8, where repeated and complex roots also appear.

::: pitfall
Partial fractions only apply directly when the numerator has lower degree than the denominator. If it does not, divide first. A constant term left over corresponds to an impulse in the time domain.
:::

### Modulation

Multiplying a signal by $\cos\omega_c t$ copies its spectrum to $\pm\omega_c$, each copy at half height:
$$m(t)\cos\omega_c t \;\leftrightarrow\; \tfrac12 M(j(\omega - \omega_c)) + \tfrac12 M(j(\omega + \omega_c)).$$
This is how radio works: each station's audio is moved to its own slot in the spectrum. To recover the message, multiply by the same cosine again. One copy returns to baseband and another appears at $2\omega_c$, which a low-pass filter removes. The calculation is a lovely illustration of how the frequency-domain view turns a puzzling circuit into an obvious one.

### Energy and Parseval

Parseval's theorem says that energy can be computed in either domain. Its practical use is to ask what fraction of a signal's energy lies in a band. For $x(t) = e^{-at}u(t)$, the energy in $|\omega| < W$ is a fraction $\tfrac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}(W/a)$ of the total. At $W = a$, the 3 dB frequency, exactly half the energy is captured. That is a neat result and a useful sanity check.

### A Strategy for Transform Problems

::: strategy
1. **Recognise.** Is the signal, or a piece of it, in your table? Most are.
2. **Decompose.** Write the signal as a combination of table entries with shifts, scalings and modulations.
3. **Apply properties one at a time,** writing each step. Chained properties are where errors creep in, especially scaling combined with shifting.
4. **For inverse problems,** reduce to partial fractions in $j\omega$ and read off exponentials.
5. **Check** $X(0)$, which must equal the area $\int x\,dt$, and the symmetry: a real, even signal has a real, even transform.
:::

::: insight
I encourage students to keep a single sheet of paper with the pairs and properties on it and to add to it as they work through the problems. By the end, the sheet becomes a personal reference far more valuable than any printed table, because every line on it is one you have used.
:::

::: remember
- $e^{-at}u(t) \leftrightarrow 1/(a + j\omega)$; rectangle $\leftrightarrow$ sinc; $\delta \leftrightarrow 1$.
- Delay multiplies by $e^{-j\omega t_0}$; modulation shifts the spectrum.
- Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency.
- $X(0) = \int x(t)\,dt$.
- Short in time means wide in frequency.
:::

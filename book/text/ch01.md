## Signals: Classification, Operations and Elementary Functions

::: objectives
- Describe a signal by its type: continuous or discrete, periodic or aperiodic, even or odd, energy or power.
- Work comfortably with the unit step, the ramp, the unit impulse and the complex exponential.
- Shift, reverse and scale a signal in time without the usual slips.
- Find fundamental periods in both continuous and discrete time, including the cases where no period exists.
- Calculate energy and average power, and know at a glance which of the two is the useful measure.
:::

::: dropcap
Every course in signals and systems begins in roughly the same place, and every year a fair number of students decide that the first few weeks are the easy bit. They are not wrong, exactly. Nothing in this chapter is conceptually deep. But when I mark examination scripts, the errors that cost the most marks are rarely failures to understand the Fourier transform. They are a sign dropped in a time shift, a unit step that has wandered to the wrong side of the origin, or an impulse with a scaled argument treated as if it were an ordinary impulse. Those are errors of fluency, and fluency only comes from practice. That is what this chapter is for.
:::

A *signal* is simply a function that carries information. The voltage across a microphone, the daily closing price of a share, the brightness of a pixel as you move along a row of an image: all are signals. In this book we write a continuous-time signal as $x(t)$, with round brackets and a real variable $t$, and a discrete-time signal as $x[n]$, with square brackets and an integer index $n$. The distinction in notation is not pedantry. It reminds you, every time you write it, which set of rules applies.

### The Language of Classification

When you meet a new signal it helps to ask a short list of questions about it, in much the same way that a doctor runs through a routine examination before thinking about the unusual. Is it continuous or discrete? Is it periodic? Is it even, odd or neither? Does it have finite energy, finite power, or neither? Is it deterministic, or is it better treated as random? This book concerns itself with deterministic signals, but the other questions will appear in almost every problem you solve.

#### Periodic signals

A continuous-time signal is periodic if there is a positive number $T$ such that $x(t + T) = x(t)$ for every $t$. The smallest such $T$ is the *fundamental period* $T_0$, and $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency in radians per second. For a single sinusoid $A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ the period is $2\pi/\omega_0$ and that is the end of it.

Sums are more interesting. If $x(t) = x_1(t) + x_2(t)$ with periods $T_1$ and $T_2$, the sum repeats only when both terms repeat at the same moment. That needs whole numbers $m$ and $k$ with $mT_1 = kT_2$, so the ratio $T_1/T_2$ must be rational. When it is, the fundamental period is the lowest common multiple of the two. I find it cleaner to work with frequencies: find the largest $\omega_0$ of which every frequency in the sum is an integer multiple, and then $T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0$. For $\cos(4\pi t) + \sin(6\pi t)$, the frequencies $4\pi$ and $6\pi$ are both multiples of $2\pi$, so $\omega_0 = 2\pi$ and $T_0 = 1$ s.

::: keyidea
A sum of sinusoids is periodic if and only if every pair of frequencies has a rational ratio. The fundamental frequency is then the greatest common divisor of the individual frequencies.
:::

#### The surprising behaviour of discrete sinusoids

Discrete time brings a genuine surprise. The sequence $x[n] = \cos(\omega_0 n)$ is made of samples of a perfectly periodic curve, yet it need not be periodic itself. For $x[n + N] = x[n]$ we need $\omega_0 N = 2\pi m$ for integers $N$ and $m$, which means $\omega_0 / 2\pi = m/N$ must be a rational number. The sequence $\cos(\pi n/8)$ satisfies this with $N = 16$. The sequence $\cos(n/2.5)$ does not, because $1/(5\pi)$ is irrational, and so its samples never line up again exactly. The figure below shows both.

There is a second surprise that catches people out. In continuous time, raising the frequency always produces faster oscillation. In discrete time, the frequencies $\omega_0$ and $\omega_0 + 2\pi$ give exactly the same sequence, because $e^{j(\omega_0 + 2\pi)n} = e^{j\omega_0 n}e^{j2\pi n} = e^{j\omega_0 n}$. The highest rate of oscillation a sequence can have is at $\omega_0 = \pi$, where the samples simply alternate in sign: $\cos(\pi n) = (-1)^n$. Keep this in mind. It is the seed from which the whole theory of sampling and aliasing in Chapter 6 grows.

#### Even and odd signals

A signal is even if $x(-t) = x(t)$ and odd if $x(-t) = -x(t)$. Any signal at all can be split uniquely into an even part and an odd part:
$$x_e(t) = \tfrac12\left[x(t) + x(-t)\right], \qquad x_o(t) = \tfrac12\left[x(t) - x(-t)\right].$$
Adding the two returns $x(t)$; you can check this in your head. The split matters more than it first appears. In Chapter 4 we shall see that the even part of a real signal determines the cosine terms in its Fourier series and the odd part determines the sine terms, and the same pattern runs through every transform in the book.

The next figure shows the decomposition of the one-sided exponential $e^{-t}u(t)$. Its even part is the symmetric two-sided exponential $\tfrac12 e^{-|t|}$, and its odd part is $\tfrac12\operatorname{sgn}(t)e^{-|t|}$. Notice that the odd part must pass through zero at $t = 0$, or at least have zero average there. That is a quick sanity check worth doing every time.

#### Energy and power

The energy of a signal and its average power are defined by
$$E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2\,dt, \qquad P = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt,$$
with sums replacing integrals in discrete time. The names come from circuits: if $x(t)$ is a voltage across a $1\ \Omega$ resistor, $|x(t)|^2$ is the instantaneous power dissipated.

A signal with finite, non-zero energy has zero average power, because a finite quantity spread over an infinite time averages to nothing. A signal with finite, non-zero power has infinite energy. So most signals fall cleanly into one camp or the other. Pulses and decaying exponentials are *energy signals*. Periodic signals and constants are *power signals*. A few, such as the ramp $t\,u(t)$, are neither, because both quantities diverge.

For periodic signals the limit in the power formula reduces to an average over one period, which is much easier to compute. Two results deserve to be memorised. A sinusoid of amplitude $A$ has power $A^2/2$, whatever its frequency or phase. And the powers of sinusoids at different frequencies simply add, because the cross terms average to zero. That second fact is Parseval's theorem in disguise, and you will meet it formally in Chapter 4.

### The Elementary Signals

A handful of simple signals do almost all the work in this subject. The figure below shows four of them.

![Four elementary signals that appear in almost every problem.](figures/ch01_elementary){width=95%}

The **unit step** $u(t)$ is zero for $t < 0$ and one for $t > 0$. Its main job is to switch things on. Multiplying any signal by $u(t)$ makes it zero for negative time, and multiplying by $u(t - t_0)$ makes it zero before $t_0$. A rectangular pulse of height $A$ running from $t_1$ to $t_2$ is just $A[u(t - t_1) - u(t - t_2)]$.

The **ramp** $r(t) = t\,u(t)$ is the running integral of the step. Ramps let us build any piecewise-linear signal, in the same way that steps let us build any piecewise-constant one.

The **unit impulse** $\delta(t)$ is the odd one out. It is not a function in the ordinary sense: it is zero everywhere except at $t = 0$, yet its integral is one. The cleanest way to think of it is as the limit of a tall, narrow pulse of unit area. What matters in practice is how it behaves under an integral. The *sifting property*
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,\delta(t - t_0)\,dt = f(t_0)$$
says that the impulse picks out the value of $f$ at the instant where it fires. Closely related is the product rule $f(t)\,\delta(t - t_0) = f(t_0)\,\delta(t - t_0)$, which you will use constantly when simplifying expressions.

::: pitfall
The impulse with a scaled argument is the commonest trap in this chapter. Because $\delta(at)$ is a pulse squeezed by a factor $|a|$, its area is $1/|a|$, not one:
$$\delta(at - b) = \frac{1}{|a|}\,\delta\!\left(t - \frac{b}{a}\right).$$
Forget the $1/|a|$ and every answer that follows will be wrong by that factor.
:::

The **complex exponential** $e^{st}$, with $s = \sigma + j\omega$, is the most important signal of all, though it takes a few chapters to see why. When $s$ is purely imaginary we get $e^{j\omega t} = \cos\omega t + j\sin\omega t$, a point moving round the unit circle at $\omega$ radians per second. When $\sigma$ is negative the point spirals inwards, and its real part is a decaying sinusoid; when $\sigma$ is positive it spirals outwards. Every signal you will meet in the transform chapters is built, one way or another, from these spirals. The reason is that linear time-invariant systems treat complex exponentials in an especially simple way, as Chapter 2 will show.

You should be able to move between the forms $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $\tfrac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t} + \tfrac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega t}$ without thinking. Euler's formula does the work, and complex arithmetic in polar form makes the bookkeeping painless. If your complex numbers are rusty, the warm-up problems at the end of this chapter will bring them back quickly.

### Operations on the Time Axis

Three operations on the independent variable occur so often that they deserve careful treatment: shifting, reversal and scaling. The figure below illustrates each on the same trapezoidal pulse.

![Shifting, reversing and compressing a trapezoidal pulse.](figures/ch01_transform){width=95%}

*Time shifting.* The signal $x(t - t_0)$ is $x(t)$ delayed by $t_0$, that is, moved to the right when $t_0 > 0$. Students sometimes find it odd that a minus sign moves things to the right. The way to see it is that whatever happened in $x$ at time zero now happens in $x(t - t_0)$ when $t - t_0 = 0$, which is at $t = t_0$.

*Time reversal.* The signal $x(-t)$ is the mirror image of $x(t)$ about the vertical axis. What happened at $t = 2$ now happens at $t = -2$.

*Time scaling.* The signal $x(at)$ with $|a| > 1$ is compressed towards the origin; with $|a| < 1$ it is stretched. Think of playing a recording at double speed.

The difficulty comes when operations are combined, as in $x(2t - 3)$. There are two tidy ways to proceed, and both work if you are careful. The first is to shift first and scale second: form $v(t) = x(t - 3)$, then $y(t) = v(2t)$. The second, which I recommend, is to map the corners. A feature of $x$ at time $t_x$ appears in $y$ at the time $t_y$ that makes the argument equal to $t_x$, that is, $2t_y - 3 = t_x$, or $t_y = (t_x + 3)/2$. Heights do not change. Tabulate the corners of $x$, map each one, and join the dots.

::: strategy
**Mapping corners for $y(t) = x(at + b)$.**

1. List the corners (breakpoints) of $x(t)$ as pairs $(t_x, \text{height})$.
2. Map each one with $t_y = (t_x - b)/a$.
3. If $a < 0$ the order of the corners reverses; the shape is mirrored.
4. Join the mapped points with the same kind of segments as the original.
5. Check one point by direct substitution.
:::

The commonest error is to say that $x(2t - 3)$ is $x(2t)$ shifted right by three. It is not. Writing $x(2t - 3) = x\big(2(t - 1.5)\big)$ shows that the compressed signal is shifted by 1.5. Factor the scale out of the bracket and the true shift is staring at you.

::: insight
In tutorials I ask students to state, before any algebra, where they expect the leading edge of the transformed signal to be. Those who make the prediction catch nearly all of their own mistakes. Those who go straight to the algebra catch almost none. A five-second estimate is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
:::

### Building Signals from Steps and Ramps

Many problems give you a sketch and ask for a formula. The method is the same every time. Read the signal from left to right. At each point where the value jumps, add a step whose coefficient is the size of the jump. At each point where the slope changes, add a ramp whose coefficient is the change in slope. That is all.

Take a staircase that is 2 on $[0, 1)$, then 5 on $[1, 3)$, and zero afterwards. The jumps are $+2$ at $t = 0$, $+3$ at $t = 1$ and $-5$ at $t = 3$, so
$$x(t) = 2u(t) + 3u(t - 1) - 5u(t - 3).$$
A useful check is that the coefficients must sum to the final value of the signal, here zero. For a triangle rising from 0 at $t = 0$ to 1 at $t = 1$ and back to 0 at $t = 2$, the slope changes by $+1$, then $-2$, then $+1$, giving $r(t) - 2r(t - 1) + r(t - 2)$. The slope changes sum to zero because the slope ends where it started.

::: example
**Energy of a scaled, shifted pulse.** Let $x(t)$ have energy $E_x$. What is the energy of $y(t) = 3x(2t - 5)$?

The shift changes nothing, because energy does not care where a signal sits on the time axis. The factor of three multiplies $|x|^2$ by nine. The compression by two halves the time over which the signal lives. Substituting $\lambda = 2t - 5$ in the energy integral confirms it:
$$E_y = \int 9|x(2t - 5)|^2\,dt = \frac{9}{2}\int |x(\lambda)|^2\,d\lambda = 4.5\,E_x.$$
:::

### A Working Method for the Problems

The problems that follow are graded in three bands. The warm-up problems test definitions; the core problems are the kind you will meet in a typical examination; the challenge problems combine ideas and reward care. Whatever the band, I suggest the same habits.

First, *name the signal type* before you calculate anything. If you are about to integrate $|x|^2$ over all time for a periodic signal, stop; the answer is infinite and the question surely wants power.

Second, *draw a sketch*. Even a rough one, made in ten seconds, shows you where a signal is non-zero, which way a shift goes and whether a symmetry can save you work.

Third, *check units and limits*. A period is a time and must be positive. An energy must be non-negative. An odd part must vanish at the origin. If your answer breaks one of these rules, the error is in your working and not in the universe.

Finally, *do the special case*. If you have found the even part of $A e^{-at}u(t)$, set $a = 0$ and see whether the result makes sense for a step. If you have found the period of a sum, check that it is a whole number of periods of each term. These small checks are how professionals work, and they are the habit this book is trying to build.

::: remember
- $x(t - t_0)$ is a delay to the right when $t_0 > 0$.
- $x(at + b) = x\big(a(t + b/a)\big)$: the shift of the scaled signal is $b/a$, not $b$.
- $\delta(at) = \delta(t)/|a|$.
- A sinusoid of amplitude $A$ has power $A^2/2$; sinusoids at different frequencies add their powers.
- $\cos(\omega_0 n)$ is periodic only if $\omega_0/2\pi$ is rational.
:::

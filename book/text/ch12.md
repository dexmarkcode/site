## Discrete-Time Systems and Digital Filters

::: objectives
- Move freely between difference equations, system functions, impulse responses and frequency responses.
- Test second-order sections for stability with the stability triangle.
- Recognise and use linear-phase FIR filters.
- Design digital filters from analogue prototypes by the bilinear transform and by impulse invariance.
- Place poles and zeros to build notch filters and simple smoothers, and choose a realisation.
:::

::: dropcap
This final chapter is where the whole book comes together. A digital filter is an LTI system described by a difference equation; its behaviour at each frequency is a DTFT; its stability is a statement about poles relative to the unit circle; its design often begins with a continuous-time transfer function borrowed from Part III. ### Four Descriptions of One System

A causal LTI system can be described in four equivalent ways, and you should be able to convert between any two of them:

1. **Difference equation:** $y[n] + a_1y[n-1] + \cdots = b_0x[n] + b_1x[n-1] + \cdots$.
2. **System function:** $H(z) = \dfrac{b_0 + b_1z^{-1} + \cdots}{1 + a_1z^{-1} + \cdots}$.
3. **Impulse response:** $h[n]$, the inverse Z-transform of $H(z)$.
4. **Frequency response:** $H(e^{j\omega})$, the system function on the unit circle.

The coefficients of the difference equation appear directly in $H(z)$, so the first conversion is immediate. Two quick evaluations are always worth doing: $H(1)$ is the gain at DC, and $H(-1)$ is the gain at the Nyquist frequency $\omega = \pi$. Comparing them classifies a filter as low-pass or high-pass in seconds.

### FIR and IIR

A filter with no feedback terms ($a_k = 0$) has a finite impulse response, equal to its list of coefficients. *FIR filters* are always stable and can have exactly linear phase. A filter with feedback has an infinite impulse response. *IIR filters* achieve sharp responses with far fewer coefficients, but they can be unstable and their phase is not linear.

#### Linear phase

If the coefficients of an FIR filter are symmetric, $h[n] = h[N - 1 - n]$, then its frequency response factors as $e^{-j\omega(N-1)/2}A(\omega)$ with $A$ real. Every frequency is delayed by the same $(N-1)/2$ samples, so the waveform shape is preserved. This matters for audio, for data communication and for any application where pulses must keep their shape. Antisymmetric coefficients give the same constant delay with an extra $90^\circ$ shift, useful for differentiators.

The four types of linear-phase FIR filter (symmetric or antisymmetric, odd or even length) have forced zeros at $\omega = 0$ or $\omega = \pi$ in some cases, which restricts what each can do. A symmetric, even-length filter must have a zero at $\omega = \pi$ and so cannot be high-pass.

### Stability of Second-Order Sections

Practical IIR filters are built from second-order sections with denominators $1 + a_1z^{-1} + a_2z^{-2}$. Rather than solving for the roots each time, use the *stability triangle*: both poles lie inside the unit circle exactly when
$$|a_2| < 1 \quad\text{and}\quad |a_1| < 1 + a_2.$$
These conditions describe a triangle in the $(a_1, a_2)$ plane, and any coefficient pair inside it gives a stable section.

### Simple Filters by Pole–Zero Placement

Many useful filters can be designed simply by placing poles and zeros where you want them.

- **Moving average.** $N$ equal coefficients put zeros at the $N$th roots of unity other than $z = 1$, giving a low-pass response with deep nulls.
- **First-order smoother.** $y[n] = \alpha y[n-1] + (1 - \alpha)x[n]$ has a single pole at $\alpha$. The closer $\alpha$ is to one, the heavier the smoothing and the longer the memory. It mimics an RC filter with $\alpha = e^{-T/RC}$.
- **Notch.** Zeros on the unit circle at $e^{\pm j\omega_0}$ block one frequency completely; poles just inside at $re^{\pm j\omega_0}$ restore the gain everywhere else. The notch width is roughly $2(1 - r)$.

![Magnitude responses of three simple filters.](figures/ch12_filters){width=98%}

The intuition behind all three is geometric. The magnitude $|H(e^{j\omega})|$ is the product of distances from the point $e^{j\omega}$ on the unit circle to the zeros, divided by the product of distances to the poles. Walk round the circle and watch the distances change, and you have the frequency response.

### Designing from Analogue Prototypes

A great deal of analogue filter theory, Butterworth and Chebyshev designs among it, can be reused by mapping $H(s)$ to $H(z)$.

#### The bilinear transform

Substitute
$$s = \frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}.$$
This maps the left half $s$-plane exactly onto the inside of the unit circle, so stable analogue filters always give stable digital ones, and there is no aliasing. The price is *frequency warping*: the analogue frequency $\Omega$ lands at $\omega = 2\tan^{-1}(\Omega T/2)$. At low frequencies the map is nearly linear, but the whole infinite analogue axis is squeezed into $0 \le \omega < \pi$. When the digital cutoff must be exact, *prewarp* by designing the analogue prototype at $\Omega_c = \tfrac2T\tan(\omega_c/2)$.

![Frequency warping in the bilinear transform.](figures/ch12_warp){width=74%}

#### Impulse invariance

Alternatively, sample the analogue impulse response: $h[n] = T\,h_a(nT)$. Each analogue pole at $s = -p$ becomes a digital pole at $z = e^{-pT}$. The time-domain shape is preserved exactly, but because sampling replicates the spectrum, frequencies above $\pi/T$ alias. Impulse invariance is therefore suitable for low-pass and band-pass designs only.

::: keyidea
Bilinear transform: no aliasing, but frequencies are warped. Impulse invariance: time response preserved, but frequencies alias. Choose according to which defect your application can tolerate.
:::

### Realisation

The same $H(z)$ can be implemented in several structures. *Direct form II* uses the minimum number of delays and is the usual textbook choice. In fixed-point hardware, though, high-order direct forms are very sensitive to rounding of the coefficients, because the poles of a high-degree polynomial move a long way when its coefficients change slightly. The professional practice is to factor $H(z)$ into first- and second-order sections and implement them in *cascade*. Each pole pair then depends on only two coefficients.

::: pitfall
A filter that is stable on paper can become unstable after its coefficients are rounded to a few bits, particularly when poles are close to the unit circle. Narrow notches and sharp low-pass filters are the usual victims. Use cascaded second-order sections, and check the stability triangle with the rounded coefficients.
:::

::: strategy
**Digital filter problems.**

1. Write $H(z)$ from the difference equation; find poles and zeros.
2. Stability: poles inside the unit circle (or the stability triangle for each second-order section).
3. Character: compare $H(1)$ and $H(-1)$; sketch $|H|$ from pole and zero distances.
4. Steady state to $\cos(\omega_0 n)$: evaluate $H(e^{j\omega_0})$.
5. For designs from analogue prototypes, decide between bilinear (prewarp the critical frequency) and impulse invariance (low-pass only).
:::

::: insight
When I first implemented a notch filter on a small microcontroller to remove mains hum from an instrument, it worked perfectly in the simulator and oscillated wildly on the hardware. The culprit was coefficient rounding, exactly as described above. I mention it because the lesson stuck far better than any lecture could have, and I would rather you learned it from this page than from a smoking circuit board.
:::

::: remember
- $H(1)$ is the DC gain; $H(-1)$ the Nyquist gain.
- Symmetric FIR coefficients give linear phase with delay $(N-1)/2$.
- Second-order stability: $|a_2| < 1$ and $|a_1| < 1 + a_2$.
- Bilinear: $s = \tfrac2T\tfrac{z-1}{z+1}$, warping $\omega = 2\tan^{-1}(\Omega T/2)$.
- Impulse invariance: $z = e^{sT}$ for each pole; beware aliasing.
:::

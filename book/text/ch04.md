## Continuous-Time Fourier Series

::: objectives
- Represent a periodic signal as a sum of harmonically related complex exponentials.
- Find Fourier coefficients by inspection where possible and by integration where necessary.
- Use symmetry to predict which coefficients vanish before doing any work.
- Apply Parseval's relation to compute power and the share of power in a band of harmonics.
- Find the steady-state response of an LTI system to any periodic input.
:::

::: dropcap
In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any periodic function could be written as a sum of sines and cosines. The leading mathematicians of the day did not believe him, and in a sense they were right to be cautious: the precise conditions took the rest of the century to settle. But engineers were quick to see the practical value. If a signal is a sum of sinusoids, and an LTI system treats each sinusoid separately, then the behaviour of the system on any periodic signal reduces to its behaviour on one sinusoid at a time. This chapter makes that idea concrete.
:::

### The Exponential Fourier Series

A periodic signal $x(t)$ with fundamental period $T$ and fundamental frequency $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ can be written as
$$x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k\,e^{jk\omega_0 t}, \qquad a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{T} x(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt.$$
The first equation is the *synthesis* equation; it builds the signal from its harmonics. The second is the *analysis* equation; it extracts the amount of each harmonic present. The integral may be taken over any interval of length $T$, which is a freedom worth exploiting: choose the interval that makes the signal easiest to write down.

Why does the analysis formula work? Because the exponentials are *orthogonal* over a period. The integral of $e^{jm\omega_0 t}$ over a full period is zero unless $m = 0$, in which case it equals $T$. Multiplying the synthesis equation by $e^{-jn\omega_0 t}$ and integrating over a period kills every term except $k = n$, leaving $T a_n$. It is the same trick as finding the components of a vector by taking dot products with the basis vectors, and that analogy is a good one to keep in mind.

The coefficient $a_0$ is the average value of the signal over a period, its *DC component*. The pair $a_{\pm 1}$ describes the fundamental, $a_{\pm 2}$ the second harmonic, and so on.

### Coefficients by Inspection

Before reaching for an integral, look at the signal. If it is already a sum of sinusoids, Euler's formulas give the coefficients immediately:
$$\cos\theta = \tfrac12 e^{j\theta} + \tfrac12 e^{-j\theta}, \qquad \sin\theta = \tfrac{1}{2j}e^{j\theta} - \tfrac{1}{2j}e^{-j\theta}.$$
For $x(t) = 2 + 4\cos(3t) - 6\sin(6t)$ with $\omega_0 = 3$, we read off $a_0 = 2$, $a_{\pm1} = 2$ and $a_{\pm 2} = \pm 3j$, since $-6/(2j) = 3j$. A phase in the cosine rides along: $A\cos(k\omega_0 t + \phi)$ contributes $\tfrac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}$ to $a_k$ and $\tfrac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}$ to $a_{-k}$.

::: pitfall
$1/j = -j$. A surprising number of sign errors in Fourier series come from forgetting this. When you see $\sin$, expect imaginary coefficients, and check the sign twice.
:::

### Symmetry Saves Work

Several symmetries tell you about the coefficients before you calculate anything.

- **Real signals** have conjugate-symmetric coefficients: $a_{-k} = a_k^*$. So you only ever need to compute $a_k$ for $k \ge 0$.
- **Real and even signals** have real, even coefficients: only cosines appear.
- **Real and odd signals** have purely imaginary, odd coefficients: only sines appear, and $a_0 = 0$.
- **Half-wave symmetry**, $x(t + T/2) = -x(t)$, kills every even harmonic. Square and triangular waves centred on zero have this property, which is why their series contain only odd $k$.

If you can shift a signal in time to make it even, do so: compute the coefficients of the even version, then apply the time-shift property at the end.

### Three Signals Worth Knowing

The rectangular pulse train, the sawtooth and the triangle wave between them cover most of what examination problems ask.

For a pulse train of height $A$, width $\tau$ and period $T$, centred on the origin,
$$a_k = \frac{A\sin(k\pi\tau/T)}{k\pi} = \frac{A\tau}{T}\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{k\tau}{T}\right), \qquad a_0 = \frac{A\tau}{T}.$$
The coefficients follow a sinc-shaped envelope, shown in the figure below, and they vanish wherever $k\tau/T$ is a non-zero integer. With a duty cycle of one quarter, every fourth harmonic is missing.

![Fourier coefficients of a pulse train sit under a sinc envelope.](figures/ch04_linespec){width=90%}

For a sawtooth swinging between $\pm A$, the coefficients are $a_k = jA(-1)^k/(\pi k)$, and they fall off as $1/k$. For a triangle wave of peak $A$, only odd harmonics appear and $a_k = 4A/(\pi^2k^2)$, falling as $1/k^2$.

That difference in decay rate is not a coincidence. A signal with a jump has coefficients that decay as $1/k$; a continuous signal with a kink, like the triangle, decays as $1/k^2$; each extra degree of smoothness buys another power of $k$. You can often predict how quickly a series converges just by looking at the waveform.

::: keyidea
Smoothness in time means rapid decay in frequency. Sharp edges need high harmonics.
:::

### Convergence and the Gibbs Phenomenon

A finite number of harmonics never reproduces a jump exactly. As more terms are added, the partial sums converge to the signal everywhere except at the discontinuities, where they converge to the midpoint of the jump. Near each jump, though, the partial sums overshoot, and the overshoot does not go away. It narrows as more terms are added, but its height settles at about 9% of the jump. This is the *Gibbs phenomenon*, shown below for a square wave.

![Partial sums of a square wave. The ripple near each jump narrows but does not shrink.](figures/ch04_gibbs){width=96%}

In practice the Gibbs effect turns up as ringing on sharp edges in audio, images and digital filters whenever a spectrum is cut off abruptly. The remedy, as we shall see in Chapter 12, is to taper the cut gently rather than chop it.

### Properties

The properties of the Fourier series let you find new coefficients from old ones without integrating. If $x(t)$ has coefficients $a_k$, then:

| Operation | Coefficients |
|:--|:--|
| $x(t - t_0)$ | $a_k e^{-jk\omega_0 t_0}$ |
| $x(-t)$ | $a_{-k}$ |
| $x(ct)$, $c > 0$ | $a_k$, with fundamental $c\omega_0$ |
| $dx/dt$ | $jk\omega_0\,a_k$ |
| $x(t)e^{jM\omega_0 t}$ | $a_{k-M}$ |
| $x(t)\cos(M\omega_0 t)$ | $\tfrac12(a_{k-M} + a_{k+M})$ |

The derivative rule is especially handy. The derivative of a triangle wave is a square wave, whose coefficients are easy; dividing by $jk\omega_0$ gives the triangle's coefficients without a single integration by parts.

### Parseval's Relation

The average power of a periodic signal equals the sum of the powers in its harmonics:
$$P = \frac{1}{T}\int_T |x(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|a_k|^2.$$
This lets us ask how much of a signal's power lies below a given frequency, which is precisely the question an engineer asks when choosing the bandwidth of an amplifier or a channel. For a square wave, the fundamental alone carries $8/\pi^2 \approx 81\%$ of the power, and the first three odd harmonics carry about 93%. Remember to count both $k$ and $-k$; forgetting the negative frequencies halves your answer.

### Periodic Signals Through LTI Systems

Here is the payoff. If $x(t) = \sum a_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}$ drives an LTI system with frequency response $H(j\omega)$, then each exponential passes through scaled by $H(jk\omega_0)$, so
$$y(t) = \sum_k a_k H(jk\omega_0)\,e^{jk\omega_0 t}.$$
The output is periodic with the same period, and its coefficients are $b_k = a_k H(jk\omega_0)$. For real signals written as cosines, each term $A_k\cos(k\omega_0 t + \phi_k)$ becomes $A_k|H(jk\omega_0)|\cos(k\omega_0 t + \phi_k + \angle H(jk\omega_0))$.

::: example
**A square wave through an RC filter.** Suppose a $\pm1$ V square wave at 1 kHz drives a low-pass filter with $H(j\omega) = 1/(1 + j\omega/\omega_c)$ and $\omega_c = 2\pi\times1000$ rad/s. The fundamental is at the cut-off, so it is reduced to $1/\sqrt2$ of its amplitude and delayed by $45^\circ$. The third harmonic is reduced by a factor $1/\sqrt{10}$, on top of its natural $1/3$, and delayed by $72^\circ$. The output therefore looks much more like a sine wave than a square wave, with rounded edges, which is exactly what you see on an oscilloscope.
:::

### A Strategy for Fourier Series Problems

::: strategy
1. Identify the period $T$ and fundamental $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$. Check this twice; most wrong answers start here.
2. If the signal is a sum of sinusoids, use Euler's formula and stop.
3. Look for symmetry: real, even, odd, half-wave. Write down which coefficients must vanish.
4. Choose the integration interval that makes the signal simplest, and evaluate $a_k$ for general $k$. Handle $a_0$ separately; formulas with $k$ in the denominator often fail at $k = 0$.
5. Check: $a_0$ should be the average; $a_{-k} = a_k^*$ for real signals; the decay rate should match the smoothness.
:::

::: insight
When students first see Fourier series they tend to regard them as a technique for computing integrals. The better view is that a Fourier series is a *change of description*. The list of coefficients contains exactly the same information as the waveform, written in a language where LTI systems are simple. Once that clicks, the rest of the subject becomes a matter of choosing the description that makes your current problem easy.
:::

::: remember
- $a_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt$; $a_0$ is the average.
- Real signal: $a_{-k} = a_k^*$. Even: real coefficients. Odd: imaginary coefficients.
- Jumps give $1/k$ decay, kinks give $1/k^2$.
- $P = \sum|a_k|^2$, counting both signs of $k$.
- Through an LTI system: $b_k = a_k H(jk\omega_0)$.
:::

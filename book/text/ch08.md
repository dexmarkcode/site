## The Inverse Laplace Transform and Differential Equations

::: objectives
- Invert rational transforms by partial fractions, with distinct, repeated and complex poles.
- Handle improper functions and time delays.
- Solve linear differential equations with initial conditions in one systematic pass.
- Separate the zero-input and zero-state responses and interpret each.
- Analyse first- and second-order circuits with switched sources.
:::

::: dropcap
Transforming a problem is only half the job. Sooner or later the answer has to come back to the time domain, where the engineer, the customer and the oscilloscope live. The inverse Laplace transform is defined by a contour integral in the complex plane, but in practice nobody evaluates it. Almost every transform you meet is a ratio of polynomials, and for those the inversion reduces to one technique: partial fractions. Master it and you can invert anything in this book.
:::

### Partial Fractions: The Three Cases

Let $F(s) = N(s)/D(s)$ with the degree of $N$ less than that of $D$. Factor $D(s)$ into its roots, the poles of $F$. Each pole contributes terms whose form depends on its type.

#### Distinct real poles

For $F(s) = \dfrac{N(s)}{(s + p_1)(s + p_2)\cdots}$ we write
$$F(s) = \frac{A_1}{s + p_1} + \frac{A_2}{s + p_2} + \cdots, \qquad A_i = \left.(s + p_i)F(s)\right|_{s = -p_i}.$$
This is the *cover-up rule*: cover the factor in the denominator, substitute the pole, and read off the coefficient. Each term inverts to $A_i e^{-p_i t}$.

::: example
**Cover-up in action.** Invert $F(s) = \dfrac{2s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 4)}$.

Cover $(s + 1)$ and put $s = -1$: $A_1 = \dfrac{-2 + 3}{-1 + 4} = \dfrac13$. Cover $(s + 4)$ and put $s = -4$: $A_2 = \dfrac{-8 + 3}{-4 + 1} = \dfrac53$. So $f(t) = \left(\tfrac13 e^{-t} + \tfrac53 e^{-4t}\right)u(t)$. As a check, $f(0^+) = 2$, which agrees with $\lim_{s\to\infty}sF(s) = 2$.
:::

#### Repeated poles

A factor $(s + a)^2$ needs two terms, $\dfrac{B_1}{s + a} + \dfrac{B_2}{(s + a)^2}$. The cover-up rule still gives the coefficient of the highest power, $B_2$. For $B_1$, either differentiate $(s + a)^2F(s)$ once and evaluate at $s = -a$, or, often quicker, substitute a convenient value of $s$ and compare. The $(s + a)^{-2}$ term inverts to $t\,e^{-at}$, the signature of a repeated pole.

#### Complex poles

A quadratic factor with complex roots, $s^2 + bs + c$ with $b^2 < 4c$, should be kept intact. Complete the square, $(s + \alpha)^2 + \beta^2$, and write the numerator in the form $B(s + \alpha) + C\beta$. Then
$$\frac{B(s + \alpha) + C\beta}{(s + \alpha)^2 + \beta^2} \;\longleftrightarrow\; e^{-\alpha t}\left[B\cos\beta t + C\sin\beta t\right].$$
You could instead split into complex conjugate first-order terms, but the arithmetic with complex numbers is error-prone and the answer has to be recombined anyway. Completing the square goes straight to the real form.

::: keyidea
Real poles give exponentials, repeated poles give a factor of $t$, and complex poles give damped sinusoids. Before you calculate a single coefficient, you already know the *shape* of the answer.
:::

### Two Complications

*Improper functions.* If the numerator's degree is not less than the denominator's, divide first. The quotient is a polynomial in $s$; a constant term inverts to an impulse $\delta(t)$. This happens in systems with a direct path from input to output, such as a high-pass filter.

*Delays.* A factor $e^{-sT}$ means 'delay by $T$'. Set it aside, invert the rest, and then replace $t$ by $t - T$ everywhere, including in the step.

### Solving Differential Equations

Here is the method that made the Laplace transform indispensable. Consider
$$y'' + 5y' + 6y = x(t), \qquad y(0^-) = y_0, \quad y'(0^-) = y_1.$$
Transform term by term, using the derivative rules:
$$\big[s^2Y - sy_0 - y_1\big] + 5\big[sY - y_0\big] + 6Y = X(s).$$
Collect the terms in $Y$:
$$Y(s) = \frac{X(s)}{s^2 + 5s + 6} + \frac{(s + 5)y_0 + y_1}{s^2 + 5s + 6}.$$
Then invert by partial fractions. One pass, no separate particular and complementary solutions, no constants to fit at the end: the initial conditions are built in from the start.

The two terms in $Y(s)$ have names. The first is the *zero-state response*: the output when the system starts at rest. The second is the *zero-input response*: what the system does from its initial state with no input at all. Their sum is the complete response. The zero-state part is what the transfer function describes; the zero-input part is governed by the same poles but driven by stored energy.

### The Shape of Second-Order Responses

The denominator $s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2$ appears in so many problems that its behaviour should become second nature. The damping ratio $\zeta$ decides everything.

- $\zeta > 1$: two real poles, *overdamped*, a sluggish rise with no overshoot.
- $\zeta = 1$: a repeated pole, *critically damped*, the fastest rise without overshoot.
- $0 < \zeta < 1$: complex poles, *underdamped*, overshoot and ringing at the damped frequency $\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$.

![Step responses of a second-order system for three damping ratios.](figures/ch08_damping){width=90%}

In circuit terms, a series RLC circuit has $\omega_n = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $\zeta = \tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. Raising the resistance adds damping. You can therefore read the character of a circuit's step response from its component values before solving anything.

::: pitfall
When the input itself has a pole that coincides with a system pole, for example $e^{-2t}$ driving a system with a pole at $-2$, the partial fraction expansion has a repeated pole that is not obvious from either factor alone. The response then contains a $t\,e^{-2t}$ term. Always factor the *whole* denominator of $Y(s)$, not just the system's.
:::

::: strategy
**Solving a differential equation by Laplace transform.**

1. Transform every term, inserting the initial conditions through the derivative rules.
2. Solve algebraically for $Y(s)$ and write it over a single factored denominator.
3. Identify the poles: real, repeated or complex. This tells you the form of the answer.
4. Find the partial fraction coefficients, using cover-up wherever possible.
5. Invert term by term.
6. Check: $y(0)$ and $y'(0)$ must match the initial conditions, and the final value should match the DC gain times the final input.
:::

The checks in the last step are not optional. They take a minute and catch almost every error. In the solutions to this chapter's problems, every answer has been checked this way.

::: insight
Students often ask whether they really need to learn partial fractions when software will do it. My answer is yes, for the same reason pilots learn to fly without the autopilot. When the software gives an answer that looks wrong, you need to be able to tell whether it is, and the only way to develop that judgement is to have done enough by hand that the shape of a correct answer is familiar.
:::

::: remember
- Cover-up: $A_i = (s + p_i)F(s)$ evaluated at $s = -p_i$.
- $(s + a)^{-2} \leftrightarrow t\,e^{-at}$.
- Complete the square for complex poles: $e^{-\alpha t}[B\cos\beta t + C\sin\beta t]$.
- Divide first if the function is improper; the constant becomes $\delta(t)$.
- Total response = zero-state + zero-input.
:::

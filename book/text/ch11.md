## The Inverse Z-Transform and Difference Equations

::: objectives
- Invert rational Z-transforms by partial fractions of $X(z)/z$.
- Use the ROC to choose between right-sided, left-sided and two-sided inverses.
- Generate samples by long division and by recursion.
- Handle repeated and complex poles.
- Solve difference equations with initial conditions using the unilateral transform.
:::

::: dropcap
Difference equations are the native language of digital computation. A line of code such as `y = 0.9*y + 0.1*x`, executed once per sample, is a first-order difference equation, and it is probably running somewhere near you right now, smoothing a sensor reading or controlling a motor. To predict what such code will do, we need to solve the equation, and the Z-transform makes that a routine matter. The procedure closely mirrors Chapter 8, with one small trick that saves a great deal of algebra.
:::

### The Trick: Expand $X(z)/z$

Table entries in the Z-domain have a factor of $z$ in the numerator: $a^n u[n] \leftrightarrow z/(z - a)$. If we perform partial fractions on $X(z)$ directly, we get terms like $A/(z - a)$, which are delayed sequences and awkward to write. Instead, expand $X(z)/z$:
$$\frac{X(z)}{z} = \frac{A_1}{z - p_1} + \frac{A_2}{z - p_2} + \cdots \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X(z) = \frac{A_1z}{z - p_1} + \frac{A_2z}{z - p_2} + \cdots,$$
and each term inverts immediately to $A_ip_i^n u[n]$ for a causal sequence. The cover-up rule works exactly as before.

::: example
**A two-pole inversion.** Let $X(z) = \dfrac{z^2}{(z - \tfrac12)(z - \tfrac14)}$, causal.

Then $\dfrac{X(z)}{z} = \dfrac{z}{(z - \tfrac12)(z - \tfrac14)}$. Cover-up at $z = \tfrac12$: $A_1 = \dfrac{1/2}{1/4} = 2$. At $z = \tfrac14$: $A_2 = \dfrac{1/4}{-1/4} = -1$. So $x[n] = \left[2\left(\tfrac12\right)^n - \left(\tfrac14\right)^n\right]u[n]$. Check: $x[0] = 1 = \lim_{z\to\infty}X(z)$. Correct.
:::

If $X(z)/z$ contains a term $A/z$, that corresponds to $A\delta[n]$ in the answer, which often appears when the numerator and denominator have the same degree.

### The ROC Decides

The same algebraic $X(z)$ can correspond to different sequences. For each pole, ask whether the ROC lies outside it, in which case the term is right-sided, $A p^n u[n]$, or inside it, in which case the term is left-sided, $-Ap^nu[-n-1]$. A system with poles at $\tfrac12$ and $2$ can be causal (ROC $|z| > 2$, unstable), anti-causal ($|z| < \tfrac12$, unstable) or two-sided ($\tfrac12 < |z| < 2$, stable but not causal). It cannot be both causal and stable.

### Repeated and Complex Poles

A repeated pole $(z - a)^2$ in $X(z)/z$ contributes $\dfrac{Bz}{(z - a)^2}$, which inverts to $B\,n\,a^{n-1}u[n]$: the discrete counterpart of $t\,e^{-at}$.

A complex pair at $re^{\pm j\theta}$ gives a damped sinusoid. Keep the quadratic $z^2 - 2rz\cos\theta + r^2$ intact, read $r$ from the constant term and $\cos\theta$ from the middle coefficient, and express the numerator as a combination of $z(z - r\cos\theta)$ and $zr\sin\theta$. The inverse is then $r^n[B\cos\theta n + C\sin\theta n]u[n]$.

### Long Division and Recursion

When only the first few samples are needed, or when you want to check a closed form, divide the numerator by the denominator in ascending powers of $z^{-1}$. The quotient is $x[0] + x[1]z^{-1} + x[2]z^{-2} + \cdots$. Equivalently, and usually faster, turn the transform into its difference equation and iterate. A computer does exactly this, which is why the recursion is the most practical check of all.

### Solving Difference Equations

The unilateral Z-transform handles initial conditions through its shift rules:
$$y[n-1] \leftrightarrow z^{-1}Y(z) + y[-1], \qquad y[n-2] \leftrightarrow z^{-2}Y(z) + z^{-1}y[-1] + y[-2].$$
The procedure follows Chapter 8 step for step: transform, collect $Y(z)$, factor, expand $Y(z)/z$, invert.

::: example
**A first-order equation.** Solve $y[n] - \tfrac12 y[n-1] = u[n]$ with $y[-1] = 2$.

Transforming, $Y(z) - \tfrac12\big[z^{-1}Y(z) + 2\big] = \dfrac{z}{z - 1}$, so
$$Y(z)\left(1 - \tfrac12 z^{-1}\right) = \frac{z}{z - 1} + 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y(z) = \frac{z^2}{(z - 1)(z - \tfrac12)} + \frac{z}{z - \tfrac12}.$$
Dividing by $z$ and using cover-up on the first term gives $\dfrac{2}{z - 1} - \dfrac{1}{z - \tfrac12}$; the second term contributes $+\dfrac{1}{z - \tfrac12}$. The two $(z - \tfrac12)$ terms cancel, leaving $Y(z)/z = 2/(z - 1)$ and therefore $y[n] = 2$ for all $n \ge 0$.

Iterating confirms it: $y[0] = \tfrac12\cdot 2 + 1 = 2$, $y[1] = \tfrac12\cdot 2 + 1 = 2$. The initial condition happened to equal the steady-state value $H(1) = 2$, so there was no transient to decay. Change $y[-1]$ and a term in $(\tfrac12)^n$ reappears.
:::

The zero-input and zero-state responses separate just as they did in continuous time: one term comes from the initial conditions, the other from the input. The steady-state value for a step input is the DC gain $H(1)$.

::: pitfall
Be careful with the unilateral shift rules. The extra terms $y[-1]$, $y[-2]$ are values *before* $n = 0$. If a problem gives you $y[0]$ and $y[1]$ instead, either work backwards through the equation to find $y[-1]$ and $y[-2]$, or use the advance rules. Mixing the two conventions is a frequent source of wrong answers.
:::

::: strategy
**Difference equations by Z-transform.**

1. Transform every term, using the unilateral shift rules to insert initial conditions.
2. Solve for $Y(z)$ and factor the denominator.
3. Expand $Y(z)/z$ in partial fractions; use cover-up for simple poles.
4. Multiply back by $z$ and invert term by term.
5. Check by iterating the equation directly for $n = 0, 1, 2$.
:::

::: insight
Every solution in this chapter's problem set ends with a check by direct recursion, and I recommend you make that a habit. It takes a minute, needs nothing but arithmetic, and it is the one check that does not depend on the method you used. If the formula and the recursion agree for three samples, you can be very confident in the formula.
:::

::: remember
- Expand $X(z)/z$, not $X(z)$.
- Outside a pole: $Ap^nu[n]$; inside: $-Ap^nu[-n-1]$.
- Repeated pole: $n\,a^{n-1}$; complex pair: $r^n\cos(\theta n + \phi)$.
- $y[n-1] \leftrightarrow z^{-1}Y + y[-1]$.
- Verify by iterating the difference equation.
:::

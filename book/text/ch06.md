## Discrete-Time Fourier Analysis, the DFT and Sampling

::: objectives
- Compute the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of finite and geometric sequences.
- Understand why every DTFT is periodic in $\omega$ with period $2\pi$.
- State and apply the sampling theorem, and predict aliased frequencies.
- Compute short DFTs by hand and relate the DFT to the DTFT.
- Use the DFT for convolution without falling foul of wrap-around.
:::

::: dropcap
Almost every signal you will process in your career will pass through an analogue-to-digital converter at some point. From then on it is a sequence of numbers, and the continuous-time theory of Chapters 4 and 5 must be rebuilt for discrete time. The good news is that nearly everything carries over. The interesting news is that a few things change in ways that matter enormously, and the most important of them, aliasing, is the source of more practical bugs than any other topic in this book.
:::

### The DTFT

The discrete-time Fourier transform of a sequence $x[n]$ is
$$X(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,e^{-j\omega n}, \qquad x[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\omega})\,e^{j\omega n}\,d\omega.$$
The notation $X(e^{j\omega})$ anticipates the Z-transform of Part IV, just as $X(j\omega)$ anticipated the Laplace transform.

The most striking difference from continuous time is that $X(e^{j\omega})$ is always periodic in $\omega$ with period $2\pi$. The reason is simply that $e^{-j(\omega + 2\pi)n} = e^{-j\omega n}$ for integer $n$. As we saw in Chapter 1, frequencies $2\pi$ apart are indistinguishable in discrete time. So we only ever need to look at $-\pi < \omega \le \pi$. Low frequencies are near $\omega = 0$; the highest possible frequency is $\omega = \pi$, where the sequence alternates in sign.

For a finite sequence, the DTFT is a polynomial in $e^{-j\omega}$ and can be written down immediately. For the geometric sequence $a^n u[n]$ with $|a| < 1$, summing the series gives
$$a^n u[n] \;\leftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{1 - a e^{-j\omega}}.$$
Two values are always worth computing: $X(e^{j0}) = \sum x[n]$, the DC gain, and $X(e^{j\pi}) = \sum(-1)^n x[n]$, the response at the highest frequency. Comparing them tells you at a glance whether a filter is low-pass or high-pass.

The DTFT has properties that mirror those of the continuous transform: delay multiplies by $e^{-j\omega n_0}$, convolution becomes multiplication, and multiplication by $n$ becomes $j\,d/d\omega$. One property has no continuous counterpart worth mentioning: multiplying by $(-1)^n = e^{j\pi n}$ shifts the spectrum by $\pi$, swapping low and high frequencies. A low-pass filter with impulse response $h[n]$ becomes a high-pass filter with impulse response $(-1)^n h[n]$.

### Sampling

Suppose we sample a continuous signal $x(t)$ every $T_s$ seconds, at a rate $f_s = 1/T_s$, to get $x[n] = x(nT_s)$. What happens to the spectrum? The answer, which follows from modelling sampling as multiplication by an impulse train, is that the spectrum of the sampled signal consists of copies of $X(j\omega)$ repeated every $\omega_s = 2\pi f_s$:
$$X_s(j\omega) = \frac{1}{T_s}\sum_k X\big(j(\omega - k\omega_s)\big).$$

![Sampling replicates the spectrum. If the copies overlap, the original cannot be recovered.](figures/ch06_sampling){width=96%}

If $x(t)$ is band-limited to $\omega_M$ and $\omega_s > 2\omega_M$, the copies do not overlap, and an ideal low-pass filter can recover the original exactly. This is the *sampling theorem*, and $2\omega_M$ (or $2f_M$ in hertz) is the *Nyquist rate*.

::: keyidea
A signal band-limited to $f_M$ hertz is completely determined by samples taken at any rate faster than $2f_M$. Sample more slowly and frequencies above $f_s/2$ fold back into the band and masquerade as lower ones.
:::

If the copies overlap, high frequencies masquerade as low ones. This is *aliasing*. The figure below shows the classic case: a 9 Hz cosine sampled at 10 Hz produces exactly the same samples as a 1 Hz cosine. Once the samples are taken, no processing can tell the two apart.

![A 9 Hz cosine and a 1 Hz cosine share every sample when sampled at 10 Hz.](figures/ch06_alias){width=90%}

To find the apparent frequency of a sampled tone, reduce its frequency modulo $f_s$, and if the result is above $f_s/2$, subtract it from $f_s$. A 7 kHz tone sampled at 10 kHz appears at 3 kHz. A 12 kHz tone sampled at 10 kHz appears at 2 kHz. The cure in practice is an *anti-aliasing filter* before the converter, which removes everything above $f_s/2$ while the signal is still analogue.

::: pitfall
Nonlinear operations widen the spectrum. If $x(t)$ is band-limited to $W$, then $x^2(t)$ is band-limited to $2W$, and $x(t)\cos\omega_c t$ extends up to $\omega_c + W$. When finding a Nyquist rate, always find the *highest* frequency present after every operation has been applied.
:::

### The Discrete Fourier Transform

The DTFT is a function of a continuous variable, which a computer cannot store. The DFT samples it at $N$ equally spaced frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k/N$:
$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N}, \qquad x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X[k]\,e^{j2\pi kn/N}.$$
For a sequence of length $N$ or less, the DFT is exactly the DTFT evaluated at those points. Taking a longer DFT, by padding with zeros, simply samples the same DTFT more finely, as the figure shows.

![The 4-point and 16-point DFTs of four ones are samples of the same DTFT.](figures/ch06_dft){width=90%}

For $N = 4$ the twiddle factors $e^{-j2\pi kn/4}$ take only the values $1, -j, -1, j$, so a 4-point DFT can be done in your head once you have practised a few. The checks are always the same: $X[0]$ is the sum of the samples; for a real sequence $X[N - k] = X[k]^*$; and Parseval's relation $\sum|x[n]|^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum|X[k]|^2$ holds.

In practice the DFT is computed by the *fast Fourier transform* (FFT), an algorithm that reduces the work from about $N^2$ operations to about $N\log_2 N$. For $N = 1024$ that is a hundredfold saving, and it is the reason spectral analysis is cheap enough to run in every mobile phone.

#### Frequency resolution

The DFT bins are spaced $\Delta f = f_s/N$ apart in hertz, which equals $1/T$, where $T = N/f_s$ is the duration of the record. To separate two tones $1$ Hz apart you need roughly a one-second record. Zero-padding gives a smoother-looking plot but does not improve the true resolution. Only more data does.

### Circular Convolution

Multiplying two DFTs corresponds not to ordinary convolution but to *circular* convolution, in which the sequences wrap round modulo $N$. If the linear convolution has length $L_1 + L_2 - 1$ and the DFT length $N$ is shorter, the tail of the result wraps round and adds to the beginning. To use the DFT for ordinary filtering, zero-pad both sequences to at least $L_1 + L_2 - 1$ points. With that precaution, convolution by FFT is much faster than direct convolution for long filters, and it is how most audio and image processing software works internally.

::: strategy
**Sampling and DFT problems.**

1. For sampling, find the highest frequency present after every operation. The Nyquist rate is twice that.
2. For aliasing, reduce modulo $f_s$ and fold into $[0, f_s/2]$.
3. For DTFTs, compute $X$ at $\omega = 0$ and $\omega = \pi$ first; they reveal the character of the signal.
4. For DFTs, use the matrix of twiddle factors and check $X[0]$, conjugate symmetry and Parseval.
5. For convolution by DFT, make sure $N \ge L_1 + L_2 - 1$.
:::

::: insight
Every year at least one student project in my laboratory produces a 'mysterious' low-frequency tone that turns out to be aliased mains interference or switching noise from a power supply. The theory in this chapter is not academic. If you design a data-acquisition system, the first question to ask is what lies above $f_s/2$ and how you will keep it out.
:::

::: remember
- The DTFT is periodic with period $2\pi$; the highest frequency is $\omega = \pi$.
- Sample faster than twice the highest frequency present.
- Apparent frequency: reduce modulo $f_s$, fold into $[0, f_s/2]$.
- DFT bin spacing is $f_s/N = 1/T$.
- DFT multiplication is circular convolution; pad to $L_1 + L_2 - 1$.
:::

## The Z-Transform and Its Region of Convergence

::: objectives
- Compute Z-transforms of finite, geometric, sinusoidal and two-sided sequences.
- Determine the region of convergence and use it to identify causality and stability.
- Apply the shift, scaling, differentiation, reversal and convolution properties.
- Plot poles and zeros and interpret them.
- Use the initial and final value theorems for sequences.
:::

::: dropcap
A sequence of numbers is a humble thing. Yet when we attach each number to a power of a complex variable and add them up, something useful happens: the whole infinite sequence collapses into a compact rational function, and operations that were tiresome on the sequence become easy on the function. This is the Z-transform. Its name is plain and its definition is short, but it is the mathematical foundation of every digital filter, every control loop running on a microprocessor and every audio effect on your phone.
:::

### Definition

The Z-transform of a sequence $x[n]$ is
$$X(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,z^{-n},$$
where $z$ is a complex variable. For a finite sequence this is just a polynomial in $z^{-1}$ (and possibly $z$), and you can write it down by inspection: $\{2, -1, 3\}$ starting at $n = 0$ becomes $2 - z^{-1} + 3z^{-2}$. The coefficient of $z^{-n}$ is the sample at time $n$, so $z^{-1}$ behaves as a one-sample delay. That interpretation is worth holding on to; it makes block diagrams and difference equations transparent.

On the unit circle, $z = e^{j\omega}$, the Z-transform becomes the DTFT. So the Z-transform is to the DTFT exactly what the Laplace transform is to the Fourier transform.

### The Geometric Sequence and the ROC

Everything rests on one sum. For $x[n] = a^n u[n]$,
$$X(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(az^{-1}\right)^n = \frac{1}{1 - az^{-1}} = \frac{z}{z - a}, \qquad |z| > |a|.$$
The series converges only when $|az^{-1}| < 1$, that is, outside the circle of radius $|a|$. The left-sided sequence $-a^nu[-n-1]$ gives exactly the same expression but converges *inside* that circle. As with the Laplace transform, the formula is incomplete without its region of convergence.

![Regions of convergence in the $z$-plane. The dashed circle is $|z| = 1$.](figures/ch10_roc){width=98%}

The general rules mirror those in continuous time, with circles in place of vertical lines:

- The ROC is a ring (annulus) centred on the origin, and contains no poles.
- A right-sided sequence has an ROC outside its outermost pole.
- A left-sided sequence has an ROC inside its innermost pole.
- A two-sided sequence has a ring between poles, or no ROC at all.
- A finite sequence has an ROC that is the whole plane, except perhaps $z = 0$ or $z = \infty$.

::: keyidea
A causal LTI system is stable if and only if all poles of $H(z)$ lie strictly inside the unit circle. The unit circle plays the role that the imaginary axis played in the $s$-plane.
:::

The correspondence is no coincidence. Sampling a continuous signal at interval $T$ maps $s$ to $z = e^{sT}$. The left half of the $s$-plane folds into the inside of the unit circle, the imaginary axis wraps onto the circle itself, and the right half-plane maps outside it.

### Standard Pairs

| $x[n]$ | $X(z)$ | ROC |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $\delta[n]$ | $1$ | all $z$ |
| $u[n]$ | $\dfrac{z}{z - 1}$ | $|z| > 1$ |
| $a^n u[n]$ | $\dfrac{z}{z - a}$ | $|z| > |a|$ |
| $-a^n u[-n-1]$ | $\dfrac{z}{z - a}$ | $|z| < |a|$ |
| $n a^n u[n]$ | $\dfrac{az}{(z - a)^2}$ | $|z| > |a|$ |
| $r^n\cos(\theta n)u[n]$ | $\dfrac{z(z - r\cos\theta)}{z^2 - 2rz\cos\theta + r^2}$ | $|z| > r$ |
| $r^n\sin(\theta n)u[n]$ | $\dfrac{zr\sin\theta}{z^2 - 2rz\cos\theta + r^2}$ | $|z| > r$ |

The damped sinusoid has a pair of poles at $re^{\pm j\theta}$: the radius sets the decay and the angle sets the frequency. Draw that picture once and you will never need to memorise the formula.

### Properties

- **Linearity.** Transforms add; ROCs intersect (at least).
- **Delay.** $x[n - k] \leftrightarrow z^{-k}X(z)$ for a two-sided transform. For the unilateral transform the samples pushed in from negative time must be added: $x[n-1] \leftrightarrow z^{-1}X(z) + x[-1]$.
- **Advance (unilateral).** $x[n + 1] \leftrightarrow zX(z) - zx[0]$.
- **Scaling in $z$.** $a^n x[n] \leftrightarrow X(z/a)$, with poles moved outward by $|a|$.
- **Differentiation.** $n\,x[n] \leftrightarrow -z\,dX/dz$.
- **Reversal.** $x[-n] \leftrightarrow X(1/z)$, with the ROC inverted.
- **Convolution.** $x * h \leftrightarrow X(z)H(z)$.
- **Accumulation.** $\sum_{k\le n}x[k] \leftrightarrow \dfrac{z}{z - 1}X(z)$.

### Poles and Zeros

When working with $X(z)$ written in powers of $z^{-1}$, multiply top and bottom by the highest power of $z$ before reading off poles and zeros. The expression $1/(1 - \tfrac12 z^{-1})$ has a pole at $z = \tfrac12$ and a zero at $z = 0$, which is easy to miss when it is written in negative powers. A pole–zero plot with the unit circle drawn in tells you at a glance whether the causal system is stable and roughly what its frequency response looks like: gain rises near poles that are close to the circle and falls near zeros.

### Initial and Final Values

For a causal sequence,
$$x[0] = \lim_{z\to\infty}X(z), \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}x[n] = \lim_{z\to1}(z - 1)X(z),$$
the second valid only when all poles of $(z - 1)X(z)$ lie inside the unit circle. The same caution applies as in continuous time: check the poles first.

::: pitfall
Do not confuse a pole at $z = 0$ with instability or with something unusual. Poles at the origin simply correspond to pure delays. They sit comfortably inside the unit circle and are perfectly harmless.
:::

::: strategy
**Z-transform problems.**

1. For finite sequences, write the polynomial directly; the ROC is the plane minus $0$ and/or $\infty$.
2. For infinite sequences, reduce to geometric series or table entries with properties.
3. Always state the ROC. For a causal signal, outside the outermost pole.
4. Stability: the ROC must include the unit circle.
5. Check $x[0]$ with the initial value theorem.
:::

::: insight
A student once told me that the Z-transform finally made sense when she realised that $z^{-1}$ was just a box labelled 'remember the last sample'. That is exactly right, and it is how real digital hardware is built: delays, multipliers and adders. The algebra in this chapter is simply a compact way of describing such networks.
:::

::: remember
- $a^n u[n] \leftrightarrow z/(z - a)$, $|z| > |a|$; the left-sided version has the same formula, different ROC.
- $z^{-1}$ is a one-sample delay.
- Causal and stable $\Leftrightarrow$ all poles inside the unit circle.
- Poles at $re^{\pm j\theta}$ give $r^n\cos(\theta n + \phi)$.
- $x[0] = \lim_{z\to\infty}X(z)$.
:::

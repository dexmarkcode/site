## Convolution: Continuous and Discrete

::: objectives
- Compute discrete convolutions quickly and reliably by the multiply-and-collect method.
- Evaluate continuous convolution integrals by flip and slide, with correct limits in every region.
- Use the algebraic properties of convolution to avoid unnecessary integration.
- Predict where a convolution starts and ends, and check results with the area and sum rules.
- Undo a convolution by recursion.
:::

::: dropcap
Convolution has a reputation. Students meet it, find the integral with its moving limits awkward, and quietly hope that the transforms will let them avoid it for ever. In a sense they will: the Fourier, Laplace and Z-transforms all turn convolution into multiplication, and that is one of their great selling points. But you cannot understand why those transforms work, or check their answers, or handle the many situations in which a direct calculation is quicker, unless you can convolve by hand with confidence. This chapter gives you the method, and the problems at the end give you the practice.
:::

We met convolution in Chapter 2 as the formula that gives the output of an LTI system:
$$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k]\,h[n - k], \qquad y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,h(t - \tau)\,d\tau.$$
We write both as $y = x * h$. The asterisk is not multiplication; it is a whole operation, involving every value of both signals.

### Discrete Convolution by Hand

For finite sequences, the cleanest method is to treat convolution as polynomial multiplication. If $x[n] = \{1, 2, 1\}$ and $h[n] = \{1, -1\}$, both starting at $n = 0$, think of the polynomials $1 + 2z + z^2$ and $1 - z$. Their product is $1 + z - z^2 - z^3$, so $y[n] = \{1, 1, -1, -1\}$. This is no accident. In Part IV we shall see that the Z-transform makes the correspondence exact.

![A short discrete convolution. Every sample of the output is a sum of products whose indices add to the same $n$.](figures/ch03_dtconv){width=96%}

Two pieces of bookkeeping make the method foolproof.

*Where does it start?* The first index of $y$ is the sum of the first indices of $x$ and $h$. The last index is the sum of the last indices.

*How long is it?* If $x$ has $N_1$ samples and $h$ has $N_2$, then $y$ has $N_1 + N_2 - 1$. Remember the minus one; it matters a great deal in Chapter 6 when we use the DFT to compute convolutions.

For a check, use the *sum rule*: the sum of the samples of $y$ equals the product of the sums of $x$ and $h$. In the example above, $(1 + 2 + 1)(1 - 1) = 0$ and $1 + 1 - 1 - 1 = 0$. The check takes seconds and catches most arithmetic slips.

When I set long sequences in tutorials, some students prefer the *sliding strip* method: write $x$ on one strip of paper and $h$ reversed on another, slide one past the other, and at each position multiply the overlapping values and add. It is the same calculation arranged differently. Use whichever you find less error-prone, but use the sum rule regardless.

### Infinite Sequences

When the sequences are infinitely long, we need a closed form. The prime example is the convolution of two geometric sequences:
$$a^n u[n] * b^n u[n] = \sum_{k} a^k u[k]\,b^{n-k}u[n - k].$$
The two steps do all the work of setting the limits. The first, $u[k]$, needs $k \ge 0$. The second, $u[n - k]$, needs $k \le n$. So for $n \ge 0$ the sum runs from $0$ to $n$, and for $n < 0$ it is empty. Then
$$y[n] = b^n\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^k = \frac{a^{n+1} - b^{n+1}}{a - b}\,u[n], \qquad a \ne b.$$
If $a = b$, every term in the sum is $a^n$ and there are $n + 1$ of them, so $y[n] = (n+1)a^n u[n]$. The appearance of the factor $(n+1)$ when two identical poles meet is a pattern you will see again with repeated roots in the Laplace and Z domains.

::: keyidea
Let the unit steps set the limits. Write down the condition each step imposes on the summation (or integration) variable, combine them into a single range, and only then start the algebra.
:::

### Continuous Convolution: Flip and Slide

The continuous integral is the same idea with an integral instead of a sum. The technique that makes it manageable is to think of $h(\tau)$ as fixed on the $\tau$ axis while a reversed copy of the input, $x(t - \tau)$, slides along from left to right as $t$ increases. The output at time $t$ is the area under the product of the two at that position.

![Flip and slide. The reversed pulse $x(t - \tau)$ moves to the right as $t$ increases; the shaded overlap area is $y(t)$.](figures/ch03_flipslide){width=96%}

The approach becomes routine if you follow a fixed procedure.

::: strategy
**Flip and slide in five steps.**

1. Choose which signal to reverse. Reverse the simpler one; convolution is commutative, so either choice is valid.
2. Sketch both on the $\tau$ axis, with the reversed one positioned for a general $t$. Mark its edges in terms of $t$.
3. Identify the *regions* of $t$ where the pattern of overlap changes: no overlap, partial overlap entering, full overlap, partial overlap leaving, and so on. The boundaries occur where an edge of one signal meets an edge of the other.
4. In each region, write the integral with limits equal to the edges of the overlap, and evaluate it.
5. Check continuity at the boundaries (the output of convolving two ordinary functions is usually continuous) and check the total area.
:::

For the pulse and exponential in the figure, the overlap regions are $t < 0$ (no overlap), $0 \le t < T$ (the pulse is entering) and $t \ge T$ (the pulse lies entirely over the decaying exponential). The result rises towards a plateau while the pulse is on and decays once it has passed, which is exactly how the voltage across a capacitor behaves when a resistor–capacitor circuit is driven by a rectangular pulse.

#### Two results worth knowing

The convolution of two one-sided exponentials comes up so often that it is worth deriving once and remembering:
$$e^{-at}u(t) * e^{-bt}u(t) = \frac{e^{-at} - e^{-bt}}{b - a}\,u(t), \qquad a \ne b,$$
and $t\,e^{-at}u(t)$ when $a = b$.

The convolution of two rectangular pulses of widths $T_1 \le T_2$ is a trapezoid: it rises linearly for a time $T_1$, stays flat until $T_2$, and falls linearly to zero at $T_1 + T_2$. When the widths are equal the flat top disappears and the result is a triangle. This is the reason the triangular pulse appears in Fourier tables next to the rectangle: its transform is the square of the rectangle's.

### The Algebra of Convolution

Convolution obeys a handful of rules that can save a lot of integration.

- **Commutative:** $x * h = h * x$.
- **Associative:** $(x * h_1) * h_2 = x * (h_1 * h_2)$, which is why cascaded systems can be combined into one.
- **Distributive:** $x * (h_1 + h_2) = x * h_1 + x * h_2$, which is why parallel systems add.
- **Identity:** $x * \delta = x$, and more generally $x(t) * \delta(t - t_0) = x(t - t_0)$.
- **Shift:** if $x * h = y$ then $x(t - t_1) * h(t - t_2) = y(t - t_1 - t_2)$.
- **Differentiation and integration:** $\dfrac{dx}{dt} * h = \dfrac{dy}{dt}$, and $x * u = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x\,d\tau$.
- **Area:** the area of $y$ is the product of the areas of $x$ and $h$.
- **Support:** $y$ starts at the sum of the starts and ends at the sum of the ends.

The shift and impulse rules are the ones that matter most in problems. If an input can be written as a combination of shifted copies of a signal whose response you already know, you need not integrate at all.

::: pitfall
When convolving with a shifted impulse, shift *everything*. The result of $e^{-2t}u(t) * \delta(t - 3)$ is $e^{-2(t - 3)}u(t - 3)$. Writing $e^{-2t}u(t - 3)$, which shifts the step but not the exponential, is one of the commonest errors I see.
:::

### Deconvolution

Sometimes we know the output and the system and want the input. For finite sequences this is straightforward, because convolution with a causal $h$ can be rearranged into a recursion. If $y[n] = h[0]x[n] + h[1]x[n-1] + \dots$, then
$$x[n] = \frac{1}{h[0]}\Big(y[n] - h[1]x[n-1] - h[2]x[n-2] - \cdots\Big),$$
and we can compute $x[0], x[1], \dots$ one at a time, provided $h[0] \ne 0$. This is long division of polynomials in disguise. In real measurements the process is delicate, because noise in $y$ is amplified when $h$ is small at some frequencies, but in the clean world of the problems below it works exactly.

### Choosing Your Method

With practice you will develop a feel for which approach suits which problem. As a rough guide:

- **Short finite sequences:** multiply and collect, then check with the sum rule.
- **Geometric or exponential signals:** write the sum or integral, let the steps fix the limits, and use the geometric series or a direct integral.
- **Pulses and piecewise signals:** flip and slide, with a sketch for each region.
- **Signals built from steps or impulses:** use linearity and the shift rule with a known response.
- **Long or messy convolutions:** transform, multiply, and invert. That is what Parts II to IV are for.

::: insight
I have watched many students lose marks on convolution problems by being too clever with the algebra and not clever enough with the sketch. A rough drawing of the two signals on the $\tau$ axis, with the edges labelled in terms of $t$, takes thirty seconds and tells you the regions and limits. Nobody has ever regretted drawing it.
:::

::: remember
- Start index of $x * h$ = sum of start indices; length $= N_1 + N_2 - 1$.
- Sum (or area) of $x * h$ = product of the sums (or areas).
- $a^n u[n] * b^n u[n] = \dfrac{a^{n+1} - b^{n+1}}{a - b}u[n]$.
- $e^{-at}u(t) * e^{-bt}u(t) = \dfrac{e^{-at} - e^{-bt}}{b - a}u(t)$.
- Convolving with $\delta(t - t_0)$ shifts the whole signal by $t_0$.
:::

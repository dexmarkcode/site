## The Laplace Transform and Its Properties

::: objectives
- Compute Laplace transforms from the definition and from a table of standard pairs.
- Determine the region of convergence and understand why it matters.
- Use the shifting, differentiation, integration and scaling properties fluently.
- Transform piecewise and periodic signals built from steps and ramps.
- Apply the initial and final value theorems, and recognise when the final value theorem fails.
:::

::: dropcap
Oliver Heaviside, the self-taught English engineer who gave us much of modern circuit theory, solved differential equations by treating the derivative as an algebraic symbol and manipulating it freely. The mathematicians of his day were horrified, and he was not much bothered by their horror: his methods gave the right answers. The Laplace transform is the respectable version of Heaviside's operational calculus. It turns derivatives into multiplication by $s$, integrals into division by $s$, and differential equations into algebra. That is a remarkable trade, and this chapter teaches you how to make it.
:::

### From Fourier to Laplace

The Fourier transform of $e^{2t}u(t)$ does not exist, because the signal grows and the integral diverges. But if we first multiply by a decaying exponential $e^{-\sigma t}$ with $\sigma > 2$, the product is well behaved and its Fourier transform exists. Combining the two exponentials, $e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t} = e^{-st}$ with $s = \sigma + j\omega$, gives the *Laplace transform*:
$$X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt.$$
For causal signals, which start at $t = 0$, the lower limit becomes $0^-$ and we have the *unilateral* transform used in most of engineering. The bilateral form is needed only when signals extend into negative time.

The Fourier transform is the special case $\sigma = 0$: if the imaginary axis lies inside the region of convergence, $X(j\omega)$ is obtained simply by setting $s = j\omega$.

### The Region of Convergence

The set of values of $s$ for which the integral converges is the *region of convergence* (ROC). It is always a vertical strip in the complex plane, possibly extending to infinity on one side, and it never contains a pole.

- A **right-sided** signal has an ROC to the right of its right-most pole.
- A **left-sided** signal has an ROC to the left of its left-most pole.
- A **two-sided** signal has a strip between two poles, or no ROC at all.

![Regions of convergence (shaded) for right-sided, left-sided and two-sided signals. Crosses mark poles.](figures/ch07_roc){width=98%}

Why care? Because the algebraic expression alone does not determine the signal. Both $e^{-2t}u(t)$ and $-e^{-2t}u(-t)$ have transform $1/(s + 2)$; only the ROC distinguishes them. In unilateral work with causal signals the ROC is always a right half-plane and is usually left unstated, but in bilateral problems and in stability arguments it is essential.

::: keyidea
A causal LTI system is stable if and only if all poles of $H(s)$ lie in the open left half-plane. Equivalently, its ROC includes the imaginary axis, so its frequency response exists.
:::

### The Standard Table

You should know these pairs as well as your multiplication tables. All are for $t \ge 0$, multiplied by $u(t)$.

| $x(t)$ | $X(s)$ | ROC |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $\delta(t)$ | $1$ | all $s$ |
| $u(t)$ | $1/s$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ |
| $t^n$ | $n!/s^{n+1}$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ |
| $e^{-at}$ | $1/(s + a)$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > -a$ |
| $t^n e^{-at}$ | $n!/(s + a)^{n+1}$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > -a$ |
| $\cos\omega_0 t$ | $s/(s^2 + \omega_0^2)$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ |
| $\sin\omega_0 t$ | $\omega_0/(s^2 + \omega_0^2)$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ |
| $e^{-at}\cos\omega_0 t$ | $(s + a)/((s + a)^2 + \omega_0^2)$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > -a$ |
| $e^{-at}\sin\omega_0 t$ | $\omega_0/((s + a)^2 + \omega_0^2)$ | $\operatorname{Re}(s) > -a$ |

Notice how few of these you really need. Everything below the third row follows from $e^{-at} \leftrightarrow 1/(s + a)$ together with the properties that follow.

### The Properties

- **Linearity.** $ax_1 + bx_2 \leftrightarrow aX_1 + bX_2$.
- **Time shift.** $x(t - T)u(t - T) \leftrightarrow e^{-sT}X(s)$ for $T > 0$.
- **Frequency shift.** $e^{-at}x(t) \leftrightarrow X(s + a)$.
- **Scaling.** $x(ct) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{c}X(s/c)$ for $c > 0$.
- **Differentiation.** $x'(t) \leftrightarrow sX(s) - x(0^-)$, and $x''(t) \leftrightarrow s^2X(s) - sx(0^-) - x'(0^-)$.
- **Integration.** $\int_{0^-}^t x(\tau)\,d\tau \leftrightarrow X(s)/s$.
- **Multiplication by $t$.** $t\,x(t) \leftrightarrow -dX/ds$.
- **Convolution.** $x * h \leftrightarrow X(s)H(s)$.

The differentiation property deserves emphasis. It brings the initial conditions into the transform automatically. That is why the unilateral Laplace transform is the natural tool for circuits that are switched on with energy already stored in their capacitors and inductors.

::: pitfall
The time-shift property requires the *whole* function to be shifted. The transform of $t\,u(t - 2)$ is not $e^{-2s}/s^2$. Rewrite it first as $(t - 2)u(t - 2) + 2u(t - 2)$, and only then apply the property, to get $e^{-2s}(1/s^2 + 2/s)$.
:::

### Piecewise and Periodic Signals

Any signal made of straight-line segments can be written as a sum of shifted steps and ramps, exactly as in Chapter 1. Each piece then transforms to $e^{-sT}/s$ or $e^{-sT}/s^2$, and the transform of the whole is a sum of such terms. The method is mechanical, which is exactly what you want in an examination.

For a periodic signal with period $T$ that starts at $t = 0$, the transform is the transform of one period divided by $1 - e^{-sT}$:
$$X(s) = \frac{X_1(s)}{1 - e^{-sT}}.$$
The reason is that the signal is one period plus a delayed copy of itself, so $X = X_1 + e^{-sT}X$.

### What Poles Tell You

Each pole of $X(s)$ corresponds to a mode in $x(t)$. A real pole at $s = -a$ gives $e^{-at}$; a complex pair at $-\alpha \pm j\beta$ gives $e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\beta t + \phi)$. Poles to the left of the imaginary axis decay, poles on it persist, and poles to the right grow. The further left, the faster the decay; the further from the real axis, the faster the oscillation.

This single picture is perhaps the most useful in the whole subject. Learn to read it in both directions.

### Initial and Final Value Theorems

Two limits let us find the start and end of a signal without inverting its transform:
$$x(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} sX(s), \qquad \lim_{t\to\infty}x(t) = \lim_{s\to 0} sX(s).$$
The initial value theorem holds whenever $X(s)$ is a proper rational function. The final value theorem is more delicate. It holds only if $sX(s)$ has all its poles in the open left half-plane. Apply it blindly to $1/(s(s - 1))$ and it confidently returns $-1$ for a signal that actually grows without bound. Always check the poles before using it.

::: strategy
**Laplace transform problems.**

1. Decompose the signal into table entries, writing shifted pieces in fully shifted form.
2. Apply one property at a time; write each step down.
3. State the ROC: right of the right-most pole for causal signals.
4. Check: initial value from $\lim sX(s)$ as $s \to \infty$, final value from $s \to 0$ (only if the poles allow).
:::

::: insight
I tell students that the Laplace transform is a machine for bookkeeping. It never solves anything that could not, in principle, be solved by hand in the time domain. What it does is keep track of initial conditions, delays and convolutions so reliably that you can concentrate on the engineering rather than the algebra.
:::

::: remember
- $e^{-at}u(t) \leftrightarrow 1/(s + a)$; nearly everything else follows from properties.
- Delay by $T$ multiplies by $e^{-sT}$; multiplying by $e^{-at}$ replaces $s$ by $s + a$.
- $x' \leftrightarrow sX - x(0^-)$: initial conditions enter automatically.
- Poles in the left half-plane decay; on the axis persist; in the right half-plane grow.
- Check the poles before using the final value theorem.
:::

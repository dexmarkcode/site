Flick a light switch and the room seems to brighten instantly. Look closer, with an oscilloscope, and almost nothing electrical happens instantly. Every real circuit contains some capacitance and some inductance, and those storage elements make voltages and currents move smoothly from one state to another instead of jumping. The interval during which they move is the **transient**. In many circuits it lasts microseconds and nobody cares. In others — the delay before a relay releases, the time a camera flash takes to recharge, the surge when a motor is switched on, the edge rate of a digital signal — the transient is the whole point.

This chapter deals with circuits containing a single capacitor or a single inductor, together with resistors and DC sources. Their transients follow one universal shape, the exponential, and a single formula handles all of them. Master that formula and an entire class of examination questions becomes routine.

:::objectives
- Derive and interpret the natural response of RC and RL circuits.
- Explain the meaning of the time constant and use it to estimate settling times.
- Apply the general first-order formula using initial values, final values and the time constant.
- Find the time constant of more complicated circuits using the Thévenin resistance.
- Analyse switching problems in which the circuit changes at *t* = 0.
- Calculate energy transfers during charging and discharging.
:::

## The discharging capacitor

Start with the simplest case: a capacitor charged to *V*<sub>0</sub> is connected across a resistor *R* at *t* = 0. KCL at the top node says the capacitor current and the resistor current must sum to zero:

$$ <i>C</i> d<i>v</i>/d<i>t</i> + <i>v</i>/<i>R</i> = 0  || 8.1

This is a first-order linear differential equation, and its solution, with *v*(0) = *V*<sub>0</sub>, is

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>V</i><sub>0</sub> e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>,  τ = <i>RC</i>  || 8.2

The quantity τ (tau) is the **time constant**, measured in seconds. (Ohms multiplied by farads really is seconds; it is a useful check on units.) The voltage starts at *V*<sub>0</sub> and decays exponentially towards zero, falling by a factor of e — to about 37 % of its value — every time constant.

## The time constant

The exponential has some properties worth knowing by heart:

| Time | Fraction of change completed | Fraction remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 1τ | 63.2 % | 36.8 % |
| 2τ | 86.5 % | 13.5 % |
| 3τ | 95.0 % | 5.0 % |
| 4τ | 98.2 % | 1.8 % |
| 5τ | 99.3 % | 0.7 % |

Engineers usually treat a transient as finished after five time constants. Mathematically the exponential never quite reaches its final value, but after 5τ it is within 1 % and nobody can tell the difference.

There is a second, geometric interpretation. If the curve continued at its *initial* rate of change, it would reach its final value in exactly one time constant. The dashed line in Figure 8.1 shows this. It is a quick way to sketch an exponential accurately: draw the tangent at the start, mark where it meets the final value, and that point is one τ along the time axis.

## The charging capacitor

Now connect an uncharged capacitor to a DC source *V*<sub>s</sub> through a resistor *R*. The capacitor charges, and its voltage approaches *V*<sub>s</sub>:

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>V</i><sub>s</sub>(1 − e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>),  τ = <i>RC</i>  || 8.3

The current, *i* = *C* d*v*/d*t*, is

$$ <i>i</i>(<i>t</i>) = (<i>V</i><sub>s</sub>/<i>R</i>) e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>  || 8.4

At the instant the switch closes the capacitor voltage is still zero (continuity), so the full supply voltage appears across *R* and the current jumps to *V*<sub>s</sub>/*R*. As the capacitor charges, the voltage across *R* shrinks and the current dies away.

{{fig:t08_rc|An RC charging circuit. The switch closes at t = 0 with the capacitor uncharged.}}

:::example Charging a timing capacitor
In Figure 8.2, find the capacitor voltage two seconds after the switch closes, and the time at which it reaches 5 V.

τ = *RC* = 10 × 10<sup>3</sup> × 100 × 10<sup>−6</sup> = 1 s.

At *t* = 2 s: *v* = 10(1 − e<sup>−2</sup>) = 10 × 0.865 = 8.65 V.

For 5 V: 1 − e<sup>−<i>t</i></sup> = 0.5, so *t* = ln 2 = 0.693 s. The half-way point of any exponential is at 0.693τ, a figure worth remembering.
:::

## The RL circuit

The inductor is the dual of the capacitor, so the RL circuit behaves in exactly the same way with current playing the role of voltage. When a DC source *V*<sub>s</sub> is switched across a series *R* and *L*, the current cannot jump, so it starts at zero and rises towards its final value *V*<sub>s</sub>/*R*:

$$ <i>i</i>(<i>t</i>) = (<i>V</i><sub>s</sub>/<i>R</i>)(1 − e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>),  τ = <i>L</i>/<i>R</i>  || 8.5

Notice that the time constant is *L*/*R*, not *LR*. Making the resistance larger makes an RL circuit *faster*, the opposite of the RC case. This regularly catches people out.

{{fig:t08_rl|An RL circuit energised at t = 0.}}

For Figure 8.3, τ = 0.3/6 = 50 ms and the final current is 12/6 = 2 A. After 100 ms (two time constants) the current is 2(1 − e<sup>−2</sup>) = 1.73 A. The inductor voltage, *L* d*i*/d*t*, starts at the full 12 V and decays as 12e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>.

## The general first-order formula

Every example so far has had the same shape: a quantity starts at some initial value and moves exponentially towards some final value. That is true for *any* voltage or current in *any* circuit containing resistors, DC sources and a single storage element. So rather than solving a differential equation each time, we can use one formula:

:::key
$$ <i>x</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>x</i>(∞) + [<i>x</i>(0<sup>+</sup>) − <i>x</i>(∞)] e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>  || 8.6

To use it, find three things:

1. **The initial value** *x*(0<sup>+</sup>), just after switching, using continuity of capacitor voltage or inductor current.
2. **The final value** *x*(∞), from the DC steady state after switching (*C* open, *L* short).
3. **The time constant** τ = *R*<sub>Th</sub>*C* or *L*/*R*<sub>Th</sub>, where *R*<sub>Th</sub> is the Thévenin resistance seen by the storage element *after* switching.
:::

Equation 8.6 is the most useful single result in transient analysis. Write it down at the start of every first-order problem.

### Finding the time constant in a larger circuit

The resistance in τ is the resistance the capacitor or inductor *sees*. In a single loop that is obvious. In a larger circuit, remove the storage element, kill the independent sources, and find the Thévenin resistance between its terminals. A very common error is to use only the resistor that happens to sit next to the capacitor on the diagram.

:::example A capacitor in a divider
A 24 V source feeds a 6 kΩ resistor in series with a 3 kΩ resistor. A 10 μF capacitor, initially uncharged, is connected across the 3 kΩ resistor, and the source is switched on at *t* = 0. Find *v*<sub>C</sub>(*t*) and its value at *t* = 20 ms.

*Initial value.* The capacitor is uncharged, so *v*(0<sup>+</sup>) = 0.

*Final value.* With the capacitor open, the divider gives 24 × 3/9 = 8 V.

*Time constant.* With the source shorted, the capacitor sees 6 kΩ ∥ 3 kΩ = 2 kΩ, so τ = 2000 × 10 × 10<sup>−6</sup> = 20 ms.

*Result.* *v*<sub>C</sub>(*t*) = 8(1 − e<sup>−<i>t</i>/0.02</sup>) V. At 20 ms, that is 8 × 0.632 = 5.06 V.

If you had used 6 kΩ alone for the time constant, you would have got 60 ms, three times too slow.
:::

## Switching problems

The most common style of examination question describes a circuit that has been in one configuration "for a long time", then a switch opens or closes at *t* = 0. The procedure is always the same:

1. **Before switching** (*t* < 0): the circuit is in DC steady state. Find the capacitor voltage or inductor current.
2. **At switching** (*t* = 0<sup>+</sup>): continuity gives the initial value of that capacitor voltage or inductor current. If the question asks for some *other* quantity at 0<sup>+</sup>, redraw the circuit with the capacitor replaced by a voltage source of value *v*<sub>C</sub>(0<sup>+</sup>), or the inductor by a current source of value *i*<sub>L</sub>(0<sup>+</sup>), and solve that resistive circuit.
3. **After switching** (*t* → ∞): find the new DC steady state for the final value.
4. **Time constant:** find *R*<sub>Th</sub> seen by the storage element in the post-switching circuit.
5. Assemble the answer with Equation 8.6.

:::pitfall
Only the capacitor voltage and the inductor current are continuous. Other quantities, such as resistor currents, capacitor currents and inductor voltages, can and often do jump at the switching instant. If you need one of them at 0<sup>+</sup>, you must find it from the redrawn circuit in step 2, not from the steady state before switching.
:::

## Inductive kick

When the current in an inductor is interrupted, the inductor forces that current to keep flowing, through whatever path it can find. If the only path is a resistor *R*, the voltage across the inductor at the instant of switching is *I*<sub>0</sub>*R*, which may be very much larger than the supply voltage. With a 1 A coil current and a 10 kΩ discharge resistor, the instantaneous voltage is 10 kV.

This is why relay and solenoid coils driven by transistors are always fitted with a flyback diode, which gives the inductor current a low-resistance path and limits the spike to about 0.7 V above the supply. The price is a slower decay, since the time constant *L*/*R* becomes long when *R* is small. In applications that need fast release, a resistor or Zener diode in series with the flyback diode trades a higher spike for a quicker decay.

## Energy in RC charging

When a capacitor is charged from a constant-voltage source through a resistor, the source delivers charge *CV*<sub>s</sub> at voltage *V*<sub>s</sub>, so it supplies energy *CV*<sub>s</sub><sup>2</sup>. The capacitor stores only ½*CV*<sub>s</sub><sup>2</sup>. The other half is dissipated in the resistor, whatever its value. It is the same result as the charge-sharing puzzle of Chapter 7, and it is the reason that switched-capacitor and adiabatic logic circuits work hard to avoid charging capacitors abruptly from fixed supplies.

:::tip
A strong check on any first-order answer: substitute *t* = 0 and *t* → ∞ into your final expression and confirm that you get the initial and final values you found. It takes ten seconds and catches most sign errors and swapped terms.
:::

## Beyond DC sources

Everything in this chapter assumes the sources are DC, or at least constant after switching. With a time-varying source, such as a ramp, a sinusoid or a pulse train, the final "value" becomes a final *waveform*, and Equation 8.6 needs to be generalised. The principle is unchanged, though: the response is a forced part following the source, plus a natural part that decays with the same time constant. When the source is sinusoidal and we wait for the natural part to die away, we are left with the AC steady state that is the subject of Part IV.

:::summary
- A first-order circuit has a single storage element; every voltage and current in it follows an exponential with time constant τ.
- τ = *R*<sub>Th</sub>*C* for RC circuits and τ = *L*/*R*<sub>Th</sub> for RL circuits, using the Thévenin resistance seen by the storage element.
- After 1τ the change is 63 % complete; after 5τ it is effectively complete. The half-way point is at 0.693τ.
- General formula: *x*(*t*) = *x*(∞) + [*x*(0<sup>+</sup>) − *x*(∞)]e<sup>−<i>t</i>/τ</sup>.
- In switching problems: steady state before, continuity at the instant, steady state after, then the time constant.
- Interrupting inductor current produces a voltage spike of *I*<sub>0</sub>*R*; flyback diodes limit it.
- Charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through a resistor always wastes half the energy supplied.
:::

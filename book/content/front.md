## Preface

Circuit analysis is learned with a pencil. You can read every explanation in every textbook, nod along to every lecture, and still freeze when you are handed a circuit you have never seen before and asked for the current in one particular resistor. The only cure I have ever found is practice: lots of it, at steadily increasing difficulty, with honest feedback on each attempt.

That is what this book is for. It grew out of the problem sheets I have written over many years of teaching electrical and electronic engineering, first to apprentices and technicians, later to undergraduates and to engineers returning to study for professional qualifications. Every year the same pattern repeated itself. Students who worked through a large number of problems, checking each one carefully against a full solution, did well. Students who read the notes and attempted a handful of examples did not, however capable they were. The difference was not talent. It was repetition.

Most circuits textbooks contain good problems, but very few contain complete solutions to them, and fewer still contain enough problems on each topic to build genuine fluency. Answer-only appendices tell you that you went wrong without telling you where. This workbook takes a different approach. Each of its 800 problems has a full step-by-step solution, written out the way I would write it on the board, with the reasoning stated rather than implied. When you get something wrong, you should be able to find the exact line where your working parted company with mine.

The book is organised in four parts of three chapters each, following the order in which the subject is usually taught. Every chapter opens with a teaching section that explains the ideas from first principles, illustrates them with diagrams and worked examples, and points out the traps that catch people in examinations. The teaching sections are deliberately complete: you can learn the subject from this book alone if you need to. But their real purpose is to give you exactly what you need, and no more, before you start working problems.

The problems themselves are graded into three levels. Level 1 questions test a single idea and should take a few minutes each. Level 2 questions combine two or three ideas and are typical of coursework and class tests. Level 3 questions are at the standard of end-of-year university examinations and professional engineering papers: longer, less signposted, and occasionally designed to punish a careless assumption.

I have used British spelling and conventions throughout, including UK mains voltages and electricity prices, but the engineering is universal. Whether you are studying for a degree, an HNC or HND, an apprenticeship, or the Fundamentals of Engineering examination, the circuits behave in exactly the same way.

A great many students, colleagues and reviewers have helped to shape this book, often without knowing it, by asking the questions that showed me where an explanation was unclear. Any errors that remain are mine. If you find one, I would genuinely like to hear about it.

*Marcus J. Thornton*

## How to Use This Workbook

### If you are studying a course

Work through the chapters in step with your lectures. Read the teaching section first, even if you think you know the material; it is short, and it flags the specific mistakes examiners look for. Then attempt the Level 1 problems until they feel routine, move on to Level 2, and save Level 3 for revision or for when you want to stretch yourself.

### If you are revising for an examination

Go straight to the Level 3 problems in the chapters your examination covers. Give yourself a time limit — roughly one minute per mark is a good rule if you know the weighting — and treat each attempt as if it were real. When you get one wrong, drop down to the Level 2 problems on the same topic until the weakness is fixed, then return.

### If you are teaching yourself

Take the book in order and do not hurry. Parts I and II are the foundation for everything else, and time spent there is never wasted. Aim to work every problem in the first three chapters, then use your judgement.

### How to attempt a problem

Resist the urge to look at the solution early. Struggling with a problem for ten minutes and then reading the answer teaches you far more than reading the answer after one minute. A good routine looks like this:

1. Redraw the circuit yourself, labelling every node and every unknown current with its assumed direction.
2. Write down which method you intend to use and why.
3. Write the equations before you put any numbers into them.
4. Solve, keeping units throughout.
5. Check the result: a power balance, a second method, or simply asking whether the size and sign make physical sense.
6. Only now compare your answer with the solution, line by line.

### Conventions used in this book

Currents are conventional currents, flowing from positive to negative through external circuits. Unless a problem states otherwise, all components are ideal: wires have no resistance, voltage sources have no internal resistance, and resistors are linear. Sinusoidal phasors in Part IV are RMS values unless a problem specifies peak values. Numerical answers are usually given to three or four significant figures, and the minus sign is written as −.

Each problem is labelled with its chapter and number (for example 6.23) and with one, two or three dots showing its level. Solutions appear at the end of each chapter, in the same order as the problems.

### A note on calculators

A scientific calculator that handles complex numbers will save you a great deal of time in Part IV, and one that solves simultaneous equations will help in Part II. Learn to use these features well, but always write down the equations you are solving. Examiners award most of the marks for the method, and a calculator cannot show its working.

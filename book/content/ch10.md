The electricity in your wall socket is not DC. It is a sinusoid that reverses direction a hundred times a second, rising to a peak of about 325 V and swinging down to −325 V. The same waveform, at higher frequencies, carries radio and television broadcasts, and audio signals can be broken down into sums of sinusoids. Sinusoids matter for a deeper reason too: they are the only waveform that passes through a linear circuit unchanged in shape. Put a sinusoid into any combination of resistors, capacitors and inductors and, once the transients have died away, every voltage and current in the circuit is a sinusoid of the same frequency. Only the amplitudes and phases change.

That observation leads to one of the most elegant ideas in engineering. If the frequency is the same everywhere, we only need to keep track of amplitude and phase, and a complex number can hold both. Differential equations then become algebra, and every method from Parts I and II — Ohm's law, KCL, KVL, nodal and mesh analysis, Thévenin's theorem — works unchanged, with complex numbers in place of real ones.

:::objectives
- Describe a sinusoid by its amplitude, frequency, period, phase and RMS value.
- Convert between time-domain sinusoids and phasors, and between rectangular and polar forms.
- Find the impedance and admittance of resistors, inductors and capacitors at a given frequency.
- Analyse series, parallel and general AC circuits using phasors.
- Explain series and parallel resonance, and calculate resonant frequency, quality factor and bandwidth.
:::

## Describing a sinusoid

A sinusoidal voltage can be written

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>V</i><sub>m</sub> cos(ω<i>t</i> + φ)  || 10.1

where *V*<sub>m</sub> is the **amplitude** (peak value), ω is the **angular frequency** in rad/s, and φ is the **phase angle**. The frequency in hertz is *f* = ω/2π, and the period is *T* = 1/*f*. UK mains has *f* = 50 Hz, so ω = 314 rad/s and *T* = 20 ms.

Whether to use sine or cosine as the reference is a matter of convention; sin θ = cos(θ − 90°), so you can always convert. What matters is consistency within a problem.

{{fig:t10_sine|A 50 Hz sinusoid of amplitude 325 V (UK mains), and a second sinusoid lagging it by 60°. The dashed line marks the RMS value of 230 V.}}

When two sinusoids of the same frequency are compared, the one whose peak arrives first is said to **lead**, and the other to **lag**. In Figure 10.1 the second waveform reaches its peak 60° (3.33 ms) after the first, so it lags by 60°.

:::pitfall
Expressions like sin(314*t* + 30°) mix radians (314*t*) and degrees (30°). This is universal practice, but you must convert one of them before evaluating. Set your calculator to the right mode, and if in doubt convert everything to degrees: 314*t* rad × 57.3 = degrees.
:::

### RMS values

The **root-mean-square** (RMS) value of a waveform is the DC value that would deliver the same average power to a resistor. For a sinusoid,

$$ <i>V</i><sub>rms</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>/√2 ≈ 0.707<i>V</i><sub>m</sub>  || 10.2

When someone says "230 V mains", they mean 230 V RMS; the peak is 230√2 = 325 V. Meters, ratings and power calculations almost always use RMS values, and in this book phasors are RMS unless a problem says otherwise.

## Phasors

A **phasor** is a complex number that represents the amplitude and phase of a sinusoid. The sinusoid *V*<sub>m</sub> cos(ω*t* + φ) is represented by the phasor

$$ <b>V</b> = <i>V</i>∠φ  || 10.3

where *V* is either the peak or (more usually) the RMS value. The frequency is not part of the phasor; it is understood to be the same throughout the circuit.

Complex numbers can be written in two forms. **Rectangular** form, *a* + j*b*, is best for addition and subtraction. **Polar** form, *r*∠θ, is best for multiplication and division. Engineers use j rather than i for √−1, because i is already taken by current. The conversions are:

$$ <i>r</i> = √(<i>a</i><sup>2</sup> + <i>b</i><sup>2</sup>),  θ = tan<sup>−1</sup>(<i>b</i>/<i>a</i>);  <i>a</i> = <i>r</i> cos θ,  <i>b</i> = <i>r</i> sin θ  || 10.4

The inverse tangent on a calculator only returns angles between −90° and +90°. If the real part is negative, the point is in the second or third quadrant, and you must add or subtract 180°. Always sketch the point roughly on an Argand diagram; it takes seconds and prevents quadrant errors.

{{fig:t10_phasor|A phasor diagram. The current phasor I lags the voltage phasor V by the angle θ, as in an inductive circuit.}}

### Why phasors work

The key fact is that differentiating a sinusoid at frequency ω multiplies its phasor by jω: the amplitude is scaled by ω and the phase advances by 90°. Integration divides by jω. So the differential equations of Chapters 7 to 9 become algebraic equations in the phasor domain, which is an enormous simplification.

Adding sinusoids becomes easy too. To add 10 cos(ω*t*) and 10 cos(ω*t* + 90°), add the phasors 10∠0° + 10∠90° = 10 + j10 = 14.1∠45°. The sum is 14.1 cos(ω*t* + 45°), a result that would take some trigonometric effort to obtain directly.

## Impedance

The **impedance** **Z** of an element is the ratio of its voltage phasor to its current phasor, measured in ohms:

$$ <b>Z</b> = <b>V</b>/<b>I</b>  || 10.5

For the three basic elements:

| Element | Impedance | Phase of <b>V</b> relative to <b>I</b> |
|---|---|---|
| Resistor | <i>R</i> | in phase |
| Inductor | jω<i>L</i> | <b>V</b> leads <b>I</b> by 90° |
| Capacitor | 1/(jω<i>C</i>) = −j/(ω<i>C</i>) | <b>V</b> lags <b>I</b> by 90° |

The magnitudes ω*L* and 1/(ω*C*) are the **reactances** *X*<sub>L</sub> and *X*<sub>C</sub>. Inductive reactance rises with frequency; capacitive reactance falls. So at DC (ω = 0) an inductor is a short circuit and a capacitor an open circuit, exactly as in Chapter 7, and at very high frequencies the roles reverse.

A memory aid that generations of engineers have used: **CIVIL**. In a **C**apacitor, **I** leads **V**; **V** leads **I** in an inductor (**L**).

A general impedance is written **Z** = *R* + j*X*, with resistance *R* and reactance *X*. Positive *X* means inductive, negative *X* capacitive. The reciprocal of impedance is **admittance**, **Y** = 1/**Z** = *G* + j*B*, measured in siemens, with conductance *G* and susceptance *B*. Admittances add in parallel, just as conductances did.

## AC circuit analysis

With impedances in place of resistances, every DC technique carries over:

- Impedances in series add; admittances in parallel add.
- Voltage and current dividers work with impedances.
- KCL and KVL hold for phasors.
- Nodal analysis, mesh analysis, superposition, Thévenin and Norton all apply.

The only change is that the arithmetic is complex.

{{fig:t10_series|A series RLC circuit with impedances marked. The source is 10∠0° V RMS.}}

:::example A series AC circuit
Find the current in Figure 10.3 and the voltage across each element.

*Impedance.* **Z** = 30 + j60 − j20 = 30 + j40 Ω = 50∠53.13° Ω.

*Current.* **I** = 10∠0°/50∠53.13° = 0.2∠−53.13° A. The current lags the voltage by 53.13°, so the circuit is inductive overall.

*Element voltages.* **V**<sub>R</sub> = 30 × 0.2∠−53.13° = 6∠−53.13° V. **V**<sub>L</sub> = j60 × **I** = 12∠36.87° V. **V**<sub>C</sub> = −j20 × **I** = 4∠−143.13° V.

*Check.* The magnitudes 6 + 12 + 4 = 22 V add to much more than the 10 V supply, which surprises many people at first. But **V**<sub>L</sub> and **V**<sub>C</sub> are 180° apart and partly cancel, leaving a net reactive voltage of 8 V at right angles to the 6 V across the resistor: √(6<sup>2</sup> + 8<sup>2</sup>) = 10 V. KVL holds for phasors, not for magnitudes.
:::

:::key
In AC circuits, KVL and KCL apply to the **phasors** (complex numbers), never to the magnitudes alone. Element voltages can individually exceed the supply voltage, and branch currents can individually exceed the supply current.
:::

## Resonance

Look again at the series circuit. The inductive reactance rises with frequency and the capacitive reactance falls, so at one particular frequency they are equal and cancel completely. At that **resonant frequency** the impedance is purely resistive and at its minimum value, so the current is at its maximum:

$$ ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(<i>LC</i>),  <i>f</i><sub>0</sub> = 1/(2π√(<i>LC</i>))  || 10.6

It is the same ω<sub>0</sub> that appeared in Chapter 9, and that is no coincidence. The natural frequency at which a circuit rings when disturbed is the frequency at which it responds most strongly when driven.

### Quality factor and bandwidth

How sharply a series circuit selects its resonant frequency is measured by its **quality factor**:

$$ <i>Q</i> = ω<sub>0</sub><i>L</i>/<i>R</i> = 1/(ω<sub>0</sub><i>CR</i>)  || 10.7

and its **bandwidth**, the range between the two frequencies at which the current has fallen to 1/√2 of its peak value (the half-power points), is

$$ <i>B</i> = <i>f</i><sub>0</sub>/<i>Q</i>  || 10.8

{{fig:t10_resonance|Current against frequency for a series resonant circuit with three values of Q. Higher Q gives a sharper, narrower peak.}}

High *Q* means a narrow, sharp peak, which is what a radio tuner needs to pick one station from many. It also brings a hazard. At resonance the voltages across *L* and *C* are each *Q* times the supply voltage. A series circuit with *Q* = 50 driven from 10 V will have 500 V across its capacitor, a classic way to destroy a component that was rated only for the supply voltage.

### Parallel resonance

In a parallel RLC circuit, the inductor and capacitor *admittances* cancel at the same frequency ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(*LC*). The impedance is then at its *maximum*, equal to *R*, and the circuit draws its minimum current from the source. The quality factor of a parallel circuit is *Q* = ω<sub>0</sub>*RC* = *R*/(ω<sub>0</sub>*L*), so a *larger* parallel resistance gives a sharper resonance, mirroring the damping behaviour we met in Chapter 9. At resonance a large current circulates between *L* and *C* while only a small current is drawn from the supply. That circulating "tank" current is why a parallel LC circuit is often called a tank circuit.

:::tip
Resonance problems almost always ask for the same handful of quantities: *f*<sub>0</sub>, *Q*, bandwidth, the current or impedance at resonance, and the magnified voltage across *L* or *C*. Learn the formulas as a group and check whether you are dealing with series or parallel resonance before using any of them, since the *Q* formulas are inverted between the two.
:::

## Nodal and mesh analysis with phasors

For a circuit with more than one source, set up nodal or mesh equations exactly as in Part II, using admittances or impedances. A two-node problem gives two complex simultaneous equations. These can be solved by Cramer's rule with complex arithmetic, or by a calculator that handles complex matrices. If all the sources have the same frequency, the phasor method applies directly. If they have different frequencies, use superposition: analyse each frequency separately, convert the results back to the time domain, and only then add.

Patience pays off here. Complex arithmetic by hand is slow and error-prone, so set out every step clearly and keep track of which numbers are in rectangular form and which in polar.

:::example A parallel circuit at 50 Hz
A 46 Ω resistor and a 0.1 H inductor are connected in parallel across a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Find the supply current, and the capacitance that, added in parallel, would bring the circuit to resonance.

*Branch currents.* *X*<sub>L</sub> = 2π × 50 × 0.1 = 31.4 Ω. **I**<sub>R</sub> = 230/46 = 5∠0° A and **I**<sub>L</sub> = 230/j31.4 = 7.32∠−90° A.

*Supply current.* **I** = 5 − j7.32 = 8.86∠−55.7° A. The current lags the voltage, as expected for an inductive circuit.

*Resonance.* The capacitor must cancel the inductor's susceptance, so *X*<sub>C</sub> = *X*<sub>L</sub> = 31.4 Ω and *C* = 1/(2π × 50 × 31.4) = 101 μF.

With that capacitor fitted, the inductor and capacitor currents (7.32 A each) cancel, and the supply provides only the 5 A taken by the resistor, a reduction from 8.86 A. This is exactly the idea of power-factor correction, which Chapter 11 develops.
:::

## Check your understanding

**1. What is the RMS value of a sinusoid of peak 100 V?** 70.7 V.

**2. At what frequency does a 1 μF capacitor have a reactance of 1 kΩ?** *f* = 1/(2π × 10<sup>3</sup> × 10<sup>−6</sup>) = 159 Hz.

**3. In an inductor, does voltage lead or lag current?** Voltage leads current by 90° (CIVIL).

**4. A series resonant circuit has *Q* = 20 and is driven at resonance by 5 V. What is the voltage across the capacitor?** 100 V, *Q* times the supply.

**5. Which form of complex number is best for multiplying two phasors?** Polar: multiply the magnitudes and add the angles.

:::summary
- A sinusoid *V*<sub>m</sub> cos(ω*t* + φ) has period *T* = 2π/ω and RMS value *V*<sub>m</sub>/√2.
- A phasor **V** = *V*∠φ carries amplitude and phase; the frequency is common to the whole circuit.
- Use rectangular form for adding and polar form for multiplying and dividing. Watch the quadrant.
- Impedances: *R*, jω*L*, 1/(jω*C*). Series impedances add; parallel admittances add.
- All DC methods work with phasors. KCL and KVL apply to phasors, not magnitudes.
- Resonance at ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(*LC*). Series: minimum impedance, *Q* = ω<sub>0</sub>*L*/*R*, component voltages magnified by *Q*. Parallel: maximum impedance, *Q* = ω<sub>0</sub>*RC*. Bandwidth *B* = *f*<sub>0</sub>/*Q*.
:::

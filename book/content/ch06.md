Nodal and mesh analysis will solve any linear circuit. They are also, quite often, overkill. Suppose you are designing the output stage of a sensor interface and want to know what voltage appears across a load as you try ten different load resistances. A full nodal analysis ten times over would be tedious and would tell you nothing new. What you really want is a simple model of everything *except* the load, so that each new load needs only one line of arithmetic.

That is what network theorems give you. They are not new physics: each one follows from linearity and from Kirchhoff's laws. But they change the way you think about circuits, from "solve the whole thing" to "what does this part of the circuit look like from over here?" That shift in viewpoint is one of the most valuable things an engineer can learn.

:::objectives
- Use the superposition principle to analyse circuits with several independent sources.
- Convert between practical voltage and current sources.
- Find Thévenin and Norton equivalents, including for circuits with dependent sources.
- Apply the maximum power transfer theorem and understand its limits.
- Choose the most efficient theorem or method for a given problem.
:::

## Linearity

A circuit made of resistors, linear dependent sources and independent sources is **linear**. Every voltage and current in it is a linear function of the independent source values. Double every source and every response doubles; the response to two sources acting together is the sum of the responses to each acting alone. Everything in this chapter rests on that property.

Notice what is *not* linear: power. Power goes with the square of current or voltage, so doubling a source quadruples the power it delivers to a resistor. We shall come back to this.

## Superposition

:::key
**Superposition:** In a linear circuit with several independent sources, any voltage or current is the algebraic sum of the contributions from each independent source acting alone, with all the other independent sources set to zero.
:::

"Setting a source to zero" has a precise meaning. A voltage source set to zero volts is a **short circuit**. A current source set to zero amperes is an **open circuit**. Dependent sources are never set to zero; they stay in the circuit throughout.

Superposition is rarely the fastest way to analyse a circuit with numbers in it, because it requires one complete analysis per source. Its real strengths are elsewhere. It tells you *how much* of a response comes from each source, which is exactly what you need when, say, estimating how much ripple from a power supply reaches the output of an amplifier. And it is the theoretical basis for treating AC and DC parts of a signal separately, which every electronics engineer does without thinking.

:::example Superposition
Use superposition to find the voltage at node A in the circuit of Figure 3.1 (12 V source, 2 Ω, 4 Ω shunt, 4 Ω, 6 V source).

*12 V source alone.* Short the 6 V source. The 4 Ω shunt and the 4 Ω series resistor are now in parallel, giving 2 Ω, so *V*′ = 12 × 2/(2 + 2) = 6 V.

*6 V source alone.* Short the 12 V source. The 2 Ω and 4 Ω resistors are in parallel, giving 1.33 Ω, so *V*″ = 6 × 1.33/(4 + 1.33) = 1.5 V.

*Total.* *V*<sub>A</sub> = 6 + 1.5 = 7.5 V, which agrees with the branch-current solution in Chapter 3.
:::

:::pitfall
You cannot find the power in a resistor by adding the powers due to each source. In the example above, the power in the 4 Ω shunt resistor is 7.5<sup>2</sup>/4 = 14.1 W, whereas 6<sup>2</sup>/4 + 1.5<sup>2</sup>/4 = 9.56 W. Add the voltages or currents first, then calculate the power.
:::

## Source transformations

A practical voltage source — an ideal source *V*<sub>s</sub> in series with a resistance *R* — behaves at its terminals exactly like a practical current source *I*<sub>s</sub> = *V*<sub>s</sub>/*R* in parallel with the same *R*. You can check this by comparing their open-circuit voltages (*V*<sub>s</sub> in both cases) and short-circuit currents (*V*<sub>s</sub>/*R* in both cases). Two linear one-port networks with the same open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current are indistinguishable from outside.

Repeated source transformation is a neat way to simplify ladder-like circuits by hand. Convert a voltage source into a current source, combine it with parallel resistors and other current sources, convert back, combine with series resistors, and so on until only one source and one resistor remain. It is especially effective with several practical sources feeding a common load.

Two warnings. The transformation is only valid for the *outside* of the pair: the current in the resistor inside a transformed source is not the same as in the original. And an ideal voltage source with no series resistance, or an ideal current source with no parallel resistance, cannot be transformed.

## Thévenin's theorem

:::key
**Thévenin's theorem:** Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced, as far as its terminals are concerned, by an ideal voltage source *V*<sub>Th</sub> in series with a resistance *R*<sub>Th</sub>.
:::

The two quantities are found as follows.

- *V*<sub>Th</sub> is the **open-circuit voltage** at the terminals.
- *R*<sub>Th</sub> is the resistance seen looking into the terminals with **all independent sources set to zero**.

Alternatively, *R*<sub>Th</sub> = *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub>, where *I*<sub>sc</sub> is the current that would flow in a short circuit placed across the terminals. This second route is essential when the network contains dependent sources.

{{fig:t06_thev_orig|A network to be reduced to its Thévenin equivalent at terminals a–b.}}

For Figure 6.1, with a–b open, the 2 Ω resistor carries no current, so *V*<sub>Th</sub> is the voltage across the 3 Ω resistor: 18 × 3/(6 + 3) = 6 V. Shorting the 18 V source, the resistance seen from a–b is 2 + (6 ∥ 3) = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω. The whole network therefore behaves like the simple circuit of Figure 6.2.

{{fig:t06_thev_eq|The Thévenin equivalent of Figure 6.1: 6 V in series with 4 Ω.}}

Now any load is trivial to analyse. A 2 Ω load draws 6/(4 + 2) = 1 A; a 12 Ω load draws 6/16 = 0.375 A; and so on. That is the practical power of the theorem. Engineers use Thévenin models constantly, often without naming them: the "output resistance" of an amplifier and the "internal resistance" of a battery are both Thévenin resistances.

### Thévenin with dependent sources

If the network contains dependent sources, you cannot find *R*<sub>Th</sub> by combining resistors, because the dependent sources change the apparent resistance. Use one of two methods instead:

1. Find both *V*<sub>oc</sub> and *I*<sub>sc</sub>, and take *R*<sub>Th</sub> = *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub>.
2. Set the independent sources to zero (leaving the dependent sources in place), apply a test source — say 1 A — to the terminals, find the resulting voltage, and take *R*<sub>Th</sub> = *V*<sub>test</sub>/*I*<sub>test</sub>.

The second method is the only option when the network has no independent sources at all, in which case *V*<sub>Th</sub> = 0 and the network is equivalent to a single resistance, which may even be negative. Negative resistance is not a mathematical curiosity; it is how oscillators work.

## Norton's theorem

:::key
**Norton's theorem:** Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced by an ideal current source *I*<sub>N</sub> in parallel with a resistance *R*<sub>N</sub>, where *I*<sub>N</sub> is the short-circuit current and *R*<sub>N</sub> = *R*<sub>Th</sub>.
:::

Norton's theorem is Thévenin's theorem after a source transformation: *I*<sub>N</sub> = *V*<sub>Th</sub>/*R*<sub>Th</sub>. The network of Figure 6.1 has a Norton equivalent of 1.5 A in parallel with 4 Ω (Figure 6.3). Which form you use is a matter of convenience. Norton equivalents are natural when the load is connected in parallel with other elements, or when you are going to use nodal analysis afterwards.

## Maximum power transfer

Given a source with fixed Thévenin equivalent, what load resistance draws the most power from it? The load power is

$$ <i>P</i><sub>L</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>Th</sub><sup>2</sup><i>R</i><sub>L</sub>/(<i>R</i><sub>Th</sub> + <i>R</i><sub>L</sub>)<sup>2</sup>  || 6.1

Differentiating with respect to *R*<sub>L</sub> and setting the result to zero gives the condition

$$ <i>R</i><sub>L</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>Th</sub>,  <i>P</i><sub>max</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>Th</sub><sup>2</sup>/(4<i>R</i><sub>Th</sub>)  || 6.2

For the network of Figure 6.1 that means a 4 Ω load, receiving 36/16 = 2.25 W.

At maximum power transfer exactly half the power generated is dissipated in the source's own Thévenin resistance, so the efficiency is only 50 %. For communication and instrumentation systems, where signals are weak and wasted power is a small price for a stronger signal, matching the load to the source is standard practice. For power systems it would be ruinous. A power station runs into a load resistance many times larger than its internal impedance, delivering far less than the theoretical maximum but at high efficiency.

:::tip
A very common examination trap: the question asks for the *load* that maximises power, given a *fixed* source. If instead the load is fixed and you may choose the source resistance, the answer is completely different. Power into a fixed load is maximised by making the source resistance as small as possible, ideally zero.
:::

## A bridge solved with Thévenin's theorem

Chapter 2 left a question unanswered: what current flows through the galvanometer of an unbalanced bridge? Thévenin's theorem gives a clean answer. Remove the galvanometer and find the Thévenin equivalent seen between the two midpoints.

:::example Galvanometer current
A bridge is supplied at 10 V. The top-left arm is 100 Ω, the bottom-left 300 Ω, the top-right 200 Ω and the bottom-right 200 Ω. A 50 Ω galvanometer joins the two midpoints, a (left) and b (right). Find the galvanometer current.

*Open-circuit voltage.* Each side is a divider: *V*<sub>a</sub> = 10 × 300/400 = 7.5 V and *V*<sub>b</sub> = 10 × 200/400 = 5 V, so *V*<sub>Th</sub> = 2.5 V.

*Thévenin resistance.* Shorting the supply joins the top and bottom nodes. Seen from a, the two left-hand arms are in parallel: 100 ∥ 300 = 75 Ω. Seen from b, 200 ∥ 200 = 100 Ω. These are in series between a and b, so *R*<sub>Th</sub> = 175 Ω.

*Current.* *I*<sub>g</sub> = 2.5/(175 + 50) = 11.1 mA, from a to b.

A full mesh analysis would need three simultaneous equations to reach the same answer. Here it took three short lines.
:::

## Choosing a method

By now you have quite a toolkit. A rough guide to choosing:

| Situation | Usually best |
|---|---|
| Need every voltage and current | Nodal or mesh analysis |
| Need one quantity, or the load varies | Thévenin or Norton |
| Several sources, need each one's contribution | Superposition |
| Several practical sources in parallel | Source transformation or Millman |
| Choosing a load for best power | Thévenin, then maximum power transfer |
| Dependent sources present | Nodal/mesh, or Thévenin via *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub> or a test source |

None of these rules is absolute, and with experience you will find yourself combining methods: a Thévenin equivalent found by nodal analysis, say, or superposition applied to a circuit already simplified by source transformation.

:::example Combining practical sources
A 12 V battery with 3 Ω internal resistance and a 6 V source with 6 Ω internal resistance are connected in parallel (positive to positive) across a 4 Ω load. Find the load voltage using source transformations.

*Transform each source.* The 12 V, 3 Ω source becomes 4 A in parallel with 3 Ω. The 6 V, 6 Ω source becomes 1 A in parallel with 6 Ω.

*Combine.* The current sources add to 5 A, and 3 ∥ 6 = 2 Ω.

*Transform back.* 5 A in parallel with 2 Ω is equivalent to 10 V in series with 2 Ω. This is the Thévenin equivalent of the two sources together.

*Load.* *V*<sub>L</sub> = 10 × 4/(2 + 4) = 6.67 V and *I*<sub>L</sub> = 1.67 A.

The 6 V source's terminal voltage is 6.67 V, higher than its own EMF, so current flows *into* it: (6.67 − 6)/6 = 0.111 A. It is being charged by the 12 V battery, which a quick glance at the original circuit would not have revealed.
:::

:::example A Thévenin resistance with a dependent source
A network seen from terminals a–b consists of a 10 Ω resistor between a and b, in parallel with a voltage-controlled current source that draws 0.05*v*<sub>ab</sub> amperes from a to b. There are no independent sources. Find *R*<sub>Th</sub>.

With no independent sources, *V*<sub>Th</sub> = 0, so we apply a test voltage *V* across a–b. The current drawn is *V*/10 + 0.05*V* = 0.15*V*, so *R*<sub>Th</sub> = *V*/0.15*V* = 6.67 Ω.

The dependent source makes the network look like an extra 20 Ω resistor in parallel with the 10 Ω. Simply combining "the resistors" would have given the wrong answer of 10 Ω.
:::

## In practice: Thévenin models everywhere

Once you start looking, Thévenin equivalents are everywhere in engineering. A car battery is described by its open-circuit voltage and internal resistance, and the cold-cranking test measures exactly how far the terminal voltage sags when a heavy load is applied. An audio amplifier's datasheet quotes its output impedance, and the "damping factor" that hi-fi enthusiasts argue about is simply the ratio of loudspeaker impedance to that Thévenin impedance. A sensor's output resistance tells you how much its reading will fall when connected to a data logger with a finite input resistance.

Engineers also measure Thévenin equivalents directly. Measure the open-circuit voltage with a high-impedance voltmeter, then connect a known load resistor and measure again. The drop in voltage, together with the load current, gives the internal resistance. This two-measurement technique works on anything from a solar panel to a laboratory power supply, and it is far more reliable than trying to short-circuit the source, which is often dangerous and sometimes destructive.

## Check your understanding

**1. What replaces a voltage source when it is set to zero?** A short circuit. A current source becomes an open circuit.

**2. Can you superpose powers?** No. Superpose voltages and currents, then calculate power.

**3. What are the two quantities that define a Thévenin equivalent, and how is each found?** *V*<sub>Th</sub>, the open-circuit voltage; and *R*<sub>Th</sub>, the resistance seen with independent sources killed (or *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub>).

**4. What is the efficiency at maximum power transfer?** 50 %.

:::summary
- Superposition: in a linear circuit, add the responses to each independent source acting alone. Kill voltage sources with shorts and current sources with opens. Never superpose power.
- A practical voltage source *V*<sub>s</sub> in series with *R* is equivalent at its terminals to *V*<sub>s</sub>/*R* in parallel with *R*.
- Thévenin: *V*<sub>Th</sub> = *V*<sub>oc</sub>; *R*<sub>Th</sub> is the resistance seen with independent sources killed, or *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub>.
- Norton: *I*<sub>N</sub> = *I*<sub>sc</sub> in parallel with *R*<sub>N</sub> = *R*<sub>Th</sub>.
- With dependent sources, find *R*<sub>Th</sub> from *V*<sub>oc</sub>/*I*<sub>sc</sub> or with a test source.
- Maximum power is delivered when *R*<sub>L</sub> = *R*<sub>Th</sub>, with *P*<sub>max</sub> = *V*<sub>Th</sub><sup>2</sup>/4*R*<sub>Th</sub>, at 50 % efficiency.
:::

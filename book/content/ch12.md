Almost all the electrical energy generated in the world is produced, transmitted and distributed as three-phase AC. Walk past any substation and count the conductors on the overhead lines: they come in threes. The reasons are partly economic and partly physical. Three-phase systems carry more power for a given amount of conductor material than single-phase ones. They deliver power that is constant from instant to instant rather than pulsating, so large motors run smoothly. And they make it easy to produce a rotating magnetic field, which is the operating principle of the induction motor, the workhorse of industry.

The good news is that a balanced three-phase system, the overwhelmingly common case, can be analysed by treating just one phase and using symmetry to deduce the other two. Everything you learned in Chapters 10 and 11 still applies. This final chapter adds the vocabulary and the handful of √3 factors that make three-phase problems their own subject.

## The balanced three-phase supply

A three-phase generator has three identical windings spaced 120° apart round its stator. As the rotor turns, each winding produces a sinusoidal voltage of the same amplitude and frequency, but each is shifted by 120° from the next.

{{fig:t12_waves|The three phase voltages of a balanced supply. Each is displaced by 120°, or one third of a cycle, from the next.}}

With the voltage of phase a as reference, the three **phase voltages** (measured from each line to the neutral point, n) are, for the standard **positive** (or abc) sequence:

$$ <b>V</b><sub>an</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>p</sub>∠0°,  <b>V</b><sub>bn</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>p</sub>∠−120°,  <b>V</b><sub>cn</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>p</sub>∠+120°  || 12.1

The **phase sequence** is the order in which the phases reach their peaks. Reversing any two supply connections reverses the sequence (acb), which reverses the direction of rotation of a three-phase motor, a fact every electrician learns early, sometimes the hard way.

A crucial property of a balanced set: the three phasors sum to zero. Add them and they form a closed triangle. Consequently, in a balanced system the neutral conductor carries no current at all.

## Line and phase quantities

There are two ways to connect three sources or three loads, and the distinction between **line** quantities (measured on the conductors between source and load) and **phase** quantities (measured across an individual source winding or load element) is central to the subject.

### Star (Y) connection

In a **star** connection, one end of each element joins a common neutral point. Each line conductor connects to the other end of one element. So the line current is the same as the phase current. The **line voltage**, measured between two lines, is the difference of two phase voltages:

$$ <b>V</b><sub>ab</sub> = <b>V</b><sub>an</sub> − <b>V</b><sub>bn</sub> = √3 <i>V</i><sub>p</sub>∠30°  || 12.2

{{fig:t12_phasors|Phasor diagram of a positive-sequence supply. The line voltage V_ab is √3 times the phase voltage and leads V_an by 30°.}}

:::key
**Star:** *V*<sub>L</sub> = √3 *V*<sub>ph</sub>, *I*<sub>L</sub> = *I*<sub>ph</sub>. Line voltages lead the corresponding phase voltages by 30° in positive sequence.
:::

That √3 is where the UK's familiar pair of voltages comes from. Each phase of a low-voltage distribution system provides 230 V to neutral, and the voltage between any two phases is 230√3 ≈ 400 V. Homes are fed with one phase and neutral; factories and larger buildings take all three phases.

{{fig:t12_star|A star-connected load. Each impedance sits between one line and the neutral point n.}}

### Delta (Δ) connection

In a **delta** connection, the three elements form a closed triangle, and each line connects to one corner. Each element sits directly between two lines, so the phase voltage *is* the line voltage. Now the line current is the difference of two phase currents:

:::key
**Delta:** *V*<sub>L</sub> = *V*<sub>ph</sub>, *I*<sub>L</sub> = √3 *I*<sub>ph</sub>. Line currents lag the corresponding phase currents by 30° in positive sequence.
:::

{{fig:t12_delta|A delta-connected load. Each impedance is connected between two lines.}}

A delta has no neutral point, so a delta-connected load cannot be supplied with a neutral conductor. Delta connections are common for motors and for the high-voltage windings of distribution transformers.

## Power in three-phase systems

The total power in a balanced load is three times the power in one phase: *P* = 3*V*<sub>ph</sub>*I*<sub>ph</sub> cos φ, where φ is the angle of the load impedance. Substituting the line–phase relationships gives a result that holds for **both** star and delta:

$$ <i>P</i> = √3 <i>V</i><sub>L</sub><i>I</i><sub>L</sub> cos φ  || 12.3

with corresponding expressions *Q* = √3*V*<sub>L</sub>*I*<sub>L</sub> sin φ and *S* = √3*V*<sub>L</sub>*I*<sub>L</sub>. Equation 12.3 is used constantly, because line voltage and line current are the quantities you can actually measure without getting inside the equipment.

:::pitfall
The angle φ in Equation 12.3 is the angle of the load impedance: the angle between *phase* voltage and *phase* current. It is *not* the angle between line voltage and line current, which differs by 30°. Mixing these up is the most common error in three-phase power calculations.
:::

One further property is worth knowing. In a balanced three-phase system, the total *instantaneous* power is constant, not pulsating at twice the supply frequency as in single phase. The pulsations of the three phases cancel exactly. That is why a three-phase motor runs smoothly and quietly, whereas a large single-phase motor tends to vibrate.

## The per-phase method

To analyse a balanced system, convert any delta-connected parts into equivalent stars (for balanced impedances, **Z**<sub>Y</sub> = **Z**<sub>Δ</sub>/3, from Chapter 2), then analyse a single phase: one phase voltage driving one star impedance, with the neutral as return. The results for the other two phases are identical in magnitude and shifted by ±120°. Finally, convert back to delta quantities if the question asks for them.

:::example The same load, star and delta
A balanced load has an impedance of 8 + j6 Ω per phase and is supplied at 400 V. Find the line current and total power with the load connected (a) in star and (b) in delta.

|**Z**| = 10 Ω and the power factor is 8/10 = 0.8 lagging.

*(a) Star.* *V*<sub>ph</sub> = 400/√3 = 231 V, so *I*<sub>L</sub> = *I*<sub>ph</sub> = 231/10 = 23.1 A. *P* = 3 × 23.1<sup>2</sup> × 8 = 12.8 kW. As a check, √3 × 400 × 23.1 × 0.8 = 12.8 kW.

*(b) Delta.* *V*<sub>ph</sub> = 400 V, so *I*<sub>ph</sub> = 40 A and *I*<sub>L</sub> = √3 × 40 = 69.3 A. *P* = 3 × 40<sup>2</sup> × 8 = 38.4 kW.

Connecting the same three impedances in delta draws exactly three times the power and three times the line current. That is the principle behind the star–delta starter: a large motor is started in star to limit the inrush current, then switched to delta once it is up to speed.
:::

## Measuring power: the two-wattmeter method

A wattmeter measures the average of the product of the voltage across its voltage coil and the current through its current coil. In a three-wire system the total power can be measured with just **two** wattmeters, each with its current coil in one line and its voltage coil connected between that line and the third line. The total power is the sum of the two readings,

$$ <i>P</i> = <i>W</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>W</i><sub>2</sub>  || 12.4

whether the load is balanced or not, and whether it is star or delta. For a balanced load the readings also reveal the power factor:

$$ tan φ = √3(<i>W</i><sub>1</sub> − <i>W</i><sub>2</sub>)/(<i>W</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>W</i><sub>2</sub>)  || 12.5

since individually *W*<sub>1</sub> = *V*<sub>L</sub>*I*<sub>L</sub> cos(30° − φ) and *W*<sub>2</sub> = *V*<sub>L</sub>*I*<sub>L</sub> cos(30° + φ).

At unity power factor the two readings are equal. At pf 0.5 one reading is zero. Below 0.5, one wattmeter reads *negative*, and its current-coil connections must be reversed to obtain a reading, which is then subtracted. That negative reading is genuine and catches out many students who assume they have made an error.

:::example Two wattmeters
Two wattmeters on a balanced load read 8 kW and 4 kW. Find the total power and the power factor.

*P* = 8 + 4 = 12 kW. tan φ = √3 × (8 − 4)/12 = 0.577, so φ = 30° and pf = cos 30° = 0.866 lagging (assuming an inductive load, as is usual).
:::

## Power-factor correction and line losses

Three-phase power-factor correction follows the single-phase method of Chapter 11, with the total reactive power shared equally between three capacitors. Whether they are connected in star or in delta makes a large difference to the capacitance required. In delta each capacitor sees the full line voltage, and since reactive power goes with *V*<sup>2</sup>, a delta bank needs only one third of the capacitance of a star bank for the same correction. The price is a higher voltage rating.

Line losses are found per phase, too. If each line has resistance *R*<sub>line</sub> and carries current *I*<sub>L</sub>, the total loss is 3*I*<sub>L</sub><sup>2</sup>*R*<sub>line</sub>. Because loss depends on the square of current, improving the power factor (and so reducing current) or raising the transmission voltage (for the same power) cuts losses dramatically. This is exactly why national grids transmit at hundreds of kilovolts.

:::tip
Before any three-phase calculation, write down the connection type and the two relationships that go with it: "Star: *V*<sub>L</sub> = √3*V*<sub>ph</sub>, *I*<sub>L</sub> = *I*<sub>ph</sub>" or "Delta: *V*<sub>L</sub> = *V*<sub>ph</sub>, *I*<sub>L</sub> = √3*I*<sub>ph</sub>". If a question simply states "a 400 V supply", the 400 V is the line voltage unless it says otherwise.
:::

## Unbalanced systems

Real loads are never perfectly balanced. Single-phase loads such as lighting and sockets are distributed across the three phases as evenly as possible, but some imbalance always remains, and the neutral then carries the difference current. Unbalanced systems are analysed by straightforward nodal or mesh analysis with phasors, or by the method of symmetrical components, which is beyond the scope of this book. The problems in this chapter deal with balanced systems, which are both the most common in practice and the foundation for everything else.

In 1845, while still a student at Königsberg, Gustav Kirchhoff wrote down two short statements about electrical networks. Nearly two centuries later they remain the foundation of the entire subject. Every method in this book — nodal analysis, mesh analysis, Thévenin's theorem, transient analysis, AC phasors — is, underneath, an organised way of applying Kirchhoff's two laws together with the equations that describe individual components.

The laws themselves can be stated in a sentence each. Using them reliably is another matter. Almost every wrong answer I mark in circuit analysis can be traced back to a sign error in a Kirchhoff equation, so this chapter spends as much time on sign conventions and bookkeeping as on the laws themselves.

:::objectives
- State and apply Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) and voltage law (KVL).
- Use reference directions and polarities consistently, and interpret negative results correctly.
- Solve single-loop and single-node-pair circuits, including those with several sources.
- Use the branch-current method to analyse circuits with two or more loops.
- Handle dependent (controlled) sources.
- Verify solutions using a power balance.
:::

## Some vocabulary

A few terms need to be pinned down before we begin. A **node** is a point where two or more elements meet; all points joined by ideal wire form a single node, however spread out they look on the diagram. A **branch** is a single element, or a string of elements carrying the same current, between two nodes. A **loop** is any closed path that passes through no node more than once. A **mesh** is a loop that does not enclose any other branch, like the panes of a window.

Getting the node count right is the first step in every analysis. A common mistake is to count both ends of a long wire as separate nodes. If there is no component between two points, they are the same node.

## Kirchhoff's current law

:::key
**KCL:** The algebraic sum of the currents entering any node is zero. Equivalently, the total current flowing into a node equals the total current flowing out.
:::

KCL is a statement of conservation of charge. Charge cannot pile up at a junction of ideal wires, so whatever flows in must flow out. The law applies not only to single nodes but to any closed surface drawn round part of a circuit, a fact that is surprisingly useful. Drawing a surface round a whole sub-circuit often gives you a relationship between external currents without your needing to know anything about what happens inside.

To write a KCL equation you need a **reference direction** for every current. You choose these directions yourself, before solving, and you do not need to guess correctly. If a current turns out negative, it simply flows the other way. What you must not do is change your mind halfway through the solution. Mark the arrows on your diagram and stick to them.

## Kirchhoff's voltage law

:::key
**KVL:** The algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop is zero. Equivalently, the sum of the voltage rises round a loop equals the sum of the voltage drops.
:::

KVL is a statement of conservation of energy. A charge that travels round a closed loop and arrives back where it started must have gained exactly as much energy as it lost. Returning to the hillside picture from Chapter 1: if you walk in a circle, you end at the same height you started, however many ups and downs there were on the way.

To write a KVL equation, pick a starting point and a direction of travel, then walk round the loop adding up voltages. The convention I recommend, and use throughout the solutions, is:

- Passing through a source from − to + is a **rise**.
- Passing through a resistor in the same direction as its assumed current is a **drop** of *IR*.

Then write rises = drops. Other textbooks write Σ*v* = 0 with a different sign rule; the two are equivalent, and what matters is consistency.

## Single-loop circuits

A circuit with only one loop has only one current, which makes it the ideal place to practise KVL. Choose a direction for the loop current — clockwise is the usual habit — and walk round once.

If there are several sources, some will push current in your chosen direction and some will oppose it. A source "aids" the loop current if the current leaves its + terminal, and "opposes" it if the current enters the + terminal. An opposing source is absorbing power: a battery being charged, or a motor whose back-EMF opposes the supply.

## Single-node-pair circuits

The dual of the single loop is a circuit in which every element is connected between the same two nodes, so every element has the same voltage across it. Here KCL is the natural tool: the currents injected by the sources must leave through the resistors. Writing the resistor currents as *V*/*R*, or better as *VG*, gives one equation in one unknown, *V*.

You will notice that single loops are naturally analysed with KVL and single node pairs with KCL. That observation is the seed of the two great systematic methods of Part II. Mesh analysis generalises the single loop; nodal analysis generalises the single node pair.

## The branch-current method

For circuits with more than one loop, the most direct approach is to assign an unknown current to every branch and write enough independent KCL and KVL equations to solve for them all. For a circuit with *b* branches and *n* nodes you need *n* − 1 KCL equations (the last node gives nothing new) and *b* − *n* + 1 KVL equations, one for each mesh.

{{fig:t03_tnet|A two-source circuit with three branch currents. I₁ and I₃ are taken left to right and I₂ downward through R₂.}}

:::example Three branch currents
Find the branch currents in the circuit of Figure 3.1.

*KCL at node A:* *I*<sub>1</sub> = *I*<sub>2</sub> + *I*<sub>3</sub>.

*KVL in the left mesh:* 12 = 2*I*<sub>1</sub> + 4*I*<sub>2</sub>.

*KVL in the right mesh:* going from A down through *R*<sub>2</sub>, the drop is 4*I*<sub>2</sub>; that must equal the drop through *R*<sub>3</sub> plus the 6 V source, so 4*I*<sub>2</sub> = 4*I*<sub>3</sub> + 6.

*Solve.* From the right-mesh equation, *I*<sub>3</sub> = *I*<sub>2</sub> − 1.5. Substituting into KCL gives *I*<sub>1</sub> = 2*I*<sub>2</sub> − 1.5, and the left-mesh equation becomes 12 = 2(2*I*<sub>2</sub> − 1.5) + 4*I*<sub>2</sub> = 8*I*<sub>2</sub> − 3, so *I*<sub>2</sub> = 1.875 A, *I*<sub>3</sub> = 0.375 A and *I*<sub>1</sub> = 2.25 A.

*Power balance.* The 12 V source delivers 12 × 2.25 = 27 W. The resistors absorb 2.25<sup>2</sup> × 2 + 1.875<sup>2</sup> × 4 + 0.375<sup>2</sup> × 4 = 10.125 + 14.063 + 0.563 = 24.75 W. The 6 V source has current entering its + terminal, so it absorbs 6 × 0.375 = 2.25 W. Total absorbed: 27 W. The books balance.
:::

The branch-current method always works, but it generates more equations than necessary. A circuit with six branches needs six simultaneous equations, whereas nodal or mesh analysis might need only two or three. That inefficiency is precisely what Part II fixes.

## Dependent sources

So far every source has had a fixed value. A **dependent source** (also called a controlled source) has a value that depends on a voltage or current somewhere else in the circuit. There are four kinds:

| Type | Output | Controlled by |
|---|---|---|
| VCVS | voltage | a voltage elsewhere |
| CCVS | voltage | a current elsewhere |
| VCCS | current | a voltage elsewhere |
| CCCS | current | a current elsewhere |

Dependent sources are drawn as diamonds to distinguish them from independent sources. They are not exotic: they are how we model transistors, operational amplifiers and every other active device. A bipolar transistor in its active region behaves very much like a current-controlled current source, with collector current equal to β times base current.

The method for handling them is simple and never changes. Write your KCL and KVL equations treating the dependent source exactly like an independent one. Then write **one extra equation** expressing the controlling variable in terms of your unknowns. Substitute, and solve as normal.

:::example A dependent source in a loop
A 20 V source drives a series loop containing *R*<sub>1</sub> = 4 Ω, *R*<sub>2</sub> = 6 Ω and a VCVS of value 2*V*<sub>x</sub>, where *V*<sub>x</sub> is the voltage across *R*<sub>1</sub>. The VCVS opposes the loop current. Find the current.

*KVL:* 20 = 4*I* + 6*I* + 2*V*<sub>x</sub>.

*Control equation:* *V*<sub>x</sub> = 4*I*.

*Substitute:* 20 = 10*I* + 8*I* = 18*I*, so *I* = 1.11 A, *V*<sub>x</sub> = 4.44 V and the dependent source produces 8.89 V.

Notice that the dependent source behaves, as far as this loop is concerned, like an extra 8 Ω resistor. Spotting this kind of equivalence is a valuable skill, and it is exactly the effect that emitter degeneration exploits in transistor amplifiers.
:::

:::pitfall
Never "kill" a dependent source when using superposition or finding a Thévenin resistance. Only independent sources are set to zero. A dependent source represents a physical property of a device, and it stays put.
:::

## Power balance as a check

In any circuit, the total power delivered by the sources equals the total power absorbed by everything else. It is an extremely effective check because it uses every current and voltage you have calculated, so almost any error will show up as an imbalance. Under the passive sign convention:

- An element absorbs power *vi* if the current enters its + terminal.
- An element with current leaving its + terminal delivers power *vi*.

Resistors always absorb. Sources may do either, and a source absorbing power is perfectly normal. Think of a phone battery on charge.

## Potentials and the reference node

Once you know the currents in a circuit, finding the voltage between any two points is a matter of walking from one to the other and adding up the rises and drops on the way. It is usually cleanest to choose one node as the **reference** (0 V), compute the potential of every other node relative to it, and then subtract. The reference can be any node at all; choosing the negative terminal of the main source, or the node with the most connections, usually makes the arithmetic easiest.

:::tip
When a question asks for "the voltage *V*<sub>ab</sub>", it means the potential of a minus the potential of b. Getting this the wrong way round costs marks even when every other line is right. Write *V*<sub>ab</sub> = *V*<sub>a</sub> − *V*<sub>b</sub> explicitly before substituting numbers.
:::

## Choosing between KCL and KVL

You can analyse any circuit using KCL and KVL together, but with experience you will start to see which law gives the shortest route. Circuits with many elements in parallel and only a few nodes usually favour KCL. Circuits with many elements in series and only a few loops usually favour KVL. That instinct, sharpened into a method, is what Chapters 4 and 5 are about.

The problems that follow include plenty of single-loop and single-node-pair practice, because fluency here makes everything else easier. The later problems introduce two-mesh circuits, dependent sources and current sources in awkward places. In every one, mark your reference directions on the diagram before writing a single equation.

:::summary
- KCL (conservation of charge): currents into a node sum to zero. It applies to any closed surface as well as single nodes.
- KVL (conservation of energy): voltages round any closed loop sum to zero.
- Choose reference directions first and keep them; negative answers just mean the true direction is opposite.
- A source with current entering its + terminal is absorbing power.
- The branch-current method needs *n* − 1 KCL equations and *b* − *n* + 1 KVL equations.
- Treat dependent sources like independent ones, then add an equation for the controlling variable.
- A power balance is the most thorough single check on any solution.
:::

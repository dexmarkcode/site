Resistors have no memory. The current through a resistor right now depends only on the voltage across it right now. Capacitors and inductors are different. They store energy, a capacitor in an electric field and an inductor in a magnetic one, and their behaviour depends on what has happened to them before. That single fact is responsible for almost everything interesting in electronics: filters, oscillators, timing circuits, switch-mode power supplies, radio tuning and the smoothing of every DC supply you have ever plugged in.

This chapter introduces the two storage elements on their own. Chapters 8 and 9 put them into circuits and study how those circuits respond to sudden changes; Part IV studies their response to sinusoids.

:::objectives
- State the voltage–current relationships of ideal capacitors and inductors in both differential and integral form.
- Calculate stored energy and explain why capacitor voltage and inductor current cannot change instantaneously.
- Combine capacitors and inductors in series and parallel.
- Estimate capacitance and inductance from physical dimensions.
- Find the DC steady state of circuits containing capacitors and inductors.
:::

## The capacitor

A capacitor is, in essence, two conductors separated by an insulator, the **dielectric**. Apply a voltage and charge +*q* collects on one plate and −*q* on the other. For an ideal capacitor the charge is proportional to the voltage:

$$ <i>q</i> = <i>Cv</i>  || 7.1

The constant *C* is the **capacitance**, measured in farads (F). One farad is very large: practical capacitors range from a few picofarads in radio circuits to many thousands of microfarads in power supply reservoirs. Supercapacitors reach hundreds of farads, but only at low voltages.

Differentiating Equation 7.1 gives the terminal relationship we use in circuit analysis:

$$ <i>i</i> = <i>C</i> d<i>v</i>/d<i>t</i>  || 7.2

Read that equation carefully, because it says something striking. The current through a capacitor depends not on the voltage across it but on how fast that voltage is *changing*. A capacitor sitting at a steady 100 V carries no current at all. A capacitor whose voltage is rising quickly carries a large current.

{{fig:t07_ramp|Capacitor voltage and current for a 1 μF capacitor. While the voltage ramps up the current is constant and positive; while it is flat the current is zero; while it falls the current reverses.}}

Figure 7.1 shows this for a voltage that ramps up, holds, then ramps down. Rising by 10 V in 1 ms means d*v*/d*t* = 10<sup>4</sup> V/s, so *i* = 10<sup>−6</sup> × 10<sup>4</sup> = 10 mA. On the flat section the current is zero. Falling by 10 V over 2 ms gives −5 mA. The current waveform is the *slope* of the voltage waveform, scaled by *C*.

The integral form is just as useful:

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>v</i>(<i>t</i><sub>0</sub>) + (1/<i>C</i>) ∫<sub><i>t</i><sub>0</sub></sub><sup><i>t</i></sup> <i>i</i> d<i>t</i>  || 7.3

The capacitor voltage at any moment depends on its starting voltage plus the accumulated charge delivered since then. That is the memory. Feed a capacitor a constant current and its voltage rises in a perfectly straight line, a fact exploited in every sawtooth generator and integrating analogue-to-digital converter.

### Energy stored in a capacitor

Integrating the power *vi* as the capacitor charges from zero gives the stored energy:

$$ <i>W</i> = ½<i>Cv</i><sup>2</sup>  || 7.4

A 4700 μF reservoir capacitor charged to 50 V holds ½ × 4700 × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 2500 = 5.9 J. That is enough to give a nasty shock and to weld a screwdriver tip, which is why such capacitors must be discharged before work begins, even with the power off.

## Physical capacitance

For two parallel plates of area *A* separated by distance *d*,

$$ <i>C</i> = ε<sub>0</sub>ε<sub>r</sub><i>A</i>/<i>d</i>  || 7.5

where ε<sub>0</sub> = 8.854 × 10<sup>−12</sup> F/m and ε<sub>r</sub> is the relative permittivity of the dielectric. Large capacitance needs large area, a thin dielectric and a high permittivity. Manufacturers achieve this by rolling long strips of metallised film, stacking hundreds of ceramic layers, or using the extremely thin oxide films of electrolytic capacitors. Every approach involves trade-offs in voltage rating, stability, size and cost.

## The inductor

An inductor is usually a coil of wire, perhaps wound on an iron or ferrite core. Current in the coil creates a magnetic field, and any change in that current changes the field, which induces a voltage that opposes the change (Lenz's law). For an ideal inductor:

$$ <i>v</i> = <i>L</i> d<i>i</i>/d<i>t</i>  || 7.6

where *L* is the **inductance** in henries (H). Practical inductors range from nanohenries in radio circuits to several henries in smoothing chokes.

Equation 7.6 is the exact dual of Equation 7.2, with voltage and current swapped. A steady current through an inductor produces no voltage at all, and an ideal inductor carrying DC is a short circuit. A rapidly changing current produces a large voltage. This is the principle of the car ignition coil, which interrupts a few amperes in a fraction of a millisecond to generate tens of kilovolts, and of the boost converter in every LED driver.

The integral form gives the inductor's memory:

$$ <i>i</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>i</i>(<i>t</i><sub>0</sub>) + (1/<i>L</i>) ∫<sub><i>t</i><sub>0</sub></sub><sup><i>t</i></sup> <i>v</i> d<i>t</i>  || 7.7

### Energy stored in an inductor

$$ <i>W</i> = ½<i>Li</i><sup>2</sup>  || 7.8

Again the analogy is exact: *L* plays the role of *C*, and current plays the role of voltage.

### Physical inductance

For a long solenoid of *N* turns, cross-sectional area *A* and length *l*,

$$ <i>L</i> = μ<sub>0</sub>μ<sub>r</sub><i>N</i><sup>2</sup><i>A</i>/<i>l</i>  || 7.9

with μ<sub>0</sub> = 4π × 10<sup>−7</sup> H/m. The *N*<sup>2</sup> dependence is the important feature: doubling the number of turns quadruples the inductance. A magnetic core multiplies the inductance by μ<sub>r</sub>, which can be in the thousands for iron, but only until the core saturates, after which the extra inductance vanishes. Real inductor design is largely about avoiding saturation.

## Continuity: the most important idea in this chapter

Because energy cannot change instantaneously (that would need infinite power), and because the stored energy depends on *v* for a capacitor and on *i* for an inductor, we arrive at two rules that underpin the whole of Chapters 8 and 9:

:::key
- **The voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously.**
- **The current through an inductor cannot change instantaneously.**

Capacitor *current* and inductor *voltage* can jump abruptly; capacitor voltage and inductor current cannot.
:::

In practice this means that if a switch operates at *t* = 0, the capacitor voltage just after the switching instant, written *v*(0<sup>+</sup>), equals the voltage just before, *v*(0<sup>−</sup>). Likewise *i*<sub>L</sub>(0<sup>+</sup>) = *i*<sub>L</sub>(0<sup>−</sup>). These continuity conditions are what let us solve transient problems.

The inductor rule has a dramatic practical consequence. If you try to interrupt the current in an inductor suddenly, for example by opening a switch, the inductor generates whatever voltage is needed to keep the current flowing. That voltage can be enough to arc across the switch contacts or destroy a transistor. This is why relay coils are fitted with flyback diodes.

## Series and parallel combinations

Capacitors and inductors combine according to simple rules, which you can derive from KVL, KCL and the element equations:

| | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Inductors | <i>L</i><sub>eq</sub> = <i>L</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>L</i><sub>2</sub> + … | 1/<i>L</i><sub>eq</sub> = 1/<i>L</i><sub>1</sub> + 1/<i>L</i><sub>2</sub> + … |
| Capacitors | 1/<i>C</i><sub>eq</sub> = 1/<i>C</i><sub>1</sub> + 1/<i>C</i><sub>2</sub> + … | <i>C</i><sub>eq</sub> = <i>C</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>C</i><sub>2</sub> + … |

Inductors behave like resistors. Capacitors behave the opposite way: they *add in parallel* (more plate area) and combine reciprocally in series (effectively a thicker dielectric).

Series capacitors all carry the same charge, since the same current flows through each for the same time. The voltage therefore divides in *inverse* proportion to capacitance: the smallest capacitor takes the largest share. That is worth remembering when capacitors are stacked in series to reach a higher voltage rating, because without balancing resistors, mismatched capacitors share the voltage unevenly.

:::pitfall
Inductor formulas assume no magnetic coupling between the coils. Two inductors wound on the same core, or placed close together, have mutual inductance, and their combined inductance can be considerably larger or smaller than these formulas suggest. All the problems in this book assume no coupling unless they say otherwise.
:::

## Charge sharing and the "missing" energy

Here is a classic puzzle. A 10 μF capacitor is charged to 100 V and then connected across an identical uncharged capacitor. Charge is conserved, so the 1 mC is shared between 20 μF and the final voltage is 50 V. But the initial stored energy was ½ × 10 × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 100<sup>2</sup> = 50 mJ, and the final energy is ½ × 20 × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 50<sup>2</sup> = 25 mJ. Half the energy has vanished.

It has not vanished; it has been dissipated in the resistance of the connecting wires and radiated as electromagnetic energy during the transfer. Remarkably, the amount lost does not depend on how small the resistance is. A smaller resistance gives a larger, briefer current, and the total *i*<sup>2</sup>*R* loss comes out the same. Ideal circuit theory, with zero-resistance wires, simply cannot describe this event, which is a useful reminder that our models have limits.

## DC steady state

After a circuit containing capacitors and inductors has been connected to DC sources for a long time, all voltages and currents settle to constant values. Then every derivative is zero, so:

- every capacitor carries zero current and behaves as an **open circuit**;
- every inductor has zero voltage across it and behaves as a **short circuit**.

Replacing each capacitor with an open circuit and each inductor with a short reduces the circuit to a purely resistive one that you already know how to solve. Finding this steady state is the first step in nearly every transient problem, because it gives the initial conditions before a switch operates and the final values afterwards.

*W*<sub>C</sub> = ½ × 100 × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 8<sup>2</sup> = 3.2 mJ and *W*<sub>L</sub> = ½ × 0.05 × 2<sup>2</sup> = 0.1 J.

## Real components

Ideal elements are useful fictions. A real capacitor has some series resistance (ESR), some leakage, some series inductance, and a capacitance that may drift with temperature, voltage and age. A real inductor has winding resistance, inter-turn capacitance and core losses. At low frequencies and in the problems of this book these imperfections can usually be ignored, but in high-frequency or high-power design they often dominate. A good engineer always knows which idealisations are being made, and roughly when they stop being safe.

:::tip
Whenever a question involves a switch and a capacitor or inductor, your first two lines should almost always be: "Before the switch operates, the circuit is in DC steady state, so…" and "By continuity, *v*<sub>C</sub>(0<sup>+</sup>) = *v*<sub>C</sub>(0<sup>−</sup>)" (or the inductor equivalent). Examiners award marks for these statements, and writing them stops you forgetting them.
:::

:::example Capacitors sharing a high voltage
A 2 μF and a 4 μF capacitor are connected in series across a 300 V DC supply. Find the voltage on each, and explain why this matters for component ratings.

*Equivalent capacitance.* *C* = (2 × 4)/(2 + 4) = 1.33 μF.

*Charge.* *Q* = 1.33 × 10<sup>−6</sup> × 300 = 400 μC, and both capacitors carry this same charge.

*Voltages.* *V*<sub>1</sub> = 400/2 = 200 V on the 2 μF capacitor and *V*<sub>2</sub> = 400/4 = 100 V on the 4 μF capacitor.

The smaller capacitor takes two thirds of the voltage. A designer who assumed an even split, and fitted two 160 V-rated capacitors, would see the 2 μF part fail. In practice, high-voltage capacitor stacks use matched parts and balancing resistors.
:::

:::example An ignition-style spike
A 5 mH coil carries 4 A. The current is interrupted and falls linearly to zero in 50 μs. Find the voltage across the coil during the interruption and the energy released.

*Voltage.* *v* = *L* d*i*/d*t* = 5 × 10<sup>−3</sup> × 4/(50 × 10<sup>−6</sup>) = 400 V, nearly forty times a 12 V supply.

*Energy.* *W* = ½ × 5 × 10<sup>−3</sup> × 4<sup>2</sup> = 40 mJ, which has to go somewhere: into an arc, a spark plug, a protective diode or a snubber network.
:::

## In practice: choosing capacitors and inductors

In a real design, choosing a capacitor means far more than choosing a value. Ceramic capacitors are small, cheap and excellent at high frequencies, but some types lose much of their capacitance when a DC voltage is applied. Electrolytic capacitors give large values in a small space, but they are polarised, they have significant series resistance, and they dry out with age and heat. That is why the bulging electrolytic is the classic cause of failure in old power supplies. Film capacitors are stable and reliable but bulky. Every one of them should be operated well below its voltage rating.

Inductors present their own trade-offs. A larger inductance needs more turns or a better core; more turns mean more winding resistance and more heat; a better core saturates at a lower current unless it is made larger. Designers of switch-mode power supplies spend much of their effort on this one component. The ideal elements in this chapter are the starting point, and the problems use them throughout, but it is worth remembering that the real components on the bench are always rather more complicated.

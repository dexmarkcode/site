In a DC circuit, power is simple: multiply voltage by current. In an AC circuit, the same multiplication gives a number that changes from instant to instant, sometimes becomes negative, and does not straightforwardly tell you how much energy is being used. This chapter sorts out what "power" means once voltage and current can be out of step, and why that matters enormously to anyone who has to pay for, generate or distribute electrical energy.

The ideas here are not only academic. Power factor appears on industrial electricity bills, determines the size of cables and transformers, and is the reason factories install banks of capacitors. Electricians, building services engineers and power engineers deal with these quantities every working day.

:::objectives
- Derive instantaneous and average power in AC circuits.
- Distinguish real, reactive, apparent and complex power, and use the power triangle.
- Calculate the RMS value of non-sinusoidal waveforms.
- Find the total power and power factor of combined loads.
- Design power-factor correction.
- Apply the maximum power transfer theorem to AC circuits.
:::

## Instantaneous and average power

If a load has voltage *v* = *V*<sub>m</sub> cos ω*t* and current *i* = *I*<sub>m</sub> cos(ω*t* − θ), so that the current lags by θ, the instantaneous power is

$$ <i>p</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>vi</i> = ½<i>V</i><sub>m</sub><i>I</i><sub>m</sub>[cos θ + cos(2ω<i>t</i> − θ)]  || 11.1

The power has a constant part and a part oscillating at *twice* the supply frequency. Averaged over a cycle, the oscillating part contributes nothing, leaving the **average** or **real power**:

$$ <i>P</i> = ½<i>V</i><sub>m</sub><i>I</i><sub>m</sub> cos θ = <i>V</i><sub>rms</sub><i>I</i><sub>rms</sub> cos θ  || 11.2

{{fig:t11_instpower|Voltage, current and instantaneous power for a load whose current lags by 50°. The power dips below zero for part of each cycle, when energy flows back to the source. Its average value, P, is marked.}}

Figure 11.1 shows something remarkable: for part of each cycle the power is negative. Energy flows *out* of the load and back to the source. That is the energy stored in the load's inductance or capacitance being returned. Only the average power, *P*, represents energy that is genuinely converted into heat, light or mechanical work.

Two extreme cases are worth noting. A pure resistor has θ = 0, so *P* = *V*<sub>rms</sub>*I*<sub>rms</sub>, just like DC. A pure inductor or capacitor has θ = ±90°, so *P* = 0: energy flows in and out every cycle, but none is consumed.

## Power factor

The factor cos θ in Equation 11.2 is the **power factor** (pf), and θ is the angle between voltage and current, which equals the angle of the load impedance. Power factor is described as **lagging** when the current lags the voltage (inductive loads such as motors and transformers) and **leading** when it leads (capacitive loads).

A load with a poor power factor draws more current than it needs for the real power it uses. That extra current heats the cables, loads the transformers and causes voltage drops across the whole supply network, all without doing any useful work. This is the heart of the matter.

## Real, reactive and apparent power

It is convenient to define three related quantities:

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Real power, <i>P</i> | <i>V</i><sub>rms</sub><i>I</i><sub>rms</sub> cos θ | watt (W) |
| Reactive power, <i>Q</i> | <i>V</i><sub>rms</sub><i>I</i><sub>rms</sub> sin θ | volt-ampere reactive (var) |
| Apparent power, <i>S</i> | <i>V</i><sub>rms</sub><i>I</i><sub>rms</sub> | volt-ampere (VA) |

Real power does the work. Reactive power measures the energy that sloshes back and forth between source and load every cycle; by convention it is positive for inductive loads and negative for capacitive ones. Apparent power is simply the product of RMS voltage and current, and it is what determines the current a supply must provide. That is why transformers and generators are rated in kVA rather than kW.

The three are related by Pythagoras:

$$ <i>S</i><sup>2</sup> = <i>P</i><sup>2</sup> + <i>Q</i><sup>2</sup>,  pf = <i>P</i>/<i>S</i>  || 11.3

and they form the **power triangle** of Figure 11.2.

{{fig:t11_triangle|The power triangle for a lagging load. Real power P lies along the horizontal, reactive power Q is vertical, and apparent power S is the hypotenuse at angle θ.}}

## Complex power

All three quantities can be bundled into a single complex number, the **complex power**:

$$ <b>S</b> = <b>V I</b>* = <i>P</i> + j<i>Q</i>  || 11.4

where **V** and **I** are RMS phasors and **I*** is the complex conjugate of the current. Its magnitude is the apparent power and its angle is θ. For a load of impedance **Z** = *R* + j*X* carrying RMS current *I*, the complex power is simply *I*<sup>2</sup>**Z**, so *P* = *I*<sup>2</sup>*R* and *Q* = *I*<sup>2</sup>*X*. Only the resistance absorbs real power; only the reactance absorbs reactive power.

Complex power has one very convenient property: it is **conserved**. The total complex power delivered by the sources in a circuit equals the sum of the complex powers absorbed by all the loads. So real powers add, and reactive powers add, but apparent powers do *not* add (unless all the loads happen to have the same power factor). To combine loads, add the *P*s and the *Q*s separately, then find *S* from Pythagoras.

:::example A single load
A load of impedance 8 + j6 Ω is connected to a 230 V supply. Find *P*, *Q*, *S* and the power factor.

|**Z**| = 10 Ω, so *I* = 230/10 = 23 A.

*P* = *I*<sup>2</sup>*R* = 23<sup>2</sup> × 8 = 4232 W. *Q* = *I*<sup>2</sup>*X* = 23<sup>2</sup> × 6 = 3174 var. *S* = 230 × 23 = 5290 VA.

pf = 4232/5290 = 0.8 lagging, which is also *R*/|**Z**| = 8/10.
:::

## RMS values of other waveforms

The RMS concept applies to any periodic waveform: square it, find the mean over one period, take the square root. Some useful results:

| Waveform (peak <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>) | RMS value |
|---|---|
| Sinusoid | <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>/√2 |
| Symmetrical square wave | <i>V</i><sub>m</sub> |
| Symmetrical triangle or sawtooth | <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>/√3 |
| Half-wave rectified sinusoid | <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>/2 |
| Full-wave rectified sinusoid | <i>V</i><sub>m</sub>/√2 |

When a waveform is the sum of a DC component and sinusoids of *different* frequencies, the squares of the RMS values add: *V*<sub>rms</sub><sup>2</sup> = *V*<sub>dc</sub><sup>2</sup> + *V*<sub>1,rms</sub><sup>2</sup> + *V*<sub>2,rms</sub><sup>2</sup> + … This is because the cross terms average to zero. It is not true for two sinusoids of the same frequency, which must be combined as phasors first.

:::pitfall
Many inexpensive multimeters measure the rectified average of a waveform and multiply by 1.111, the ratio that applies to a sinusoid. On a square wave, triangle or distorted mains waveform they read wrongly, sometimes by a large margin. Meters described as "true RMS" compute the genuine value.
:::

## Power-factor correction

Most industrial loads are inductive, dominated by induction motors, so they have a lagging power factor. Since a capacitor draws *negative* reactive power, connecting capacitors in parallel with the load cancels some or all of the load's reactive power. The real power is unchanged, as the capacitor absorbs none, but the supply current falls.

To raise the power factor of a load *P* from cos θ<sub>1</sub> to cos θ<sub>2</sub>, the capacitor must supply

$$ <i>Q</i><sub>C</sub> = <i>P</i>(tan θ<sub>1</sub> − tan θ<sub>2</sub>)  || 11.5

and since a capacitor across voltage *V* takes reactive power *V*<sup>2</sup>ω*C*,

$$ <i>C</i> = <i>Q</i><sub>C</sub>/(ω<i>V</i><sup>2</sup>)  || 11.6

:::example Correcting a workshop load
A 10 kW load runs at 0.7 lagging from a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Find the capacitance needed to raise the power factor to 0.95 lagging, and the reduction in supply current.

tan θ<sub>1</sub> = tan(cos<sup>−1</sup> 0.7) = 1.020 and tan θ<sub>2</sub> = tan(cos<sup>−1</sup> 0.95) = 0.329.

*Q*<sub>C</sub> = 10 000 × (1.020 − 0.329) = 6915 var.

*C* = 6915/(2π × 50 × 230<sup>2</sup>) = 416 μF.

The current falls from 10 000/(230 × 0.7) = 62.1 A to 10 000/(230 × 0.95) = 45.8 A, a reduction of 26 %, with no change in the useful power delivered.
:::

Why not correct all the way to unity? Partly cost, since the last few per cent of improvement needs a disproportionate amount of capacitance. Partly risk: as loads switch off, an over-sized capacitor bank can leave the installation with a leading power factor and raised voltages. In practice, targets of 0.95 to 0.98 are common, often with automatically switched capacitor stages.

## Maximum power transfer in AC circuits

For a source with Thévenin impedance **Z**<sub>Th</sub> = *R*<sub>Th</sub> + j*X*<sub>Th</sub>, the load that absorbs the most average power is the **complex conjugate**:

$$ <b>Z</b><sub>L</sub> = <b>Z</b><sub>Th</sub>* = <i>R</i><sub>Th</sub> − j<i>X</i><sub>Th</sub>  || 11.7

The load reactance cancels the source reactance, leaving a purely resistive loop, and *P*<sub>max</sub> = *V*<sub>Th,rms</sub><sup>2</sup>/(4*R*<sub>Th</sub>). If the load is restricted to a pure resistance, the best choice is *R*<sub>L</sub> = |**Z**<sub>Th</sub>|, which delivers less power than the conjugate match. Conjugate matching is fundamental in radio-frequency engineering, where antennas, amplifiers and transmission lines must be matched to transfer weak signals efficiently.

:::tip
If a question gives powers in kW and power factors, work entirely with *P* and *Q* and avoid impedances altogether. If it gives impedances, work with currents and impedances. Mixing the two approaches midway through a problem is the source of most errors in this topic.
:::

## Energy and billing

Electricity meters for homes and small businesses measure real energy in kWh, so a poor power factor does not directly increase a domestic bill. Large consumers are different. Suppliers and network operators commonly charge them for maximum demand in kVA, or apply a reactive-power charge, precisely because poor power factor imposes real costs on the network. For such customers, power-factor correction capacitors usually pay for themselves within a year or two.

:::example Combining several loads
A 230 V single-phase supply feeds three loads: a 3 kW heater (unity power factor), a 2 kW motor at 0.8 lagging and 1 kW of lighting at 0.9 lagging. Find the total power, the overall power factor and the supply current.

*Reactive power of each load.* Heater: 0 var. Motor: 2000 × tan(cos<sup>−1</sup> 0.8) = 2000 × 0.75 = 1500 var. Lighting: 1000 × tan(cos<sup>−1</sup> 0.9) = 1000 × 0.484 = 484 var.

*Totals.* *P* = 3000 + 2000 + 1000 = 6000 W. *Q* = 0 + 1500 + 484 = 1984 var.

*Apparent power and power factor.* *S* = √(6000<sup>2</sup> + 1984<sup>2</sup>) = 6320 VA, so pf = 6000/6320 = 0.949 lagging.

*Supply current.* *I* = 6320/230 = 27.5 A.

Had we simply added the apparent powers of the loads (3000 + 2500 + 1111 = 6611 VA) we would have overestimated the current at 28.7 A. Only complex powers add.
:::

## Check your understanding

**1. What does a wattmeter measure?** Real (average) power, *P*.

**2. A load draws 10 A at 230 V with pf 0.6 lagging. What are *P*, *Q* and *S*?** *S* = 2300 VA, *P* = 1380 W, *Q* = 1840 var.

**3. Why are transformers rated in kVA rather than kW?** Because their heating depends on current and voltage, not on the power factor of the load.

**4. Does connecting power-factor correction capacitors change the real power drawn by a motor?** No. It reduces only the reactive power and the supply current.

**5. What is the RMS value of 5 + 10 sin ω*t* V?** √(25 + 50) = 8.66 V.

**6. Why is complete correction to unity power factor rarely attempted?** The last few per cent need disproportionately large capacitance, and a bank sized for full load can push the installation into a leading power factor when the load falls.

**7. What load impedance extracts the most power from a source of impedance 6 + j8 Ω?** Its conjugate, 6 − j8 Ω.

:::summary
- Instantaneous AC power oscillates at twice the supply frequency; its average is *P* = *V*<sub>rms</sub>*I*<sub>rms</sub> cos θ.
- Power factor cos θ: lagging for inductive loads, leading for capacitive.
- *P* (W), *Q* (var) and *S* (VA) form the power triangle, *S*<sup>2</sup> = *P*<sup>2</sup> + *Q*<sup>2</sup>.
- Complex power **S** = **VI*** = *P* + j*Q* is conserved; real and reactive powers add separately.
- RMS values: sine *V*<sub>m</sub>/√2, square *V*<sub>m</sub>, triangle *V*<sub>m</sub>/√3, half-wave *V*<sub>m</sub>/2.
- Power-factor correction: *Q*<sub>C</sub> = *P*(tan θ<sub>1</sub> − tan θ<sub>2</sub>), *C* = *Q*<sub>C</sub>/(ω*V*<sup>2</sup>).
- AC maximum power transfer requires **Z**<sub>L</sub> = **Z**<sub>Th</sub>*.
:::

Most real circuits are not single loops. Even a simple torch has a battery's internal resistance, contact resistance and the lamp itself; a car's lighting system has dozens of lamps in parallel; and the input stage of almost any electronic instrument is a careful arrangement of resistors in series and in parallel. Before we develop the heavy machinery of nodal and mesh analysis in Part II, it pays to become quick and confident at simplifying networks by hand. Often you will not need the heavy machinery at all.

This chapter is about seeing structure. The arithmetic of series and parallel resistors is easy. The skill that takes practice is recognising which elements really are in series or in parallel when the diagram has been drawn awkwardly, and knowing what to do when they are neither.

:::objectives
- Combine resistors in series and in parallel, and reduce series–parallel networks to a single equivalent.
- Apply the voltage-divider and current-divider rules, and understand the effect of loading.
- Convert between delta (Δ) and star (Y) networks.
- Analyse balanced and unbalanced Wheatstone bridges.
- Design simple ammeter shunts and voltmeter multipliers.
:::

## Series connection

Two elements are in **series** when they carry exactly the same current. In practice this means they share a single node and nothing else is connected to that node. The equivalent resistance of resistors in series is simply their sum:

$$ <i>R</i><sub>eq</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>R</i><sub>2</sub> + … + <i>R</i><sub>n</sub>  || 2.1

This follows directly from Kirchhoff's voltage law, which we shall meet formally in the next chapter: the source voltage must equal the sum of the drops, and each drop is *IR*<sub>k</sub> with the same *I*.

{{fig:t02_series|Three resistors in series across a 24 V source. The same current flows through each, and the voltage divides in proportion to resistance.}}

For the circuit of Figure 2.1, *R*<sub>eq</sub> = 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 kΩ, so *I* = 24/12 = 2 mA. The individual drops are 4 V, 8 V and 12 V, which add to 24 V as they must. Notice the pattern: the largest resistor takes the largest share of the voltage. In a series circuit, voltage divides in direct proportion to resistance.

## Parallel connection

Elements are in **parallel** when they are connected between the same pair of nodes, so they share the same voltage. For resistors in parallel it is the conductances that add:

$$ 1/<i>R</i><sub>eq</sub> = 1/<i>R</i><sub>1</sub> + 1/<i>R</i><sub>2</sub> + … + 1/<i>R</i><sub>n</sub>  || 2.2

For exactly two resistors this rearranges into the "product over sum" formula, which is quicker on paper:

$$ <i>R</i><sub>1</sub> ∥ <i>R</i><sub>2</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>1</sub><i>R</i><sub>2</sub>/(<i>R</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>)  || 2.3

Use it for pairs only. Applying "product over sum" to three resistors at once is one of the most common algebra errors in the subject; combine two first, then combine the result with the third.

{{fig:t02_parallel|Three resistors in parallel. Every branch sees the full 12 V, and the branch currents add to give the supply current.}}

For Figure 2.2, 1/*R*<sub>eq</sub> = 1/6 + 1/12 + 1/4 = 6/12, so *R*<sub>eq</sub> = 2 Ω. The branch currents are 2 A, 1 A and 3 A, totalling 6 A, which agrees with 12 V ÷ 2 Ω. Two quick checks are worth making a habit of. The equivalent of a parallel group is always *smaller* than its smallest member. And *n* equal resistors in parallel give exactly 1/*n* of one of them.

:::key
Series: same current, voltages add, resistances add.
Parallel: same voltage, currents add, conductances add.
:::

## Series–parallel networks

Most practical networks mix the two. The method is always the same: find a group of elements that are unambiguously in series or in parallel, replace them with their equivalent, redraw, and repeat. With ladder networks it is usually easiest to start at the end furthest from the source and work back towards it.

Once you have the total current, you reverse the process, expanding the network again step by step and using Ohm's law and the divider rules to find whatever voltage or current was asked for. Students sometimes find this second half harder than the first. The secret is to keep the intermediate diagrams: every simplified drawing you made on the way in is a map for the way back out.

:::pitfall
Two resistors that look parallel on the page are not necessarily in parallel. They must connect to the *same two nodes*. If there is another element between their ends — even a single resistor in one of the connecting wires — they are not in parallel, however neat the drawing looks.
:::

## The voltage divider

A pair of resistors in series across a voltage is a **voltage divider**, the most widely used sub-circuit in electronics:

$$ <i>V</i><sub>out</sub> = <i>V</i><sub>in</sub> <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>/(<i>R</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>)  || 2.4

where *V*<sub>out</sub> is taken across *R*<sub>2</sub>. It sets transistor bias points, scales sensor outputs into the range of an analogue-to-digital converter, and forms the basis of every potentiometer.

{{fig:t02_divider|A voltage divider. With no load connected, the output is the fraction R₂/(R₁ + R₂) of the input.}}

### Loading

Equation 2.4 assumes that nothing draws current from the output. As soon as a load is connected, it appears in parallel with *R*<sub>2</sub>, the lower leg of the divider becomes smaller, and the output falls. This is **loading**, and it is one of the most practically important ideas in the chapter.

:::example A divider under load
The divider of Figure 2.3 uses *R*<sub>1</sub> = 10 kΩ and *R*<sub>2</sub> = 4.7 kΩ from a 12 V supply. Find the output unloaded, and with a 10 kΩ load connected.

*Unloaded.* *V*<sub>out</sub> = 12 × 4.7/14.7 = 3.84 V.

*Loaded.* The lower leg becomes 4.7 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 3.20 kΩ, so *V*<sub>out</sub> = 12 × 3.20/(10 + 3.20) = 2.91 V.

The load has pulled the output down by about 24 %. A designer who wanted 3.84 V at the load would either use much smaller divider resistors (wasting more standing current) or, better, buffer the output with an amplifier. As a rule of thumb, a divider holds its ratio to within about 10 % only if the load is at least ten times larger than *R*<sub>2</sub>.
:::

## The current divider

The dual of the voltage divider is the **current divider**. When a current *I* splits between two parallel resistors, the current in *R*<sub>1</sub> is

$$ <i>I</i><sub>1</sub> = <i>I</i> <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>/(<i>R</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>)  || 2.5

Notice that the *other* resistor appears on top. This catches people out constantly. The logic is that current prefers the easier path: if *R*<sub>2</sub> is large, more current is pushed through *R*<sub>1</sub>. A quick check with extreme values helps. If *R*<sub>2</sub> were infinite (an open circuit) all the current would go through *R*<sub>1</sub>, and the formula correctly gives *I*<sub>1</sub> = *I*.

## When elements are neither in series nor in parallel

Some networks cannot be reduced by series and parallel combination alone. The classic example is the bridge, in which five resistors connect four nodes in such a way that no two are in series and no two are in parallel. There are three ways forward. You can use the systematic methods of Part II, which always work. You can use Thévenin's theorem (Chapter 6). Or you can use a **delta–star transformation**, which converts three resistors connected in a triangle into three connected to a common centre point, or vice versa, without changing anything seen from the outside.

### Delta to star

For a delta with resistors *R*<sub>ab</sub>, *R*<sub>bc</sub> and *R*<sub>ca</sub>, the equivalent star has arms

$$ <i>R</i><sub>a</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>ab</sub><i>R</i><sub>ca</sub>/Σ, <i>R</i><sub>b</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>ab</sub><i>R</i><sub>bc</sub>/Σ, <i>R</i><sub>c</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>bc</sub><i>R</i><sub>ca</sub>/Σ  || 2.6

where Σ = *R*<sub>ab</sub> + *R*<sub>bc</sub> + *R*<sub>ca</sub>. In words: each star arm is the product of the two delta resistors that touch its terminal, divided by the sum of all three.

### Star to delta

The reverse transformation is

$$ <i>R</i><sub>ab</sub> = <i>P</i>/<i>R</i><sub>c</sub>, <i>R</i><sub>bc</sub> = <i>P</i>/<i>R</i><sub>a</sub>, <i>R</i><sub>ca</sub> = <i>P</i>/<i>R</i><sub>b</sub>  || 2.7

where *P* = *R*<sub>a</sub>*R*<sub>b</sub> + *R*<sub>b</sub>*R*<sub>c</sub> + *R*<sub>c</sub>*R*<sub>a</sub>. Each delta resistor is *P* divided by the star arm on the *opposite* terminal.

:::example Delta to star
Convert a delta with *R*<sub>ab</sub> = 30 Ω, *R*<sub>bc</sub> = 60 Ω and *R*<sub>ca</sub> = 90 Ω into a star.

Σ = 180 Ω. Then *R*<sub>a</sub> = 30 × 90/180 = 15 Ω, *R*<sub>b</sub> = 30 × 60/180 = 10 Ω and *R*<sub>c</sub> = 60 × 90/180 = 30 Ω.

*Check.* With terminal c left open, the delta presents 30 ∥ (60 + 90) = 30 ∥ 150 = 25 Ω between a and b. The star presents *R*<sub>a</sub> + *R*<sub>b</sub> = 25 Ω. They agree, as they must.
:::

You do not need to memorise both sets of formulas if you remember the checking method: the resistance between any two terminals, with the third open, must be the same in both networks. Writing that condition for all three pairs gives three equations from which either set of formulas can be derived. When the three resistors are equal the result is pleasingly simple: *R*<sub>Y</sub> = *R*<sub>Δ</sub>/3. We shall use exactly this result for balanced three-phase loads in Chapter 12.

## The Wheatstone bridge

The **Wheatstone bridge** (Figure 2.4) is two voltage dividers side by side, sharing a supply, with a sensitive detector — traditionally a galvanometer — connected between their midpoints. When the two dividers produce the same output voltage, no current flows in the detector and the bridge is said to be **balanced**.

{{fig:t02_bridge|The Wheatstone bridge. At balance the galvanometer G carries no current, and R₁/R₃ = R₂/Rₓ.}}

At balance the two midpoints are at the same potential, so

$$ <i>R</i><sub>1</sub>/<i>R</i><sub>3</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>2</sub>/<i>R</i><sub>x</sub>,  so  <i>R</i><sub>x</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>2</sub><i>R</i><sub>3</sub>/<i>R</i><sub>1</sub>  || 2.8

The beauty of the bridge is that the supply voltage does not appear in the result, and nor does the detector's resistance. Accuracy depends only on the reference resistors and on how sensitively you can detect zero current, which is why bridge methods were the standard for precision resistance measurement for well over a century. Strain gauges, platinum resistance thermometers and many pressure sensors are still read using bridge circuits today.

When the bridge is *not* balanced, the open-circuit voltage between the midpoints is simply the difference between the two divider outputs. Finding the current that flows when a detector of known resistance is connected is a classic examination problem; it is easiest with Thévenin's theorem, and we shall return to it in Chapter 6.

## Meters: shunts and multipliers

A traditional moving-coil meter movement is a sensitive ammeter: perhaps 1 mA for full-scale deflection, with a coil resistance of a hundred ohms or so. Series and parallel resistors turn it into a useful instrument.

To measure larger currents, a low-value **shunt** is placed in parallel with the movement so that most of the current bypasses it. Because the shunt and coil share the same voltage at full scale, *R*<sub>sh</sub> = *I*<sub>fs</sub>*R*<sub>m</sub>/(*I* − *I*<sub>fs</sub>). To measure voltage, a high-value **multiplier** is placed in series, chosen so that the full-scale voltage drives exactly the full-scale current: *R*<sub>se</sub> = *V*/*I*<sub>fs</sub> − *R*<sub>m</sub>. The quantity 1/*I*<sub>fs</sub>, quoted in ohms per volt, is the voltmeter's **sensitivity**, and it tells you how much the meter will load the circuit under test. Digital meters have largely replaced moving-coil instruments, but the same ideas govern their input stages, and the loading question never goes away.

:::tip
In bridge and divider problems, write the potential of every node relative to one reference point, usually the negative supply rail. Then any voltage the question asks for is just a subtraction. This approach is quicker and far less error-prone than trying to reason about voltages "across" things.
:::

## Working strategy

When you meet a new network in the problems that follow, try this routine:

1. Label every node with a letter. Nodes connected by plain wire are the same node and get the same letter.
2. Look for elements between the same pair of letters: they are in parallel.
3. Look for nodes with only two elements attached: those elements are in series.
4. Combine, redraw and repeat.
5. If you get stuck with no series or parallel groups left, look for a delta or a star to transform, or move on to the methods of Part II.

Above all, redraw. A messy diagram hides structure; a clean one reveals it.

:::example Reducing a ladder network
A 20 V source feeds a ladder: 2 kΩ in series, then 6 kΩ to the return rail, then a further 1 kΩ in series, and finally 2 kΩ to the return rail. Find the supply current and the voltage across the final 2 kΩ resistor.

*Start at the far end.* The last series resistor and the final shunt carry the same current, so they combine to 1 + 2 = 3 kΩ.

*Combine with the 6 kΩ shunt.* 6 ∥ 3 = 18/9 = 2 kΩ.

*Add the first series resistor.* *R*<sub>eq</sub> = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ, so the supply current is 20/4 = 5 mA.

*Work back out.* The voltage at the junction after the first resistor is 5 mA × 2 kΩ = 10 V, the drop across the 2 kΩ equivalent. That 10 V then divides between 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ, giving 10 × 2/3 = 6.67 V across the final resistor.

*Check.* The current in the last branch is 10/3 = 3.33 mA and the current in the 6 kΩ shunt is 10/6 = 1.67 mA. Together they make 5 mA, the supply current.
:::

## Check your understanding

**1. Three 12 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. What is the equivalent?** 4 Ω: *n* equal resistors in parallel give 1/*n* of one.

**2. In a two-resistor current divider, which resistor carries more current?** The smaller one. Current prefers the easier path.

**3. A voltage divider gives 5 V unloaded. A load equal to the lower resistor is connected. Does the output rise or fall?** It falls, because the lower leg is now smaller.

**4. Does a Wheatstone bridge's balance condition depend on the supply voltage?** No. Only resistance ratios appear, which is the source of its accuracy.

**5. Three equal 30 Ω resistors in delta are converted to star. What is each star arm?** 10 Ω, since *R*<sub>Y</sub> = *R*<sub>Δ</sub>/3.

:::summary
- Series elements carry the same current; their resistances add and the voltage divides in proportion to resistance.
- Parallel elements share the same voltage; their conductances add and the current divides in inverse proportion to resistance.
- The voltage-divider rule assumes no load; a load in parallel with the lower leg reduces the output.
- In the two-resistor current divider, the opposite resistor goes on top.
- Delta–star transformations unlock networks that cannot be reduced by series and parallel combination alone; for equal resistors, *R*<sub>Y</sub> = *R*<sub>Δ</sub>/3.
- A Wheatstone bridge balances when *R*<sub>1</sub>/*R*<sub>3</sub> = *R*<sub>2</sub>/*R*<sub>x</sub>, independently of supply voltage.
- Shunts extend ammeter ranges; multipliers turn a movement into a voltmeter.
:::

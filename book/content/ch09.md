Put a capacitor and an inductor in the same circuit and something new happens. Energy can now slosh back and forth between the electric field of the capacitor and the magnetic field of the inductor, much as a pendulum trades potential energy for kinetic energy and back again. If the resistance is small, the circuit rings: voltages and currents oscillate as they die away. If the resistance is large, the oscillation is smothered and the response creeps towards its final value without overshooting.

These second-order circuits appear everywhere. The LC tank in a radio, the output filter of a switch-mode power supply, the parasitic inductance and capacitance that make a fast digital edge ring, a long cable driven by a pulse: all behave in the way this chapter describes. The mathematics is a step up from Chapter 8, but it follows a recipe, and once you have worked a few problems the recipe becomes automatic.

:::objectives
- Derive the characteristic equation of series and parallel RLC circuits.
- Calculate the neper frequency α, the undamped natural frequency ω<sub>0</sub>, the damping ratio ζ and the damped frequency ω<sub>d</sub>.
- Classify a response as overdamped, critically damped or underdamped.
- Find the complete natural response from initial conditions in each case.
- Find the step response of a series RLC circuit, including peak time and overshoot.
- Design simple RLC circuits for a given natural frequency and damping.
:::

## The series RLC circuit

Consider a capacitor charged to *V*<sub>0</sub>, connected at *t* = 0 to a series resistor and inductor (Figure 9.1). One current *i* flows round the loop. KVL gives

$$ <i>L</i> d<i>i</i>/d<i>t</i> + <i>Ri</i> + <i>v</i><sub>C</sub> = 0  || 9.1

and since *i* = *C* d*v*<sub>C</sub>/d*t*, this becomes a second-order equation in the capacitor voltage:

$$ d<sup>2</sup><i>v</i>/d<i>t</i><sup>2</sup> + (<i>R</i>/<i>L</i>) d<i>v</i>/d<i>t</i> + <i>v</i>/(<i>LC</i>) = 0  || 9.2

{{fig:t09_series|A source-free series RLC circuit. The capacitor is charged to V₀ before the switch closes.}}

Trying a solution of the form *v* = *A*e<sup><i>st</i></sup> turns the differential equation into an algebraic one, the **characteristic equation**:

$$ <i>s</i><sup>2</sup> + 2α<i>s</i> + ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0  || 9.3

with two key parameters:

$$ α = <i>R</i>/(2<i>L</i>),  ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(<i>LC</i>)  || 9.4

α is the **neper frequency** or damping coefficient, in s<sup>−1</sup>, and ω<sub>0</sub> is the **undamped natural frequency** (also called the resonant frequency), in rad/s. Their ratio, ζ = α/ω<sub>0</sub>, is the **damping ratio**, a pure number that tells you the character of the response at a glance.

The roots of Equation 9.3 are

$$ <i>s</i><sub>1,2</sub> = −α ± √(α<sup>2</sup> − ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup>)  || 9.5

and everything depends on the sign of the quantity under the square root.

## The parallel RLC circuit

For a resistor, inductor and capacitor all in parallel (Figure 9.2), KCL at the top node leads to exactly the same characteristic equation, with the same ω<sub>0</sub> but a different damping coefficient:

$$ α = 1/(2<i>RC</i>)  || 9.6

{{fig:t09_parallel|A source-free parallel RLC circuit.}}

Note the inversion. In a series circuit, *more* resistance means more damping, because the resistor impedes the current that carries energy between *L* and *C*. In a parallel circuit, *more* resistance means *less* damping, because a large parallel resistor draws little current and so removes little energy. An infinite parallel resistance, an open circuit, gives a lossless LC circuit that oscillates for ever.

## Three kinds of response

### Overdamped: α > ω<sub>0</sub> (ζ > 1)

The roots are real, negative and distinct, and the response is the sum of two decaying exponentials:

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = <i>A</i><sub>1</sub>e<sup><i>s</i><sub>1</sub><i>t</i></sup> + <i>A</i><sub>2</sub>e<sup><i>s</i><sub>2</sub><i>t</i></sup>  || 9.7

There is no oscillation. The response is sluggish: the slower exponential (the root closer to zero) dominates the tail and can take a long time to die away.

### Critically damped: α = ω<sub>0</sub> (ζ = 1)

The roots are equal, *s* = −α, and the response takes the form

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = (<i>D</i><sub>1</sub> + <i>D</i><sub>2</sub><i>t</i>)e<sup>−α<i>t</i></sup>  || 9.8

This is the boundary case. It gives the fastest possible return to the final value *without* overshoot, which is why it is the target for things like meter movements and some control systems.

### Underdamped: α < ω<sub>0</sub> (ζ < 1)

The roots are complex conjugates, −α ± jω<sub>d</sub>, where

$$ ω<sub>d</sub> = √(ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup> − α<sup>2</sup>)  || 9.9

is the **damped natural frequency**. The response is a decaying oscillation:

$$ <i>v</i>(<i>t</i>) = e<sup>−α<i>t</i></sup>(<i>B</i><sub>1</sub> cos ω<sub>d</sub><i>t</i> + <i>B</i><sub>2</sub> sin ω<sub>d</sub><i>t</i>)  || 9.10

The envelope e<sup>−α<i>t</i></sup> sets how quickly the ringing dies; ω<sub>d</sub> sets how fast it oscillates. With light damping ω<sub>d</sub> is very close to ω<sub>0</sub>.

{{fig:t09_damping|Step responses of a second-order circuit for three damping ratios. Light damping gives a fast rise with overshoot and ringing; heavy damping gives a slow, smooth approach.}}

## Finding the constants

Each form contains two unknown constants, so we need two initial conditions. For the source-free series circuit these are normally:

1. The capacitor voltage, *v*<sub>C</sub>(0<sup>+</sup>) = *V*<sub>0</sub> (continuity of capacitor voltage).
2. The inductor current, *i*(0<sup>+</sup>) = *I*<sub>0</sub> (continuity of inductor current), which gives the initial *slope* of the capacitor voltage through d*v*<sub>C</sub>/d*t* = *i*/*C*.

The second condition is the one people forget. You need d*v*/d*t* at *t* = 0<sup>+</sup>, and the only way to get it is through the capacitor current, which equals the inductor current in a series loop. Watch the sign: if *i* is defined as flowing *into* the capacitor's + terminal, then d*v*/d*t* = *i*/*C*; if the current flows out, as it does when the capacitor discharges, d*v*/d*t* = −*i*/*C*.

:::example One circuit, three dampings
A series RLC circuit has *L* = 0.1 H and *C* = 10 μF. The capacitor starts at 10 V with zero inductor current. Find the response for *R* = 40 Ω, 200 Ω and 300 Ω.

ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(0.1 × 10<sup>−5</sup>) = 1000 rad/s throughout.

*R = 40 Ω.* α = 40/0.2 = 200 s<sup>−1</sup> < ω<sub>0</sub>: underdamped. ω<sub>d</sub> = √(10<sup>6</sup> − 4 × 10<sup>4</sup>) = 980 rad/s. From *v*(0) = 10, *B*<sub>1</sub> = 10. From d*v*/d*t*(0) = 0: −α*B*<sub>1</sub> + ω<sub>d</sub>*B*<sub>2</sub> = 0, so *B*<sub>2</sub> = 200 × 10/980 = 2.04. Hence *v*(*t*) = e<sup>−200<i>t</i></sup>(10 cos 980*t* + 2.04 sin 980*t*) V.

*R = 200 Ω.* α = 1000 = ω<sub>0</sub>: critically damped. *D*<sub>1</sub> = 10 and *D*<sub>2</sub> = α*D*<sub>1</sub> = 10<sup>4</sup>, so *v*(*t*) = (10 + 10<sup>4</sup>*t*)e<sup>−1000<i>t</i></sup> V.

*R = 300 Ω.* α = 1500 > ω<sub>0</sub>: overdamped. *s*<sub>1,2</sub> = −1500 ± √(2.25 × 10<sup>6</sup> − 10<sup>6</sup>) = −382 and −2618 s<sup>−1</sup>. From *A*<sub>1</sub> + *A*<sub>2</sub> = 10 and *s*<sub>1</sub>*A*<sub>1</sub> + *s*<sub>2</sub>*A*<sub>2</sub> = 0, *A*<sub>1</sub> = 11.7 V and *A*<sub>2</sub> = −1.71 V, so *v*(*t*) = 11.7e<sup>−382<i>t</i></sup> − 1.71e<sup>−2618<i>t</i></sup> V.

Notice that the overdamped response, despite its larger α, is dominated by the slow root at −382 s<sup>−1</sup>. It takes considerably longer to settle than the critically damped case, even though it has more damping.
:::

## The step response

If a DC source *V*<sub>s</sub> is switched into a series RLC circuit, the capacitor voltage eventually settles to *V*<sub>s</sub> (in DC steady state the capacitor is open, so no current flows and no voltage is dropped across *R* or *L*). The complete response is this final value plus a natural response of exactly the forms above:

$$ <i>v</i><sub>C</sub>(<i>t</i>) = <i>V</i><sub>s</sub> + (natural response)  || 9.11

The constants are found from the initial conditions in the same way, applied to the complete expression. For an initially de-energised, underdamped circuit the result is

$$ <i>v</i><sub>C</sub>(<i>t</i>) = <i>V</i><sub>s</sub> − <i>V</i><sub>s</sub>e<sup>−α<i>t</i></sup>(cos ω<sub>d</sub><i>t</i> + (α/ω<sub>d</sub>) sin ω<sub>d</sub><i>t</i>)  || 9.12

Two features of this response matter in practice. The first peak occurs at *t*<sub>p</sub> = π/ω<sub>d</sub>, and the fractional **overshoot** is e<sup>−απ/ω<sub>d</sub></sup>. With the 40 Ω circuit from the example, the overshoot would be e<sup>−200π/980</sup> = 0.53, so a 10 V step would carry the capacitor to almost 15.3 V before it settled. Overshoot like that can damage components, mis-trigger logic and upset control loops. Choosing the damping is often the central design decision.

:::key
For any second-order circuit, compute α and ω<sub>0</sub> first. Their comparison tells you which of the three solution forms to use before you do any more work. Then: final value from the DC steady state, two constants from the two initial conditions.
:::

## Energy and the lossless limit

With *R* = 0 in a series circuit (or *R* → ∞ in a parallel one), α = 0 and the circuit oscillates indefinitely at ω<sub>0</sub>. The total stored energy ½*Cv*<sup>2</sup> + ½*Li*<sup>2</sup> stays constant, swapping completely between capacitor and inductor twice every cycle. This gives a quick way to find the peak values: when all the energy is in the capacitor, *V*<sub>max</sub> = √(2*W*/*C*); when it is all in the inductor, *I*<sub>max</sub> = √(2*W*/*L*). Real circuits always have some loss, but in a good radio-frequency tank circuit the loss per cycle can be well under 1 %.

## Designing for damping

Because ζ depends on all three components, a designer can choose them to meet a specification. For a series circuit:

$$ ζ = (<i>R</i>/2)√(<i>C</i>/<i>L</i>)  || 9.13

so critical damping requires *R* = 2√(*L*/*C*). For a parallel circuit, ζ = (1/2*R*)√(*L*/*C*), and critical damping requires *R* = ½√(*L*/*C*). A damping ratio of about 0.7 is a popular compromise in filters and control systems: it gives a quick response with only about 5 % overshoot.

:::pitfall
When you design a series RLC circuit, the resistance in the formulas is the *total* series resistance, including the inductor's own winding resistance and the source resistance. In low-damping designs these "hidden" resistances can be larger than the resistor you deliberately added.
:::

## A practical example of ringing

Look at a fast digital signal on a long ribbon cable with an oscilloscope and you will often see the edges overshoot and ring. The cable's inductance and the input capacitance of the receiving chip form a series RLC circuit, with only the small driver resistance to damp it. The fix that engineers use, a small series resistor of a few tens of ohms at the driver, is simply raising *R* towards critical damping. You now know exactly why it works.

:::tip
In examinations, state the classification explicitly ("α = 200 s<sup>−1</sup> is less than ω<sub>0</sub> = 1000 rad/s, so the response is underdamped") before writing the solution form. If you choose the wrong form, an examiner can still award the marks for α and ω<sub>0</sub>, but only if you have shown them.
:::

:::summary
- Characteristic equation: *s*<sup>2</sup> + 2α*s* + ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0 with ω<sub>0</sub> = 1/√(*LC*).
- Series RLC: α = *R*/2*L*. Parallel RLC: α = 1/2*RC*. Damping ratio ζ = α/ω<sub>0</sub>.
- Overdamped (α > ω<sub>0</sub>): two real exponentials. Critically damped (α = ω<sub>0</sub>): (*D*<sub>1</sub> + *D*<sub>2</sub>*t*)e<sup>−α<i>t</i></sup>. Underdamped (α < ω<sub>0</sub>): decaying sinusoid at ω<sub>d</sub> = √(ω<sub>0</sub><sup>2</sup> − α<sup>2</sup>).
- Two initial conditions are needed: capacitor voltage, and inductor current (which gives the initial slope).
- Step response = final value + natural response. Underdamped peak at π/ω<sub>d</sub>; overshoot e<sup>−απ/ω<sub>d</sub></sup>.
- Critical damping: *R* = 2√(*L*/*C*) for series, *R* = ½√(*L*/*C*) for parallel.
:::

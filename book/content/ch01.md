I once watched a second-year student spend forty minutes on a nodal analysis problem, filling two sides of A4 with beautifully organised matrices, only to get an answer that was out by a factor of a thousand. The mathematics was flawless. The mistake was on the first line: a resistance written in kilohms had been treated as ohms. Nothing in the rest of the working could rescue it.

I tell that story because this first chapter can look like the easy part, the bit you skim on the way to the "real" material. Please don't skim it. Charge, current, voltage and power are the vocabulary of everything that follows, and the habits you form here — writing units, checking orders of magnitude, being fussy about signs — are the habits that separate the student who usually gets the right answer from the one who always does.

:::objectives
- Define charge, current, voltage, resistance, power and energy, and use their SI units without hesitation.
- Apply Ohm's law in all three of its arrangements and recognise when it does not apply.
- Use the passive sign convention to decide whether an element absorbs or delivers power.
- Calculate energy consumption and running costs in kilowatt-hours.
- Predict how wire dimensions, material and temperature affect resistance.
:::

## Charge and current

Everything electrical begins with **charge**. The coulomb (C) is the unit, and a single electron carries −1.602 × 10<sup>−19</sup> C. That number is so tiny that one coulomb represents about 6.24 × 10<sup>18</sup> electrons, more than the number of grains of sand on a decent-sized beach. We almost never count electrons individually; we let the coulomb do the bookkeeping.

**Current** is the rate at which charge flows past a point:

$$ <i>i</i> = d<i>q</i>/d<i>t</i>  || 1.1

For a steady (DC) current this simplifies to *I* = *Q*/*t*, which is the form you will use most of the time. One ampere is one coulomb per second. A phone charger delivers perhaps 2 A; an electric kettle draws about 13 A from a UK socket; the starter motor in a car can briefly demand several hundred amperes.

There is a historical wrinkle that confuses almost everybody the first time they meet it. When Benjamin Franklin and his contemporaries decided which way current "flowed", they guessed — and they guessed that positive charge moved. We now know that in metal wires it is the negatively charged electrons that drift, and they drift the opposite way. Rather than rewrite two centuries of textbooks, engineers kept the original convention. **Conventional current** flows from the positive terminal of a source, round the external circuit, and back into the negative terminal. Every equation in this book uses conventional current. The electrons can do as they please.

:::key
Current is not "used up" by a circuit. Whatever current flows into a resistor flows out of the other end. What is used up — or more accurately, converted to heat, light or motion — is energy.
:::

## Voltage

If current is how much charge is moving, **voltage** is how hard it is being pushed. More precisely, the voltage between two points is the energy gained or lost per unit charge in moving between them:

$$ <i>v</i> = d<i>w</i>/d<i>q</i>  || 1.2

One volt is one joule per coulomb. A 9 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it nine joules of energy, which the coulomb then surrenders on its way round the external circuit.

Voltage is always a difference between two points. When we say "the voltage at node A is 5 V" we always mean 5 V relative to some reference point, usually labelled ground, earth or simply 0 V. Forgetting this is the source of a surprising number of errors, especially when a circuit has more than one source. I encourage students to think of voltage as height on a hillside: the absolute height above sea level is a matter of convention, but the difference in height between two places is a physical fact that determines which way water will run.

## Resistance and Ohm's law

Most materials impede the flow of charge to some extent. The ratio of voltage across an element to the current through it is its **resistance**, measured in ohms (Ω). For a great many materials, especially metals at steady temperature, that ratio is constant. This is **Ohm's law**:

$$ <i>V</i> = <i>IR</i>  || 1.3

It can be rearranged as *I* = *V*/*R* or *R* = *V*/*I*, and you should be able to move between all three forms without thinking. The reciprocal of resistance is **conductance**, *G* = 1/*R*, measured in siemens (S). Conductance turns out to be the more natural quantity when we deal with parallel circuits and nodal analysis, so it is worth getting comfortable with it now.

### When Ohm's law does not apply

Ohm's law is not a law of nature in the way that conservation of charge is. It describes a particular kind of component, the linear resistor. A filament lamp is a good example of something that does not obey it: as the filament heats up its resistance rises sharply, so doubling the voltage does much less than double the current. Diodes, transistors and gas discharge tubes are wildly non-linear. Figure 1.2 compares a linear resistor with a lamp.

{{fig:t01_vi|Current–voltage characteristics. The resistor gives a straight line through the origin; the lamp's curve flattens as the filament heats and its resistance rises.}}

In this book every resistor is assumed linear unless stated otherwise. That assumption is what lets us use the powerful systematic methods of later chapters.

## Power and the passive sign convention

**Power** is the rate at which energy is transferred, measured in watts (W), where one watt is one joule per second. Because voltage is energy per charge and current is charge per time, their product is energy per time:

$$ <i>p</i> = <i>vi</i>  || 1.4

For a resistor we can substitute Ohm's law to obtain two further forms:

$$ <i>P</i> = <i>I</i><sup>2</sup><i>R</i> = <i>V</i><sup>2</sup>/<i>R</i>  || 1.5

Which form should you use? Whichever needs the quantities you already know. If you have current and resistance, use *I*<sup>2</sup>*R*; if you have voltage and resistance, use *V*<sup>2</sup>/*R*. All three give the same answer, so calculating it two ways is a cheap and very effective check.

Now to signs. An element can either **absorb** power (a resistor heating up, a battery being charged) or **deliver** it (a battery discharging, a generator). To keep track, engineers use the **passive sign convention**:

:::key
If the reference direction of current enters the terminal marked + on an element, then *p* = *vi* is the power **absorbed** by that element. A negative result means the element is actually **delivering** power.
:::

Resistors always absorb power, so if you ever calculate a resistor delivering power, you have a sign error somewhere. Sources may do either. A car battery delivers power when starting the engine and absorbs it when the alternator is charging it. In any complete circuit the total power delivered equals the total power absorbed. This **power balance** follows directly from conservation of energy, and checking it is one of the best ways to confirm that a solution is right.

:::example A heater element
A heating element draws 9.6 A from a 230 V supply. Find its resistance, the power it consumes and the power it would consume if the supply fell by 10 %.

*Resistance.* *R* = *V*/*I* = 230/9.6 = 23.96 Ω.

*Power.* *P* = *VI* = 230 × 9.6 = 2208 W, about 2.2 kW. As a check, *I*<sup>2</sup>*R* = 9.6<sup>2</sup> × 23.96 = 2208 W.

*Reduced supply.* At 207 V, and assuming the resistance stays constant, *P* = *V*<sup>2</sup>/*R* = 207<sup>2</sup>/23.96 = 1788 W.

Notice that a 10 % fall in voltage gave a 19 % fall in power. Power depends on the *square* of voltage, which is why brown-outs are so noticeable on heaters and incandescent lamps.
:::

## Energy and what it costs

Energy is power multiplied by time. The joule is the SI unit, but it is uncomfortably small for domestic use: a 3 kW kettle consumes 3000 joules every second. Electricity suppliers therefore bill in **kilowatt-hours** (kWh), the energy used by a one-kilowatt load running for one hour:

$$ 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> J  || 1.6

To find the cost of running an appliance, convert its power to kilowatts, multiply by the hours of use, and multiply by the unit price. The arithmetic is trivial; the traps are in the units. Watch for powers given in watts, times given in minutes, and prices given in pence when the answer is wanted in pounds.

:::example A month of showers
An electric shower rated at 9.5 kW is used for a total of 20 minutes a day. Electricity costs 26.0p per kWh. What is the cost over 30 days?

Daily energy = 9.5 kW × (20/60) h = 3.167 kWh. Over 30 days that is 95.0 kWh, costing 95.0 × 26.0p = 2470p, or £24.70.

A quick sense check: 9.5 kW is a large load (the shower needs its own 40 A or 45 A circuit), but it runs for only a third of an hour a day, so a bill of tens of pounds a month is plausible.
:::

## Resistivity: why wires have resistance

The resistance of a uniform conductor depends on its material, length and cross-sectional area:

$$ <i>R</i> = ρ<i>L</i>/<i>A</i>  || 1.7

Here ρ (rho) is the **resistivity** of the material in ohm-metres. Copper's resistivity is about 1.72 × 10<sup>−8</sup> Ω m, aluminium's about 2.8 × 10<sup>−8</sup> Ω m, and nichrome — the alloy used for heating elements precisely because it resists well — about 1.1 × 10<sup>−6</sup> Ω m, roughly sixty times worse than copper.

The equation tells you two practical things. Resistance doubles if you double the length, which is why voltage drop matters on long cable runs. And resistance falls with the *square* of diameter, because area is π*d*<sup>2</sup>/4. Going from 1.5 mm<sup>2</sup> to 2.5 mm<sup>2</sup> cable reduces resistance by 40 %, which is why socket circuits use the thicker cable.

:::pitfall
Area must be in square metres. A wire of diameter 1 mm has *A* = π(0.001)<sup>2</sup>/4 = 7.85 × 10<sup>−7</sup> m<sup>2</sup>, not 0.785. Mixing millimetres into the formula is the commonest error in resistivity problems, and it produces answers out by a factor of a million.
:::

## Temperature and resistance

The resistance of most metals rises with temperature. Over moderate ranges the rise is close to linear:

$$ <i>R</i><sub>θ</sub> = <i>R</i><sub>0</sub>[1 + α(θ − θ<sub>0</sub>)]  || 1.8

where α is the **temperature coefficient of resistance**, about 0.0039 per °C for copper referred to 20 °C. That means a copper winding gains roughly 0.4 % of its resistance for every degree it warms up. A motor winding running at 75 °C has about 21 % more resistance than it did when cold, which affects both its efficiency and the current it draws. Engineers routinely use this effect in reverse, estimating the internal temperature of a winding from a measurement of its resistance.

Some materials have negative coefficients. Carbon and most semiconductors become better conductors as they warm, which is the principle behind the NTC thermistors used for temperature sensing.

## Efficiency

No real device converts energy perfectly. **Efficiency** is the ratio of useful output power to input power:

$$ η = <i>P</i><sub>out</sub>/<i>P</i><sub>in</sub>  || 1.9

The difference, *P*<sub>in</sub> − *P*<sub>out</sub>, is lost, nearly always as heat. That lost power matters for two reasons: it costs money, and it has to be removed, otherwise the device overheats. A power supply that is 85 % efficient delivering 100 W is dissipating about 17.6 W internally, enough to need a sensible heatsink.

## Batteries and stored charge

Battery capacity is usually quoted in ampere-hours (Ah) or milliampere-hours (mAh), which is simply a unit of charge: 1 Ah = 3600 C. A 3000 mAh cell can, in principle, supply 3 A for one hour or 300 mA for ten hours. To find the stored energy, multiply the charge by the nominal voltage: a 3.7 V, 3000 mAh cell stores about 11.1 Wh.

In practice the usable capacity is less than the rating. Cells are not discharged to zero volts; capacity falls at high currents and low temperatures, and it declines with age. Treat any runtime you calculate from the label as an upper estimate.

:::tip
Examiners love questions that combine two or three of the ideas in this chapter: for example, find the resistance of a heater from its rating, then find the length of nichrome wire needed to make it. Before you start calculating, list the quantities you know and the one you want, then pick the equation that links them. It sounds obvious, but it stops you wandering.
:::

## A word about significant figures

The values printed on components are rarely precise. A resistor marked 4.7 kΩ with a 5 % tolerance may be anywhere from 4.47 to 4.94 kΩ. Quoting a calculated current to eight significant figures is therefore not just untidy; it is misleading. In this book answers are generally given to three or four significant figures, which is more than enough for engineering purposes. Keep full precision in your calculator during intermediate steps and round only at the end. Rounding halfway through can accumulate surprising errors in longer problems.

## How to use the problems in this chapter

The problems that follow start very gently. If you are confident with the material, work through the Level 1 questions quickly, just to make sure, and spend your time on Levels 2 and 3. If you are returning to the subject after a break, or meeting it for the first time, do every one. Fluency with these basic relationships pays off in every later chapter, when you will be applying Ohm's law dozens of times in a single problem and simply cannot afford to hesitate over it.

## Check your understanding

Before starting the problems, make sure you can answer these without looking back. Brief answers follow each question.

**1. A wire carries 2 A for one minute. How many coulombs pass?** 120 C, since *Q* = *It* = 2 × 60.

**2. Why does a filament lamp not obey Ohm's law?** Its resistance rises steeply as the filament heats, so the *V*–*I* graph is a curve, not a straight line through the origin.

**3. A resistor has current entering its + terminal and *vi* = −3 W. What does that tell you?** That a sign has gone wrong somewhere: a resistor can only absorb power, never deliver it.

**4. How many joules are there in one kilowatt-hour?** 3.6 million: 1000 W for 3600 s.

**5. If a copper wire is replaced by one of the same length but twice the diameter, what happens to its resistance?** It falls to a quarter, because area goes with the square of diameter.

If any of these caused hesitation, reread the relevant section now. Every later chapter takes these ideas for granted.

:::summary
- Current is the rate of flow of charge, *I* = *Q*/*t*; conventional current flows from + to − through the external circuit.
- Voltage is energy per unit charge and is always measured between two points.
- Ohm's law, *V* = *IR*, describes linear resistors; lamps, diodes and many other devices do not obey it.
- Power *P* = *VI* = *I*<sup>2</sup>*R* = *V*<sup>2</sup>/*R*. Under the passive sign convention, a positive *vi* means power absorbed.
- Energy in kWh is power in kW multiplied by time in hours; 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.
- Resistance *R* = ρ*L*/*A* and rises with temperature in metals according to *R* = *R*<sub>0</sub>(1 + αΔθ).
- Efficiency η = *P*<sub>out</sub>/*P*<sub>in</sub>; the difference appears as heat.
:::

# Preface

The word *kyōhan* means a model or textbook, a book that sets a standard you can measure yourself against. It is an old-fashioned word, and I chose it on purpose. There are many good books about Goju-Ryu karate. There are histories, kata manuals, collections of interviews with great teachers and beautifully photographed books of technique. What I wanted when I was a younger student, and could never quite find, was a textbook in the plain sense: a book that took the inner training of the style one piece at a time, explained it clearly, showed it step by step and then told me what to do when it went wrong.

That last part matters most to me. In more than three decades of teaching I have noticed that students rarely fail because they do not know what to do. They fail because they do it, it does not work, and nobody tells them why. They stand in Sanchin for years with a knee that drifts outward or a breath that rises into the chest, and their teacher, busy with a class of twenty, never quite has the time to unpick it. They try iron body training from a magazine article, hurt their wrists and give up. They hear a great deal about *ki* and have no idea how to train it, so they either dismiss the whole idea or wander off into mysticism.

This book is my attempt to answer those problems. It is built around four themes that run through Goju-Ryu like threads through cloth.

**Sanchin tension.** How to build, hold, release and test the connected tension that gives Goju-Ryu its extraordinary structural strength, without the strain and breath-holding that injure so many students.

**Rooting.** How to connect with the ground so that you are hard to move, and how to send the ground's force up through your body into your techniques.

**Iron body methods.** How to use the traditional tools of *hojo undō* and progressive impact conditioning safely, patiently and effectively.

**Internal energy.** What the old teachers meant when they spoke of *ki*, how much of it we can explain today, and how to train the real, practical skills that sit underneath the word.

The book is illustrated with a large number of line drawings arranged in plates. They are deliberately simple. A photograph shows you one person on one day, with all their individual quirks. A line drawing can show you the principle cleanly, from whichever angle makes it easiest to see, and it can break a movement into as many steps as it takes. Please do not just look at them. Stand up, take the posture, and compare your body with the page.

A word about my voice. I have written this book as I teach: directly, in plain English, and with the occasional story from the dojo. The students I mention are real people, though I have changed details so that nobody will recognise themselves. I have tried throughout to say clearly what we know, what is tradition and what is my own opinion. Where Goju-Ryu schools differ, I have said so. Where I am uncertain, I have said that too.

Finally, a word of thanks. Every teacher is the sum of their own teachers and their students. I owe more than I can say to the people who stood in front of me in Sanchin and struck my shoulders until I learned to stand properly, and to the students who have trusted me with their training ever since. Any errors in this book are mine. Anything useful in it belongs to them.

*Dr Beverly J. Stivers*

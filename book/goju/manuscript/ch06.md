# Shime: Testing, Checking and Correcting Sanchin

::: chapter-intro
Shime is the practice of testing a student's Sanchin by pressing, pushing and striking the body while the kata is performed. It is the feedback loop that turns Sanchin from an exercise into a skill. This chapter explains what shime is for, how to give it and how to receive it, the safe targets and the forbidden ones, a twelve-month progression, and how to read what each test reveals so that you can correct the fault behind it.
:::

The word *shime* comes from a Japanese verb meaning to tighten, close or fasten. In Goju-Ryu it refers to the checks that a teacher or senior student gives during Sanchin: a hand pressing down on the shoulders, a slap across the back as the punch lands, a push to the chest, a strike to the abdomen or the thighs. To an onlooker, it can look harsh. In practice, given well, it is one of the most precise teaching tools in any martial art.

I want to stress the words "given well". I have seen shime used as a test of toughness, a way of establishing who is in charge or simply an excuse to hit people. All of these are abuses. Good shime is closer to a doctor's examination than a fight. Its purpose is to find out, accurately and quickly, whether the structure you have built in Sanchin is sound, and if not, exactly where it is weak. The person giving the check is not trying to hurt or defeat the student. They are asking the body a question.

## Why We Test

In Chapter 1 we met the method Notice, Name, Test, Adjust. Shime is the *Test* step made physical. Without it, Sanchin has a serious problem: you cannot see your own structure. You can stand in front of a mirror and see the shape, but you cannot see whether the inner thighs are engaged, whether the arm is connected to the back, or whether the abdomen would hold firm under a blow. Those things only show up under load.

Shime supplies the load, and it does so in a controlled, graduated way. A press on the shoulders tells you whether the stance and spine are stacked. A slap on the back as you punch tells you whether the lats are engaged. A push to the chest tells you whether your root is real. A strike to the abdomen tells you whether the breath and core are holding pressure. Each test has its own message, and with practice you learn to read them all.

There is a second purpose, which is conditioning. Repeated, sensible shime over months and years toughens the muscles and skin and teaches the nervous system not to flinch. We will return to this in Part Four. But conditioning is secondary. If shime is given only to toughen, and the information it carries is ignored, half its value is lost.

## The Two Roles

Shime is a partnership. One person performs Sanchin; the other, whom I shall call the tester, gives the checks. Both roles are skills, and both need to be learned.

### Receiving shime

The student's job is to keep doing Sanchin. That sounds obvious, but the natural reaction to being pressed or struck is to stop, brace, flinch or change the kata. None of these helps. The student should continue the breath and the movement exactly as before, and simply notice what happens in the body when the check arrives. Did the knees give? Did the breath catch? Did the shoulder lift? Did the push travel down into the floor, or did it rock you?

After the kata, the student and tester should talk, briefly, about what each noticed. This conversation is where most of the learning happens.

### Giving shime

The tester's job is harder, and far more often done badly. A good tester:

- knows the safe targets and the forbidden ones;
- matches the force to the student's level and the day's condition;
- times the check to the student's breath and movement;
- strikes with a relaxed arm and a loose, open hand or soft fist, never with a straight, stiff jab;
- watches the whole body, not just the point of contact;
- stops at once if the student shows signs of distress.

The tester also learns a great deal. Feeling the difference between a connected Sanchin and a disconnected one is one of the fastest ways to understand your own.

## The Shoulder Press

If you learn only one shime, learn this one. It is gentle, safe, quick to give and extraordinarily informative. Plate 6.2 shows it.

![**Plate 6.2** The shoulder press. (a) The tester's hands are held above the shoulders. (b) They rest lightly on the tops of the shoulders, close to the neck. (c) The tester presses straight down, gradually. (d) The student holds, and the pressure passes into the floor. (e–f) The same, viewed from behind. (g) Fault: the student folds forward at the hips. (h) Fault: the knees give way.](figures/p6_2.png)

1. The tester stands behind the student in a stable stance, close enough to keep the elbows slightly bent.
2. The tester rests the palms on the tops of the student's shoulders, between the neck and the point of the shoulder, on the thick muscle there (the *trapezius*).
3. As the student exhales, the tester presses straight down, building gradually over about two seconds. The pressure is vertical, not forward or backward.
4. The tester holds the pressure for a moment, then releases as the student inhales.

What does it tell you? If the student's spine is stacked and the stance is sound, the pressure passes straight down through the body into the feet. The student feels heavier, more rooted, and often says it is easier to stand with the pressure than without it. If anything is out of line, the press finds it immediately. A head that pokes forward makes the neck strain. An arched lower back makes the lower back ache. A forward lean makes the student fold at the hips. Knees that are not braced make the stance sink.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "When my shoulders are pressed, my lower back hurts."**

*The problem.* Under the shoulder press, you feel a pinching or aching in the lower back.

*Why it happens.* Your pelvis is tipped forward and your lower back is arched, so the vertical load is pressing on the small joints at the back of the spine rather than travelling down through the centre of the body.

*The solution.* Draw the tailbone down gently and let the lower back lengthen, as described in Chapter 2. Ask the tester for a very light press while you adjust. You should feel the pressure move from your lower back to your legs. Then repeat the wall test from Chapter 2 daily for a fortnight.
:::

## Checking the Back

The back is where the arm–back line of Chapter 5 lives, and it is the most important place to check during the punches. Plate 6.3 shows the usual checks.

![**Plate 6.3** Back checks. (a) The tester's palms rest on the large muscles below the shoulder blades as the student punches. (b) A slap is given at the moment the punch reaches full extension. (c–d) One side at a time. (e) Between the shoulder blades. (f) The long muscles beside the lower spine. (g) The same check seen from the side. (h) The buttocks.](figures/p6_3.png)

The most valuable of these is the slap on the lats as the punch lands, frame (b). If the arm–back line is engaged, the tester's hand meets a firm, broad muscle that does not give. If the student is punching with the shoulder alone, the tester's hand sinks into a soft back, and the student often feels the slap jolt the shoulder forward.

The tester uses an open hand, relaxed, swinging from the elbow. The slap should make a satisfying sound and sting a little; it should not bruise. The area between the shoulder blades and the long muscles beside the lower spine may also be checked with a flat palm, but the spine itself must never be struck.

## Checking the Abdomen

Abdominal checks test the breath and the deep core. They are also where shime is most often overdone, so read this section carefully. Plate 6.4 shows a progression.

![**Plate 6.4** Abdominal checks, in order of increasing firmness. (a) A fingertip touch to direct the student's attention. (b) A flat palm press. (c) A light palm strike. (d) The upper abdomen. (e) The side of the belly. (f) A soft fist, for advanced students only. (g) Fault: the student doubles up. (h) The strike always comes on the exhalation.](figures/p6_4.png)

The golden rule is that **the abdomen is only ever checked during the exhalation**, when the breath is controlled and the core is pressurised. A strike during the inhalation catches the student with the diaphragm moving and the abdominal wall relaxing, and can wind them badly.

Begin with the fingertips and the flat palm. Many students never need more than this. A light palm strike is the next stage, and a soft fist the last, given only to students who have trained Sanchin breathing for many months and who are comfortable with the lighter checks. The fist is never driven through the target; it lands and stays, like a heavy object being set down on a table.

The fault in frame (g), doubling up, is a sign that the strike was too hard for the student's current condition, or arrived at the wrong moment. The tester should immediately reduce the force.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

I do not strike the abdomens of students who are pregnant, who have had recent abdominal surgery or who have a hernia, and I do not strike the abdomens of children at all. For these students, the fingertip and flat-palm checks give all the information we need. There is no virtue in hitting people simply because tradition allows it.
:::

## Checking the Legs

The legs carry the root, and leg checks test whether the stance is braced. Plate 6.5 shows them.

![**Plate 6.5** Leg checks. (a) The tester's hands rest on the student's thighs. (b) The tester tries to draw the knees outward. (c) The tester tries to push the knees inward. (d) A slap to the front of the thigh. (e) A slap to the outer thigh. (f) The backs of the thighs. (g) A check to the calf and shin. (h) Fault: the knees open under the test.](figures/p6_5.png)

The most informative are the knee tests in frames (b) and (c). The tester crouches in front, places the hands on the inside or outside of the student's knees and applies a slow, steady pressure. A well-braced Sanchin dachi holds the knees in place; the inner leg line of Chapter 5 resists being drawn apart, and the outer hip muscles resist being pushed together. If the knees move, the stance is not braced.

Slaps to the fronts, sides and backs of the thighs check the muscles' readiness and condition them. They should be given with an open hand to the thick muscle, never to the knee itself.

## Checking the Arms

Arm checks test the guard and the punch. Plate 6.6 shows the main ones.

![**Plate 6.6** Arm checks. (a) The tester presses the student's guarding fist back toward the shoulder. (b) A strike to the forearm. (c) A push on the elbow from the side. (d) Both forearms at once. (e) Pressing the extended fist back along the line of the arm. (f) A strike to the extended arm. (g) A sideways push on the extended arm. (h) Fault: the arm folds at the elbow.](figures/p6_6.png)

Frame (e) is particularly valuable. The tester places a palm against the student's extended fist and pushes straight back along the line of the arm. If the arm is connected through the back to the stance, the push travels into the body and down to the feet, and the student does not move. If the arm is supported only by the shoulder, the elbow folds, as in frame (h), or the shoulder is driven back.

## Push Tests

Push tests check the root as a whole. They are less dramatic than strikes, but in many ways they are the most revealing. Plate 6.7 shows them.

![**Plate 6.7** Push tests. (a) A one-hand push to the upper chest. (b) A two-hand push. (c) A push from the side. (d) A pull from behind. (e) A low push at the hips. (f) A side push at the hips. (g) Fault: the student leans back to resist. (h) Correct: the push travels into the ground.](figures/p6_7.png)

The tester pushes slowly and steadily, building over about three seconds, and releases slowly. Sudden shoves are not useful here; they test reaction, not structure. The student does not push back. They simply maintain the stance, breathe and let the force pass through them into the floor.

The most common fault is frame (g): the student leans into the push to resist it. Leaning feels strong, but it is a trap. The moment the tester removes the push, the student falls forward. A rooted student, by contrast, stays upright and lets the force go down.

## Safe Targets and Forbidden Ones

Plate 6.8 summarises the areas that may be checked and the areas that must never be struck.

![**Plate 6.8** Targets for shime. (a) Front: shoulders, chest muscles and abdomen may be checked. (b) Back: shoulders, the large back muscles and the backs of the arms. (c) Front: never strike the head, throat or groin. (d) Back: never strike the head, neck, spine or kidneys.](figures/p6_8.png)

| May be checked | Never strike |
|---|---|
| Tops of the shoulders (press) | Head, face and jaw |
| Large back muscles (lats) | Throat and front of the neck |
| Upper chest muscles (press, light slap) | Back of the neck and spine |
| Abdomen, on the exhalation | Kidneys (lower back, either side of the spine) |
| Fronts, sides and backs of the thighs | Groin |
| Forearms and upper arms | Kneecaps and the sides of the knee joint |
| Calves | Floating ribs at the sides, with any force |

The kidneys deserve a special word. They sit just under the lowest ribs at the back, either side of the spine, and they are vulnerable. A careless strike to the lower back can bruise a kidney seriously. When checking the back, keep to the broad muscles higher up, below the shoulder blades.

## A Twelve-Month Progression

Shime must be introduced gradually. Plate 6.9 shows the progression that I use for adult students in reasonable health.

![**Plate 6.9** A twelve-month progression for shime. Pressing and pushing begin in the first month. Open-hand slaps are introduced in the third month and build slowly. Light fist strikes, to the trunk only, are added after about six months and never exceed a moderate level for most students.](figures/p6_9.png)

**Months one to two.** Pressing and pushing only: shoulder press, push tests, knee tests and pressing the fist back. Very light.

**Months three to five.** Add open-hand slaps to the back, thighs and arms, starting gently. Fingertip and flat-palm checks to the abdomen.

**Months six to nine.** Slaps become firmer. Light palm strikes to the abdomen on the exhalation. Light, soft-fist checks to the large muscles of the back and thighs.

**Months ten to twelve.** For students who are comfortable with everything above, a soft fist to the abdomen on the exhalation, at a light to moderate level.

There is no need to go beyond a moderate level for most students, ever. The information shime provides is available at quite modest forces, and heavier striking adds risk without adding much learning.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I flinch every time I'm touched."**

*The problem.* As soon as the tester's hand approaches, your body braces, your breath catches and you stop moving smoothly.

*Why it happens.* Flinching is a natural protective reflex. It reduces with familiarity, but only if the checks are predictable and never more than you can comfortably take.

*The solution.* Ask your tester to announce each check for the first few weeks ("shoulders", "back", "push") and to use a lighter touch than you think you need. Keep the breath audible and moving through every check. Gradually, the tester stops announcing. Most students stop flinching within two to three months.
:::

## Self-Shime: Checking Yourself

If you train alone, you can still do a great deal of checking. Plate 6.10 shows a set of self-checks.

![**Plate 6.10** Self-checks. (a) Palms on the belly during the exhalation. (b) Light taps with the fists to the abdomen. (c) Hands on the ribs to check their width. (d) Slapping the thighs. (e) Checking the opposite arm's tension. (f) Squeezing the muscle above the shoulder. (g) Hands on the back of the waist. (h) Re-setting the guard.](figures/p6_10.png)

Self-checks cannot replace a partner, because you cannot test your root on yourself. But they can tell you a great deal about the breath, the core and the tension in the limbs. Practise them during a slow, single repetition of Sanchin, pausing at each check, and then perform the kata through without stopping.

You can also use the wall for push tests. Stand in Sanchin dachi facing a wall, close enough to place your palms on it with the elbows bent. Press gently into the wall and notice where the reaction force goes. If you feel it in your feet, your structure is connected. If you feel it in your shoulders or your lower back, it is not.

## Mistakes Testers Make

Finally, a word to testers. Plate 6.11 shows eight mistakes that I see far too often.

![**Plate 6.11** Mistakes made by testers. (a) Jabbing with a stiff, straight arm. (b) Striking the kidneys. (c) Striking during the student's inhalation. (d) Striking near the head. (e) Striking low toward the groin. (f) Striking the spine. (g) Striking the kneecap. (h) Striking without warning before the student is ready.](figures/p6_11.png)

A stiff-armed jab, frame (a), concentrates force in a small area and jars the tester's own elbow and shoulder. A relaxed arm swinging from the elbow delivers the check more safely and more informatively. Striking during the inhalation, frame (c), is the single commonest cause of a winded student. Striking without warning in the early months, frame (h), teaches flinching rather than structure.

And there is one more mistake that cannot be drawn. It is the tester who uses shime to show off, to punish or to dominate. If you ever find yourself enjoying the impact more than the information, step back and let someone else test.

## Reading the Results

The real value of shime lies in what you do with what it reveals. The following table links each common result to the fault behind it and the chapter where the correction is explained.

| What the test shows | Likely fault | Correction | See |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shoulder press: student folds forward | Leaning; head forward | Restack the spine; plumb line | Ch. 2 |
| Shoulder press: lower back aches | Pelvis tipped forward | Draw tailbone down | Ch. 2 |
| Shoulder press: knees sink | Inner legs not braced | Inner leg line | Ch. 5 |
| Slap on back: soft, shoulder jolts | Arm–back line not engaged | Elbows heavy; lats | Ch. 5 |
| Abdomen: breath catches | Breath held or high | Low, audible ibuki | Ch. 3 |
| Abdomen: student doubles | Core not pressurised; strike too hard | Reduce force; pressure partnership | Ch. 3 |
| Knee test: knees move | Stance not braced; feet wrong | Re-measure stance | Ch. 4 |
| Fist pressed back: elbow folds | Arm not connected | Elbow brushes ribs | Ch. 5 |
| Push: student rocks backward | Stance too short; weight back | Lengthen; centre weight | Ch. 4 |
| Push: student leans into it | Resisting with lean, not root | Let force pass down | Ch. 7 |

Over months, keep a note in your training journal of the shime results. You will see the faults change: a fault that dominated in spring fades by summer, and another, hidden beneath it, emerges. This is how Sanchin improves, one layer at a time.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Shime is a test, not a beating. Its main purpose is information; conditioning is secondary.
- The student keeps performing Sanchin and notices; the tester checks, watches and adjusts the force.
- The shoulder press is the most useful and safest single check.
- The back slap as the punch lands tests the arm–back line.
- The abdomen is checked only on the exhalation, starting with the fingertips and the flat palm.
- Never strike the head, neck, spine, kidneys, groin or knee joints.
- Introduce shime gradually over a year; moderate force is enough for nearly all students.
- Record the results and use them to guide your corrections.
:::

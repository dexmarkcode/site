# A Lifetime of Practice: Programmes, Progressions and Problem Solving

::: chapter-intro
Everything in this book is only useful if you practise it, regularly, for years. This final chapter turns the methods of the previous fourteen into practical programmes: a daily routine, weekly plans for three levels, a twelve-week block, adaptations for older students, children and injuries, a set of self-assessment tests, and a problem-solving guide that brings together the commonest difficulties from every part of the book.
:::

When I was about forty, I went through a bad patch with my training. Work was demanding, my knees were complaining and I had started to feel that I was simply going through the motions. I mentioned this to my old teacher, then in her late seventies, expecting sympathy. She listened, nodded and asked me how often I did Sanchin. "Two or three times a week, in class," I said. "And at home?" There was a pause. "Not really." She laughed. "Then you haven't got a karate problem," she said. "You've got a habit problem."

She was right. I went home and began doing twenty minutes every morning before work: some warm-up, some breathing, Sanchin and Tensho, a little conditioning, a few minutes of sitting. Within a month the slump had gone. Within a year I was moving better than I had at thirty. The secret was not intensity. It was regularity.

This chapter is about building that regularity into a life that already has plenty in it.

## The Daily Practice

If you take only one thing from this chapter, let it be this: a short daily practice is worth far more than an occasional long one. Plate 15.1 shows a twenty-minute routine that covers the core of the book.

![**Plate 15.1** A twenty-minute daily routine. (a) A standing bow to begin. (b–e) Warm-up: neck, arms, trunk and squats. (f) Abdominal breathing. (g) Sanchin. (h) Tensho. (i) Chi-ishi. (j) Self-conditioning. (k) Stretching. (l) Mokuso to finish.](figures/p15_1.png)

| Minutes | Activity | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Bow; stand quietly; body scan | 1, 13 |
| 1–5 | Junbi undō: neck, arms, trunk, hips, knees, ankles | 2 |
| 5–7 | Abdominal breathing, standing | 3 |
| 7–11 | Sanchin, once or twice | 4–6 |
| 11–14 | Tensho, once | 9 |
| 14–16 | Chi-ishi or grip work (alternate days) | 10, 12 |
| 16–17 | Self-conditioning slaps | 11 |
| 17–19 | Cool-down stretches | this chapter |
| 19–20 | Mokuso | 3, 13 |

Do this at the same time every day if you can. Mornings suit many people, because nothing else has yet crowded the day. Others prefer the evening, when the body is warmer and more supple. What matters is that it becomes as automatic as cleaning your teeth.

### The five-minute version

Some days, twenty minutes is impossible. On those days, do five. Plate 15.11 shows a routine that takes no longer than boiling a kettle.

![**Plate 15.11** A five-minute morning routine. (a) Stand and scan the body. (b) Reach up. (c) Hinge forward. (d) Twist. (e) Sink. (f) Three Sanchin breaths. (g–h) Open and close with the breath.](figures/p15_11.png)

Five minutes keeps the habit alive. On a day when you have done five minutes, you have trained. On a day when you have done nothing, the habit begins to weaken. I would rather a student did five minutes every day than an hour once a week.

## Weekly Programmes

Your daily practice is the foundation. On top of it sit your dojo classes and any longer sessions you have time for. Plate 15.3 shows the overall shape of a week at three levels.

![**Plate 15.3** Weekly training time at three levels, in minutes per day, including both home practice and classes.](figures/p15_3.png)

### Beginner (first year)

**Three sessions of about thirty minutes, plus one longer weekend session.** Concentrate on posture, stances, breathing and the basic Sanchin. Light hojo undō with the chi-ishi and grip work twice a week. Shime limited to pressing and pushing. No impact conditioning beyond light self-slapping in the first three months.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Monday | Warm-up; breathing; Sanchin dachi and crescent step; Sanchin |
| Wednesday | Warm-up; Sanchin; light chi-ishi; durability work (hands, hips) |
| Friday | Warm-up; breathing; Sanchin with partner pushes if possible |
| Saturday | Class or longer session: kata, basics, partner work |

### Intermediate (years two to four)

**Five or six sessions, varying from twenty minutes to an hour.** Sanchin and Tensho daily. Hojo undō two or three times a week. Kote kitae and makiwara two or three times a week, following the progressions in Chapter 11. Shime with open-hand slaps. Kakie from the fixed pattern to slow free practice.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Monday | Sanchin, Tensho; hojo undō; durability (shoulders, spine) |
| Tuesday | Class: kata, kakie, partner work |
| Wednesday | Sanchin, Tensho; kote kitae; makiwara |
| Thursday | Light day: breathing, stretching, mokuso |
| Friday | Sanchin with shime; hojo undō; durability (hands, legs) |
| Saturday | Class or long session: kata, conditioning, kakie |
| Sunday | Rest, or five-minute routine only |

### Advanced (black belt and beyond)

**Six days a week, with one or two longer sessions.** The emphasis gradually shifts from building to refining. Conditioning reaches a maintenance level and stays there. Kakie becomes free practice. The classical kata are studied in depth, alongside their applications. Internal training, including standing practice and moving meditation, takes up more of the time.

The most important thing for the advanced student is variety within consistency: the same core every week, with different emphases from month to month, so that the body is never asked to do exactly the same thing for too long.

## The Twelve-Week Block

Many students make faster progress if they organise their training into blocks of about three months, each with a clear focus. Plate 15.4 shows a typical block.

![**Plate 15.4** A twelve-week training block. Volume (solid line) and intensity (dashed line) rise gradually through the foundation, build and consolidation phases, with lighter weeks at weeks four, eight and twelve.](figures/p15_4.png)

**Weeks one to four: foundation.** Choose one main focus for the block, such as the root, the breath or the grip. Review the relevant chapter. Train it at a moderate level, and test yourself at the start using the assessments later in this chapter.

**Weeks five to eight: build.** Increase the volume and intensity of your main focus gradually. Week eight is lighter.

**Weeks nine to eleven: consolidate.** Bring the volume up to its highest level, and integrate the focus into your kata and partner work.

**Week twelve: review.** Train lightly, repeat the assessments, write up your journal and choose the focus for the next block.

The lighter weeks are not optional. They are when your body catches up with the training, and they protect you from the slow build-up of fatigue that leads to injury and loss of motivation.

## Testing Your Progress

It is easy to feel that you are not improving, particularly in the slow, subtle areas of internal training. Regular, simple tests will show you the truth. Plate 15.7 shows eight that I use.

![**Plate 15.7** Eight self-assessment tests. (a) One-leg balance. (b) The wall test. (c) A timed Sanchin hold. (d) A partner push test. (e) A grip hold. (f) A wall sit. (g) A forward reach. (h) A breath count.](figures/p15_7.png)

| Test | How to do it | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| One-leg balance | Stand on one leg, eyes open, then closed | Seconds held, each leg |
| Wall test | As in Chapter 2 | Which points touch; gap at lower back |
| Sanchin hold | Hold Sanchin dachi in the guard, nogare breathing | Minutes held with good form |
| Push test | Partner pushes chest slowly, then releases suddenly | Moved? Fell forward on release? |
| Grip hold | Hold a heavy object in each hand, standing | Seconds before grip fails |
| Wall sit | Back against wall, thighs near horizontal | Seconds held |
| Forward reach | Hinge forward from standing, knees soft | How far the hands reach |
| Breath count | Slow ibuki exhalation with good form | Seconds of controlled exhale |

Do these tests at the start and end of each twelve-week block, and write the results in your journal. Over a year or two, the numbers will tell a story that your feelings never could.

## Adapting for Older Students

Goju-Ryu is one of the best martial arts for older people, because so much of its training is slow, controlled and focused on posture, breath and balance. But some adaptations are wise. Plate 15.2 shows eight.

![**Plate 15.2** Adaptations for older students. (a) A higher Sanchin dachi. (b) A lower guard. (c) Balance practice beside a wall. (d) A short wall sit. (e) Nogare breathing in place of ibuki. (f) Gentle Tensho. (g) Light wrist work. (h) Hinging at the hips rather than bending the spine.](figures/p15_2.png)

- **Higher stances.** Reduce the depth of all stances to protect the knees and hips.
- **Soft breathing.** Use nogare rather than ibuki, especially if blood pressure is a concern.
- **Moderate tension.** Keep Sanchin tension at about half; avoid maximal efforts.
- **Balance work every day.** As discussed in Chapter 7, this is one of the most valuable things an older student can do.
- **Gentle conditioning.** Self-slapping and light kote kitae are fine for most; heavy impact work is not necessary.
- **Emphasise Tensho and kakie.** These develop the soft, sensitive skills that improve with age.
- **Longer warm-ups, longer recovery.** Allow more time for both.

I have taught students who began Goju-Ryu in their sixties and seventies. They will never punch like a twenty-five-year-old, but many of them have developed a root, a sensitivity and a calm that their younger classmates envy.

## Adapting for Children

Children love the energy of karate and benefit greatly from its discipline, its emphasis on posture and its physical demands. But their bodies are still growing, and some traditional methods are not appropriate for them.

- **No heavy breath-holding or maximal Sanchin tension.** Children can practise Sanchin with natural breathing and light tension.
- **No impact conditioning** beyond gentle self-slapping. Growing bones should not be repeatedly struck.
- **No shime strikes.** Pressing and pushing tests only.
- **Light hojo undō** with very light tools, or none at all; body-weight exercises are better.
- **Plenty of games**, balance challenges and movement.

The habits of posture, breath and self-control that children learn in Goju-Ryu can last a lifetime. The heavy conditioning can wait until they are fully grown.

## Training Around Injuries

Almost everyone who trains for long enough will be injured at some point. An injury does not have to mean stopping altogether. Plate 15.5 shows ways of continuing to train around some common problems.

![**Plate 15.5** Training around injuries. (a–b) For a sore knee, use a higher Sanchin or a natural stance. (c–d) For a sore shoulder, lower the guard and shorten the punch. (e–f) For a sore back, use a supported wall sit and avoid deep shiko dachi. (g) For a sore wrist, use open hands. (h) Whatever the injury, breathing practice can continue.](figures/p15_5.png)

The principle is to train everything that does not hurt, and to modify or rest what does. A sprained ankle does not stop you practising Sanchin breathing, the arm movements of Tensho while seated, or grip work. Keeping the rest of the body active speeds recovery and keeps the habit of training alive.

But know when to stop altogether and seek help. Sharp or worsening pain, swelling, numbness, pins and needles, weakness, pain that wakes you at night, or any injury to the head, chest or abdomen all need professional assessment. Do not try to "train through" these.

## The Cool-Down

Every session should end with a few minutes of gentle stretching and quiet. Plate 15.6 shows a simple cool-down.

![**Plate 15.6** A cool-down routine. (a) Hamstrings. (b) Hip flexors. (c) Inner thighs. (d) Side of the trunk. (e) Shoulder. (f) Wrists. (g) Neck. (h) Resting in seiza.](figures/p15_6.png)

Hold each stretch for about thirty seconds, breathing slowly. Do not bounce or force. Then sit quietly for a minute or two. The cool-down helps the body shift from effort to recovery, and it is the moment when the lessons of the session settle in.

## Training While Travelling

You do not need a dojo or equipment to keep your training going. Plate 15.10 shows a routine that can be done in a hotel room.

![**Plate 15.10** A routine for training while travelling, with no equipment. (a) Breathing. (b) Sanchin in place. (c) Wall press. (d) Shiko dachi hold. (e) Wringing a towel. (f) Balance. (g) Wall sit. (h) Mokuso.](figures/p15_10.png)

Sanchin can be performed in place, stepping forward and back, in very little space. A towel replaces the grip tools. A wall replaces the partner for push and press work. Twenty minutes in a hotel room is twenty minutes of real training.

## The Kata as a Lifelong Curriculum

The kata of Goju-Ryu were designed to be studied for a lifetime. Plate 15.9 shows a common teaching order.

![**Plate 15.9** A common teaching order for the Goju-Ryu kata, shown by a representative posture from each. The order differs between schools.](figures/p15_9.png)

In most schools, Sanchin and the two Gekisai kata come first, followed by Tensho and Saifa, and then the classical kata in roughly increasing order of complexity, ending with Suparinpei. A student who trains steadily might learn a new kata every year or two. But learning the sequence is only the beginning. Each kata contains a lifetime of study: its stances, its breath, its tension, its applications and its internal qualities. Many senior teachers say that they only began to understand Sanchin after twenty years.

## How Training Changes with Age

Plate 15.8 shows, in a rough and illustrative way, how the emphasis of training shifts across a lifetime.

![**Plate 15.8** An illustrative view of training across a lifetime. Raw power and speed (dashed) peak in early adulthood and then decline. The volume of impact conditioning (dotted) should rise and then fall. Skill, sensitivity and internal quality (solid) can continue to grow for decades.](figures/p15_8.png)

This is the great promise of Goju-Ryu. Power and speed fade with age for everyone. But the qualities this book has spent most of its time on, the root, the connected structure, the breath, the soft hand and the quiet, attentive mind, can go on improving long after the body has passed its physical peak. That is why the old teachers could do things that astonished their young students. It is also why Goju-Ryu can be practised, with pleasure and benefit, for a whole lifetime.

## A Problem-Solving Guide to the Whole Book

Finally, here is a summary of the problems students most often bring to me, with the chapters where you will find the solutions.

| Problem | First thing to check | See |
|---|---|---|
| "My Sanchin feels weak when tested." | Stance measurements; knees over feet | 4, 6 |
| "I get dizzy in Sanchin." | Breath held or forced | 3 |
| "My shoulders ache after Sanchin." | Arm–back line; elbows heavy | 5, 12 |
| "My knees hurt in stances." | Knee tracking; depth | 2, 12 |

| "I can't feel anything in kakie." | Tension; contact too heavy | 13, 14 |
| "I've lost motivation." | Habit, not karate | this chapter |
| "I don't know if I'm improving." | Self-assessment tests | this chapter |

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I've plateaued."**

*The problem.* After a few years of steady progress, you feel that nothing is improving. Your kata look the same, the tests give the same results and training has become routine.

*Why it happens.* Plateaus are a normal part of learning any complex skill. They often mean that you have exhausted the gains from your current way of training and need a new stimulus.

*The solution.* Change the focus. Use a twelve-week block to work on something you have neglected: Tensho if you are a Sanchin person, kakie if you mostly train alone, internal work if you mostly condition. Train with different partners. Teach a beginner, which forces you to understand what you do. Review your journal to see how far you have actually come. And be patient. Plateaus often end suddenly, with a leap.
:::

## A Final Word

I began this book with a story about a church hall and a group of ordinary people standing in a narrow stance, breathing loudly and being struck by their teacher. I have since spent the larger part of my life in halls like that one, and I am still learning.

Goju-Ryu does not make anyone invincible, and it does not offer secret powers. What it offers is something better: a method, tested over more than a century, for building a body that is strong and supple, a breath that is calm and controlled, a structure that is rooted and connected, and a mind that is steady under pressure. It offers this to anyone who will practise patiently, regardless of size, strength or age.

Hardness and softness, the old precept says, are in the breathing. Breathe in; breathe out. Stand in Sanchin tomorrow morning, and the morning after that. The rest will follow.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Regularity matters more than intensity; a short daily practice is the foundation.
- Use the twenty-minute routine on most days and the five-minute routine on busy days.
- Build weekly programmes appropriate to your level, with lighter days.
- Organise training into twelve-week blocks with a clear focus and lighter weeks.
- Test yourself at the start and end of each block, and keep a journal.
- Adapt training for older students, children and injuries; train what does not hurt.
- Power fades with age; skill, sensitivity and internal quality can keep growing.
:::

# Ki and Internal Energy: What It Is, What It Is Not and How to Train It

::: chapter-intro
Few words in martial arts cause as much confusion as *ki*. Some teachers speak of it as a literal energy that can be stored and projected; others dismiss it as superstition. This chapter takes a practical middle path. It explains what the old teachers were pointing at, what modern physiology can and cannot explain, which demonstrations are real skills and which are tricks, and, above all, how to train the genuine internal qualities that the word describes.
:::

When I was a brown belt, a visiting instructor gave a seminar on ki. He stood with one arm extended and invited the strongest people in the room to bend it. They could not. He then let a slight young woman lift him off the ground, and asked a burly man to try. The burly man could not budge him. He finished by pushing a student over with what looked like a gentle touch from across a metre of empty space.

I was impressed, and also uneasy. The first two demonstrations I later learned to do myself; they are real and they can be explained. The third, I am quite sure, was the result of a student who had been conditioned to fall over when the teacher gestured. It was not dishonest, exactly. I think both the teacher and the student believed it. But it was not a martial skill, and it would not have worked on a stranger in the street.

This chapter is about separating the first two kinds of demonstration from the third. There is a great deal of real, trainable skill hidden behind the word *ki*. There is also a great deal of wishful thinking. You deserve to know which is which.

## What the Old Teachers Meant

*Ki* is the Japanese reading of the Chinese *qi*. In classical Chinese thought, it is a broad concept: breath, vapour, the vital force of living things, the energy of weather and seasons. It underpins traditional Chinese medicine, with its theory of channels and points. When Chinese boxing reached Okinawa, the vocabulary came with it, and Okinawan and Japanese teachers used *ki* to describe a family of experiences that are hard to put into ordinary words.

If you read what Goju-Ryu teachers actually said, as opposed to what is sometimes attributed to them, you find that they used the idea of ki in fairly practical ways. They spoke of ki "sinking" to the tanden, of ki being "full" in the abdomen, of ki "flowing" through the arms, of the importance of the breath and of a calm, focused spirit. These descriptions map closely onto things we can now describe in physical terms: a low centre of gravity, abdominal pressure, connected tension, coordinated breathing and a steady state of mind.

I do not think we should throw the word away. It is a useful shorthand, and it directs the attention in helpful ways. But I think we should be clear about what we are training when we train it.

## A Working Definition

In this book, I use the term *internal energy* to mean the combined effect of six trainable skills. None of them is mysterious, and all of them can be tested.

1. **Centring.** Organising the body around a low, stable centre of mass near the tanden.
2. **Breath.** Using the breath and the pressure partnership of Chapter 3 to stabilise the trunk and coordinate movement.
3. **Connection.** Linking the body through chains of appropriate tension so that force travels through it efficiently (Chapters 5 and 7).
4. **Relaxation.** Releasing unnecessary tension, so that only the muscles that are needed are working.
5. **Intention.** Directing the attention in a way that organises the body's movement before it happens.
6. **Sensitivity.** Feeling a partner's force, balance and intention through contact (Chapter 14).

When all six work together, the result feels quite unlike ordinary physical effort. Movements feel effortless and whole. Force seems to "flow". Partners feel a heaviness, a springiness or a power that does not match the apparent effort. It is not surprising that people have described this with a word that means "energy". But you can train each part separately, and you can check each one.

## Centring: The Tanden

The *tanden* (Chinese *dantian*) is the point in the lower abdomen, a few finger-widths below the navel and some way inside the body, on which Goju-Ryu teachers ask you to focus. Plate 13.1 marks it in eight postures.

![**Plate 13.1** The tanden marked in eight postures: standing, seiza, Sanchin dachi, shiko dachi and neko ashi dachi, from the front and side. In every posture, the tanden sits low in the abdomen, near the body's centre of mass.](figures/p13_1.png)

It is no coincidence that the tanden lies close to the body's centre of mass. When you focus your attention there, several useful things happen. Your posture tends to settle, because you stop holding yourself up from the chest. Your breath tends to drop, because attention to the lower belly encourages diaphragmatic breathing. And your movements tend to begin from the hips and centre rather than the arms and shoulders, which is exactly what the kinetic chain of Chapter 8 requires.

So when a teacher tells you to "move from the tanden" or "keep your ki in the tanden", you can take the instruction literally: put your attention in the lower abdomen and let movement begin there. Whatever your view of ki, the effect on your karate will be real.

::: drill
**Drill: Finding the centre**

Stand in heiko dachi. Place one palm on your lower abdomen, just below the navel, and the other on your lower back, directly behind it. Close your eyes and breathe slowly into the space between your hands. Now imagine a small, heavy ball sitting in that space. As you breathe out, let the ball sink a little lower. After a minute, remove your hands and keep the image. Then walk slowly around the room, keeping your attention on the ball. Notice how your walk changes.
:::

## The Body Scan: Training Relaxation

Most people carry tension they are not aware of. It hides in the jaw, the shoulders, the hands, the belly and the toes. This hidden tension acts like a brake on every movement and blocks the flow of force through the body. Learning to find and release it is one of the most important internal skills. Plate 13.3 shows a systematic body scan.

![**Plate 13.3** The body scan. The attention moves slowly down the body, from (a) the crown and face, to (b) the neck and shoulders, (c) the chest, (d) the arms and hands, (e) the belly, (f) the pelvis, (g) the knees and (h) the feet, releasing unnecessary tension at each stage.](figures/p13_3.png)

Stand or sit comfortably. Bring your attention to the top of your head and your face. Notice any tension: in the forehead, around the eyes, in the jaw. Let it go with an exhalation. Move down to the neck and shoulders; let the shoulders drop. Continue down through the body, one region per breath or two, all the way to the feet. Then rest your attention in the tanden for a few breaths.

The body scan takes three to five minutes. Done daily, it gradually teaches you to notice tension the moment it appears, which is the first step to controlling it.

### The heavy arm

A partner can help you check whether you are truly relaxed. Plate 13.7 shows the test.

![**Plate 13.7** The heavy arm test. (a) The partner supports the relaxed arm under the wrist and elbow. (b) The partner lifts it gently. (c) The partner suddenly removes their hands. (d) A truly relaxed arm drops immediately and hangs heavily.](figures/p13_7.png)

Let your arm hang relaxed. Your partner lifts it gently by the wrist and elbow, then suddenly lets go. If your arm is truly relaxed, it drops straight down like a sandbag. If it hangs in the air for a moment, or you "help" it down, you are holding tension you did not know about. Most people are surprised by how hard this is.

## Breath and Movement: Opening and Closing

In Chapter 3 we coordinated the breath with simple arm movements. Internal training takes this further, linking the breath not just to the arms but to the whole body expanding and contracting around the centre. Plate 13.4 shows an exercise often called "opening and closing".

![**Plate 13.4** Opening and closing. (a) The hands gather in front of the tanden. (b–e) With a slow inhalation, the hands rise and open outward, as though the body were expanding from the centre. (f–h) With a slow exhalation, they close and return to the centre.](figures/p13_4.png)

As you breathe in, imagine the body gently expanding from the tanden in all directions, and let the hands drift outward as part of that expansion. As you breathe out, imagine it gently condensing back to the centre, and let the hands return. The arms do not move by themselves; they ride on the expansion and contraction of the trunk.

Plate 13.5 shows the same idea schematically.

![**Plate 13.5** Breath, centre and intent, shown schematically. (a) A settled centre. (b) The breath sinks as the diaphragm lowers. (c) Pressure spreads evenly from the centre. (d) Intention rises from the centre and directs movement outward.](figures/p13_5.png)

### What slow breathing does

There is good scientific evidence that slow, deep breathing, particularly at around six breaths a minute with a longer exhalation than inhalation, has measurable effects on the nervous system. It tends to slow the heart, lower blood pressure in the short term and shift the body toward the relaxed, "rest and digest" state governed by the parasympathetic nervous system. Plate 13.6 shows an illustrative pattern.

![**Plate 13.6** An illustrative pattern of heart rate during slow breathing practice. After slow breathing begins, the heart rate gradually falls and settles. Individual responses vary.](figures/p13_6.png)

This is one solid, physiological basis for the old teachers' claims that breath training calms the mind and strengthens the spirit. A fighter who can slow their breathing under pressure can think more clearly, react more accurately and recover more quickly. That is internal training in a very practical sense.

## Intention

The fifth skill is the most elusive, and the one most often described in mystical terms. Intention, in Japanese *i* or sometimes *ishiki*, means the direction of the mind. Internal martial arts consistently teach that intention should lead movement: you decide, clearly and fully, where you are going, and the body follows.

There is a real physical basis for this. The brain plans movements before they happen, preparing the postural muscles to stabilise the body in advance. When your intention is clear, this preparation is clean and the movement is efficient. When it is vague or divided, the preparation is muddled, and the movement is hesitant and weak. Athletes in many sports report the same experience: when they "see" the shot or the throw clearly, it tends to go well.

### The extended arm

The classic demonstration of intention is the "unbendable arm". Plate 13.2 shows it.

![**Plate 13.2** The extended arm test. (a) The arm is extended, relaxed but not limp. (b) A partner tries to bend it at the elbow. (c) If the student resists with muscle, the arm usually bends. (d) If the student keeps the arm relaxed and connected, it holds. (e) The attention is directed beyond the fingertips. (f) Side view, with the direction of attention shown. (g) A line of connection runs from the rear foot to the hand. (h) The student breathes out and settles.](figures/p13_2.png)

1. Extend one arm in front of you, slightly bent, with the hand open and the wrist relaxed. Let the elbow point down.
2. Your partner places one hand on top of your forearm near the wrist and the other under your elbow, and tries to bend your arm by pushing the wrist toward your shoulder.
3. First, resist by tensing your arm with all your strength. Notice what happens: usually, a strong partner can bend it.
4. Now relax your arm, keeping it extended. Imagine that your arm continues beyond your fingertips to the far wall, like a fire hose with water flowing through it. Keep your attention on the far wall, not on your partner. Breathe out and settle into your stance.
5. Your partner tries again. Most people find that the arm is now much harder to bend.

Why does this work? Several reasons combine. When you tense everything, the muscles that straighten the elbow and the muscles that bend it fight each other, and your partner is helped by the bending muscles. When you relax and "extend", the straightening muscles work smoothly without opposition. The arm is also connected through the shoulder and back to the stance, as in the arm–back line of Chapter 5. And by directing your attention past your partner, you stop reacting to them and stop "guarding" the elbow, which removes the hesitation that a partner can exploit.

None of this requires a mysterious energy. All of it requires skill. And it is a genuinely useful skill: an arm that stays extended and connected without fighting is exactly what you need to hold off an attacker in a clinch.

## Weight Underside: The Unliftable Body

The second demonstration that impressed me so much at that seminar was the "unliftable" body. Plate 13.10 shows it.

![**Plate 13.10** The "unliftable" body. (a) The partner places hands under the student's arms. (b) If the student is tense and upright, they are easily lifted. (c) Reset. (d) The student relaxes and sinks, attention in the tanden. (e) The weight drops into the feet. (f) Seen from behind. (g) The hips move very slightly back. (h) The partner finds the student much harder to lift.](figures/p13_10.png)

There are good mechanical explanations. When you are tense, your body moves as one rigid block, and your partner can lift it efficiently. When you relax completely, your body becomes floppy, so that the lift has to deal with a body that does not "come with" it. Your shoulders rise in their sockets while the rest of you stays down. If you also sink slightly and let the hips move a fraction back, the partner is lifting at a worse angle and against a lower centre of mass. Finally, as in the extended arm, your attention is directed down into the floor rather than at resisting.

The effect is surprising and well worth experiencing. It also teaches the sinking skill of Chapter 7 in a vivid way. But notice that it works best when the partner lifts under the arms. If the partner wraps their arms round your waist, the trick largely disappears. That tells you that it is a skill of relaxation and mechanics, not an energy that makes you heavier.

## What Is Not Real

Now to the third kind of demonstration. Over the years I have seen teachers claim to knock students down without touch, to stop attacks with a shout, to light candles or move objects with ki, and to be immune to strikes through internal power alone. When such demonstrations have been tested fairly, with partners who do not know the teacher and have no reason to cooperate, they have consistently failed.

I am not saying this to mock anybody. The power of expectation in a dojo is enormous. Students who have trained with a teacher for years really do respond to tiny cues they may not even notice consciously. But a skill that works only on your own students is not a martial skill. If you are shown something that seems impossible, the right response is politely to ask whether it works on a stranger who has not been told what to expect.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

I tell my students a simple rule: *if a demonstration only works on people who believe in it, it is a demonstration of belief, not of ki.* The real internal skills in this chapter work just as well on sceptics. In fact, I like testing them on sceptics best of all.
:::

## Zanshin: The Lingering Mind

Not all internal training is about force. Some of it is about awareness. *Zanshin*, often translated as "remaining mind", is the state of continued alertness after a technique is completed. Plate 13.8 shows it.

![**Plate 13.8** Zanshin after a technique. (a) The technique lands. (b) The intention is held. (c–d) The arm withdraws slowly as the guard re-forms. (e) Zanshin: guard in place, attention spread. (f) The breath settles. (g–h) The awareness extends to the left and right.](figures/p13_8.png)

Beginners finish a technique and immediately relax, mentally as well as physically. Experienced karateka finish a technique and remain ready: the guard re-forms, the stance holds, the breath settles and the attention widens to take in the whole space. This is not tension. It is a calm, expanded awareness. In kata, zanshin shows at the end of every sequence and especially at the very end, before the closing bow.

## Moving Meditation

Finally, internal training brings all these skills together in slow, attentive movement. Sanchin and Tensho can both be practised in this way, and Plate 13.11 shows the crescent step performed as walking meditation.

![**Plate 13.11** Slow walking in Sanchin dachi as moving meditation. Throughout, the attention rests in the tanden and the soles of the feet. (a) Feel both feet. (b) The weight settles. (c) One foot lightens. (d–e) It draws in and arcs out. (f) It feels the floor before taking weight. (g) It lands. (h) The weight is shared again.](figures/p13_11.png)

Walk as slowly as you possibly can, taking perhaps thirty seconds for each step. Keep your attention on the soles of your feet and the tanden. Feel the weight shift, the foot lighten, the floor arrive under the moving foot. Ten minutes of this is remarkably demanding, both physically and mentally.

Plate 13.9 shows the transition from sitting meditation to standing practice, which is how I like to begin a session focused on internal work.

![**Plate 13.9** From sitting to standing practice. (a) Mokuso. (b) The eyes lower. (c) Rising to one knee. (d) Standing. (e) Standing quietly. (f) The hands come to the centre. (g) Sanchin dachi. (h) The kata begins, with the calm of the sitting practice carried into it.](figures/p13_9.png)

### Sensing between the palms

One last exercise is a favourite of students interested in the sensations associated with ki. Plate 13.12 shows it.

![**Plate 13.12** Sensing between the palms. The open hands are held facing each other in front of the chest, and slowly brought together and apart, from touching (a) to wide (d) and back (e–g), with the attention on the sensations in the palms. (h) The arms apart.](figures/p13_12.png)

Hold your palms facing each other, a few centimetres apart, in front of your chest. Relax the shoulders and breathe slowly. Move the hands very slowly apart and together. Many people report sensations of warmth, tingling, fullness or a "magnetic" feeling between the hands. These sensations are real experiences, probably arising from increased blood flow and heightened attention to the nerve endings of the palms. Whatever their cause, the exercise trains the attention and the relaxation of the hands, and it is a pleasant, calming way to finish a session.

## Troubleshooting Internal Training

| Problem | Likely cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| "I feel nothing in the tanden" | Attention too vague; high breathing | Hands-on-belly drill; heavy ball image |
| Extended arm still bends | Arm limp, or tensing when pushed | Keep the arm alive; attention past the partner; breathe out |
| Easily lifted | Holding tension in shoulders and trunk | Body scan; sink; let shoulders rise in sockets |
| Mind wanders constantly | Normal at first | Return gently; count breaths; shorter sessions |
| Feel dizzy in slow breathing | Over-breathing | Breathe smaller, not just slower; stop if it persists |
| Bored by slow practice | No clear focus | Pick one sensation per session to explore |
| Sensations seem "imagined" | They may partly be; that is fine | Judge by results: posture, root, calm |

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- *Ki* is a traditional word for a family of real experiences; treat it as useful shorthand, not a literal force.
- Internal energy in this book means six trainable skills: centring, breath, connection, relaxation, intention and sensitivity.
- The tanden lies close to the centre of mass; attending to it settles posture, breath and movement.
- The body scan and the heavy arm test train relaxation.
- Slow breathing has measurable calming effects on the nervous system.
- The extended arm and the unliftable body are real skills with mechanical explanations.
- Demonstrations that only work on believers are not martial skills.
- Zanshin and moving meditation carry internal training into every technique.
:::

# Appendix A: Answers to Review Questions

These are model answers. Where a question asks for your own judgement, any answer that applies the principles of the chapter correctly is acceptable.

## Chapter 1

1. "The way of inhaling and exhaling is hardness and softness." It links *go* and *ju* to the breath, showing that they are phases of one cycle, not categories of technique.
2. The fist travels relaxed, the body locks at the instant of impact, then releases. The body is hardest at the moment of contact.
3. Sanchin and Tensho; Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni, created by Miyagi Chōjun around 1940.
4. Hardness is mainly skeletal alignment held together by well-timed tension; a strong person in poor posture is weak.
5. Notice, Name, Test, Adjust. For example: "My front knee drifts outward when I step."
6. Because the body is connected; several simultaneous changes hide which one mattered, and the attention cannot hold them all.
7. High blood pressure, heart disease, glaucoma, hernia, history of stroke, pregnancy (see also the safety page).
8. Near-side limbs are white; far-side limbs have a light grey wash.

## Chapter 2

1. Stacking (segments sit on one another), footings (the feet as foundations) and bracing (muscles hold angled joints).
2. Near the ear, the point of the shoulder, the hip joint, just in front of the knee and just in front of the ankle.
3. Pelvis tipped forward with an arched back (draw the tailbone down); feet turned out too far or stance too deep (reduce toe-out and depth).
4. The heel, the ball behind the big toe and the ball behind the little toe.
5. Hip- to shoulder-width; about one and a half shoulder-widths long; 60 front, 40 back.
6. Because the feet are also turned inward, so each knee still points over its own foot.
7. You should be able to lift the front foot at any moment without shifting weight.
8. Push test: a slow push to the sternum shows whether force passes to the heels. Shoulder press: pressure down through the shoulders reveals a forward head or arched back.

## Chapter 3

1. It contracts and flattens downward, enlarging the chest; the abdominal organs are pushed outward, so the belly moves.
2. Like a sealed can that holds weight because of its internal pressure, the pressurised abdomen stiffens the spine from within.
3. Forceful breath-holding against a closed throat, which raises chest pressure and blood pressure sharply. Sanchin breath keeps air always moving out through a narrowed throat.
4. The sound proves the breath is moving and not held.
5. Recovery between techniques; the breath of Tensho; a safe alternative to ibuki for those at risk.
6. Breath held or strained, or over-breathing; quieter, slower, continuous breath and about half the tension.
7. Emptying the lungs collapses the trunk pressure and posture and makes the next inhalation a gasp.
8. Engaging the pelvic floor and lower abdomen to meet the pressure from above.

## Chapter 4

1. A short front-to-back offset; shoulder-width spacing; toes turned in; knees drawn in with thighs braced.
2. Set width to the outside of the shoulders; slide the right foot forward until its heel is level with the left toes; turn the front toes in 20–30°; turn the back foot in slightly; bend and draw in the knees; share the weight.
3. It protects the groin, keeps the knees connected, keeps the centre stable and hides intent.
4. Feet on one line or stance too narrow; check width first.
5. Whether the foot skims the floor or presses and stamps.
6. The hips lead; the guard keeps its shape.
7. A partner slides a coin under the foot edges; if it passes under the heel, the weight is too far forward.
8. Tension locks in whatever structure exists; a poor stance with strong tension simply reinforces the fault.

## Chapter 5

1. A ladder with tight rungs carries load; loosen the rungs and it folds. Tension tightens the body's rungs so force travels through it.
2. Maximal effort all the time; gripping fists and jaw; holding the breath; violent shaking; a contest of straining.
3. Elbow pressed down and in, engaging the lats; test by having a partner push the fist toward the shoulder.
4. Feet, inner legs, pelvis, core, back, arms; force must be supported from the ground up.
5. Base tone (between techniques); moving tension (as the arm travels); peak (at extension or impact); release (immediately after).
6. To keep the arm–back line engaged so the arm is connected to the trunk.
7. The arm is held up by the front of the shoulder; press the elbow toward the hip and set the shoulder blades.
8. Long maximal holds raise blood pressure without adding much benefit; effort is the useful stimulus.

## Chapter 6

1. To tighten or fasten; to obtain accurate information about the soundness of the Sanchin structure.
2. Palms on the trapezius, press straight down gradually on the exhalation; lower back pain indicates a forward-tipped pelvis.
3. During exhalation the abdomen is pressurised and controlled; on inhalation the student can be winded.
4. Check: shoulders, lats, abdomen (on exhalation), thighs, forearms. Never: head, throat, spine, kidneys, groin, kneecaps.
5. The arm–back line; the hand sinks into a soft back and the shoulder jolts forward.
6. It is resistance by leaning, not rooting; when the push is removed they fall forward.
7. Months 1–2 pressing; 3–5 open slaps; 6–9 firmer slaps and light palm strikes; 10–12 soft fist to abdomen if ready.
8. Palms on belly during exhalation; fists tapping belly; hands on ribs, thighs, opposite arm, back of waist.

## Chapter 7

1. The floor pushes back with equal force; it can only do so through aligned bones and good foot contact.
2. Good even foot contact; aligned skeleton; steady connecting tension; a low, stable centre over the base.
3. A rigid body tips as a lever; a rooted body adjusts so force reaches the ground.
4. Release downward from shoulders to knees with each exhalation; a sunk posture is heavy, a perched one is held up by tension.
5. Sanchin and zenkutsu, because a foot sits behind the line of the push.
6. Sink; align; ground; hold without pushing back.
7. Whether the student was leaning: a leaning student stumbles forward when the push is suddenly removed.
8. Daily single-leg holds with a chair for support, building time, then closing the eyes.

## Chapter 8

1. Segments move in sequence from large and central to small and distal; wrong order wastes the body's mass.
2. Foot presses; leg drives; hips turn; trunk follows; arm extends; body locks; release.
3. Leading hips stretch the trunk muscles, which then contract more strongly; turning together wastes the stored energy.
4. The braced legs stop the rotation leaking away, so it all reaches the trunk.
5. The fist and the settling stance arrive together.
6. As the front foot settles and takes the weight, so the root is behind the fist.
7. Whether the root holds, whether the wrist is aligned, and whether the body is behind the fist (by the sound).
8. A soft link fails under real impact; strike targets regularly and drill the lock and the wall shove.

## Chapter 9

1. Open hands; circular paths; flowing tension; softer breath (any four from the table).
2. It rises and falls smoothly like a wave; like squeezing an accelerator on and off rather than stamping.
3. Rise; turn; press forward; turn over; press down; return.
4. Both hands circle in opposite directions around a point before the chest, then draw in and push with palms forward and thumb and fingers spread.
5. Meet, do not clash; stick; lead; counter in the same breath.
6. The hands sink with the body as the stance lowers, so the press comes from the weight and root.
7. Tension is held constantly; let it fall on every gathering movement.
8. Its breath is soft and its tension moderate, so it does not strain the cardiovascular system.

## Chapter 10

1. Grip and pulling; wrist strength; shoulder stability; leg strength and root; connection under load.
2. Anchor a 45 cm handle with crossed nails in a tub of concrete; cure a week; sand.
3. Leverage: the weight acts at the end of a long handle.
4. Fingertips and thumb on the rim; avoid leaning, lifting the shoulders or hurrying.
5. Kettlebell; sandbag or loaded rucksack; barbell; ankle weights.
6. Rest a week or two; choke up, slow the movement, reduce reps; see a physiotherapist if it persists.
7. Momentum greatly increases the load on the knee and lower back.
8. Slow rise in load and repetitions, with a lighter week every fourth week.

## Chapter 11

1. Skin, muscle, bone and nervous system.
2. Adaptation happens during recovery; training before recovery adds damage.
3. Low (downward), middle (inward), high (rising).
4. Should: the muscular part between a hand-width from wrist and elbow. Should not: wrist, elbow, bony ridge.
5. Touch and press; light strikes; medium strikes; variety of surfaces.
6. Stop until bruises clear; halve force and reps; contact muscle; tense and exhale.
7. Lasting bruises; numbness or tingling; swollen knuckles; point pain on a bone; split skin (and others in the table).
8. Strong, resilient and fully functional hands for life.

## Chapter 12

1. Poorer blood supply; muscles can generate forces the connective tissue is not yet ready for.
2. Slow movements; regular moderate load; full range; isometric holds; patience.
3. Spread, half claw, full claw, hook grip, roll in and squeeze, fan; ten times daily.
4. With rice or beans, few gentle thrusts; if joints ache, rest and return to finer material.
5. To build strength and alignment safely before full body weight is applied.
6. Band pull-aparts and wall slides.
7. It strengthens the outer hip muscles that stop the knee collapsing inward.
8. Bending forward from the hips with a long spine; it protects the lower back when lifting.

## Chapter 13

1. Centring, breath, connection, relaxation, intention, sensitivity.
2. It is near the centre of mass; attention there settles posture, lowers breathing and starts movement from the hips.
3. Body scan: release tension region by region from head to feet. Heavy arm: a partner drops your supported arm; a relaxed arm falls at once.
4. Slow breathing with a long exhalation tends to slow the heart and shift the body toward a relaxed state.
5. Relaxed extensors work without opposition, the arm is connected to the back and stance, and attention is not fighting the partner.
6. Relaxed shoulders rise in their sockets while the body stays down; waist lifts largely defeat it.
7. If it only works on believers, it demonstrates belief, not ki.
8. Continued calm alertness after a technique; it is expanded awareness, not muscular tension.

## Chapter 14

1. Goju-Ryu works at contact range, where attacks must be felt rather than seen.
2. Contact; push; yield; turn the waist; circle; push back.
3. Stick (keep contact); listen (feel force and balance); yield; turn from the centre; stay rooted.
4. The waist moves the line of force away from your centre; the arm alone lets the push follow you in.
5. Off-balancing; the weak lines run diagonally between the feet.
6. To rely on touch alone, greatly heightening sensitivity.
7. Agree a low force level, count the cycle slowly, stop and reset if either pushes harder.
8. Different bodies push differently; it prevents you becoming skilled at only a narrow game.

## Chapter 15

1. Regular practice builds and maintains habits and adaptations; occasional sessions let them fade.
2. Bow and scan; junbi undō; breathing; Sanchin; Tensho; chi-ishi or grip; self-conditioning; stretching; mokuso.
3. Foundation; build; consolidate; review (with lighter weeks).
4. Any five from the table, for example one-leg balance (balance), wall test (alignment), Sanchin hold (endurance), push test (root), grip hold (grip).
5. Higher stances; soft breathing; moderate tension; daily balance work; gentle conditioning (and others listed).
6. Heavy breath-holding, impact conditioning, shime strikes and heavy hojo undō, because their bodies are still growing.
7. Train what does not hurt; stop and seek help with sharp or worsening pain, swelling, numbness, weakness, night pain or head, chest or abdominal injury.
8. While power and speed decline, skill, sensitivity and internal quality can keep improving for decades.

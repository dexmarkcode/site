# Rooting: Ground, Weight and Structural Connection

::: chapter-intro
Rooting is the quality that makes a well-trained Goju-Ryu karateka feel as if they have grown out of the floor. It is not mysterious, and it is not simply strength. This chapter explains rooting in plain physical terms, shows you how to sink, shift and settle your weight, teaches you to receive a push and send it into the ground, and gives you a set of drills and troubleshooting tools for building a root that holds when it matters.
:::

The first person who ever really tested my root was not a karateka at all. She was a dairy farmer's daughter who had come to class with a friend, and in the push tests she simply would not move. She had never done a martial art in her life. When I asked her about it afterwards, she laughed and said that she had spent her childhood leaning against cows to move them along the milking line. "If you push a cow with your arms, you go nowhere," she said. "You have to push with the floor."

She had put into one sentence what this chapter takes several thousand words to explain. Rooting is the art of using the floor. Everything you do to stand, to resist and to strike is ultimately a conversation between your body and the ground beneath it. When that conversation is clear, you are rooted. When it is muddled by lean, tension in the wrong places or a poor stance, you are not.

## What Rooting Is

Every time you push on something, it pushes back on you with an equal force. This is Newton's third law, and it is the foundation of rooting. When you stand still, the floor pushes up on your feet with a force exactly equal to your weight. When someone pushes you, their push must be matched by an equal push from the floor through your feet, or you will move.

The floor can only push back through your feet, and it can only push back as hard as the friction and the angle of your legs allow. So a rooted body is one in which:

1. the feet are in good, even contact with the floor;
2. the skeleton is aligned so that a force arriving anywhere on the body can travel through bone to the feet without bending a joint;
3. the muscles hold that alignment steadily, without bracing the wrong joints or adding unnecessary tension;
4. the centre of mass sits low and stable over the base of the feet.

Plate 7.1 shows the idea. The floor pushes up through both legs, into the centre and on to the hands. When a partner pushes on your chest, the force is carried along a line through your body to your rear foot, and the floor absorbs it.

![**Plate 7.1** The line of support. (a) The floor pushes up through the feet. (b) The force rises through both legs. (c) It gathers at the centre. (d) It can continue to the hands. (e) A partner's push arrives at the chest. (f) In a rooted posture, it travels along a line to the rear foot. (g) The floor absorbs it. (h) Fault: leaning back against the push.](figures/p7_1.png)

### Rooting is not stiffness

A common misunderstanding is that rooting means locking the whole body rigid. In fact, rigid bodies are easy to topple. Think of a wardrobe: it is very stiff, but a firm push near the top will tip it over, because the force acts on a lever with nothing to absorb it. A tall reed, by contrast, bends but returns.

A rooted karateka is somewhere between the two. The skeleton is aligned and the connecting chains of tension are present, but the joints are not locked, and the body can make tiny adjustments to keep the line of force passing to the ground. This is why rooting is easier to feel than to see, and why it takes practice.

## Sinking: Lowering the Centre

The centre of mass of a standing adult sits roughly in the lower abdomen, a little in front of the base of the spine. This is close to the point that Japanese teachers call the tanden. The lower the centre and the wider the base beneath it, the harder it is to tip you over.

But sinking does not just mean bending the knees. It means relaxing downward, letting the weight of the body settle into the feet rather than holding it up with tension in the shoulders, chest and back. Plate 7.2 shows the process.

![**Plate 7.2** Sinking, seen from the side and front. (a) Standing tall. (b) The knees soften. (c) The hips settle down and slightly back. (d) The weight drops toward the centre. (e) The feet spread slightly under the load. (f) Rooted. (g) Front view of the rooted posture. (h) Front view of a "perched" posture, held up by tension.](figures/p7_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill: Sinking**

1. Stand in heiko dachi with your hands in a light guard.
2. Breathe in naturally. As you breathe out, let your shoulders drop, then your chest soften, then your belly settle, then your knees bend by a few centimetres. Imagine the breath flowing down through your body into the floor.
3. Feel your feet spread slightly as they take more weight. The toes rest; they do not grip.
4. Breathe in without rising. Breathe out and sink a little further.
5. Continue for five breaths. You should finish a few centimetres lower than you started, and feel noticeably heavier.
:::

The difference between a sunk posture and a "perched" one is often invisible in a photograph. You feel it immediately when you push someone. The perched student is light, springy and moves backward. The sunk student is heavy, and the push seems to disappear into the floor.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "When I sink, my thighs burn and I start to shake."**

*The problem.* As you lower your stance, your thigh muscles tire quickly and begin to tremble.

*Why it happens.* You are probably holding yourself up with the thighs alone, perhaps with the weight too far back on the heels, so the front of the thigh does all the work.

*The solution.* Sink less, at first. Let the weight settle over the middle of the feet so that the buttocks and the inner thighs share the load. Use short holds of five breaths and build up over weeks. Strengthen the legs with the squats and wall sits in Chapter 12.
:::

## Weight Shifting

A root is not a fixed thing. In movement, the weight shifts from foot to foot, and the root must shift with it. A karateka who can only root with the weight equally on both feet is easily moved the moment they step.

Plate 7.3 and Plate 7.4 show a simple weight-shifting drill.

![**Plate 7.3** Weight shifting from side to side, seen from the front. The pelvis travels sideways over the feet while the head and trunk stay upright. The dotted line marks the position of the centre.](figures/p7_3.png)

![**Plate 7.4** Weight shifting shown under the feet. The percentages give the approximate share of body weight on each foot; grey feet carry most of the weight. (a–f) From centre to ninety per cent on the left, back through the centre and to ninety per cent on the right. (g) Sanchin dachi, even. (h) Neko ashi dachi, nearly all the weight on the rear foot.](figures/p7_4.png)

1. Stand in a wide heiko dachi, feet a little wider than the hips.
2. Without leaning, move your pelvis sideways over your left foot until you feel about ninety per cent of your weight on it. Your head travels with your pelvis; it does not tip.
3. Return to the centre.
4. Repeat to the right.
5. Do ten slow shifts each way, breathing out as you move and in as you return.

The test is the unweighted foot. When you are fully over the left foot, you should be able to lift the right foot without any further movement of the body. If you have to lean to lift it, you were not fully over the left foot.

## Feeling the Floor

Rooting begins with sensation. The soles of the feet are richly supplied with nerve endings, and they constantly report the pressure, texture and slope of the floor to the brain. Many adults have dulled this sense by years of cushioned shoes. Training barefoot, as Goju-Ryu does, gradually restores it. Plate 7.11 shows eight exercises for waking up the feet.

![**Plate 7.11** Exercises for feeling the floor, shown on the left foot from above. (a) Spreading the toes. (b–e) Moving the weight to the heel, the ball, the outer edge and the inner edge. (f) Settling evenly onto the whole foot. (g) Focusing on the "bubbling spring" point just behind the ball of the foot. (h) Circling the weight slowly round the foot.](figures/p7_11.png)

Frame (g) shows a point that Chinese martial arts call the "bubbling spring", in the slight hollow just behind the ball of the foot, between the big-toe and little-toe pads. Whatever you believe about its traditional meaning, it is a useful focus for attention, because when you feel your weight centred over it, the weight is usually well spread across the tripod of the foot.

::: drill
**Drill: Circling the foot**

Stand on both feet. Slowly move your weight in a circle around your feet: forward onto the balls, across to the outer edges, back onto the heels, across to the inner edges, and forward again. Make five circles in each direction, getting smaller each time, until you settle in the centre. Do this at the start of every Sanchin practice.
:::

## Standing Practice

Many internal martial arts use long periods of motionless standing to develop the root. Goju-Ryu does too, though it is less often discussed: Sanchin itself, performed slowly, contains long held postures, and many Goju-Ryu teachers ask their students to hold Sanchin dachi or shiko dachi for minutes at a time. Plate 7.5 shows eight standing postures.

![**Plate 7.5** Standing postures for developing the root. (a–b) "Holding the ball", the arms rounded at chest height. (c–d) The hands low, near the tanden. (e–f) The Sanchin hold. (g) Shiko dachi with the Sanchin guard. (h) Shiko dachi with open, rounded arms.](figures/p7_5.png)

Begin with one or two minutes in any of these postures, breathing naturally and low. Your attention moves slowly down through the body, releasing unnecessary tension in the shoulders, chest and belly, and settling into the feet. Over weeks, build up to five and then ten minutes.

You will discover things in standing practice that you never notice in movement. Tension hides in odd places: the jaw, the toes, the backs of the hands. Small aches tell you where your alignment is slightly out. The legs tremble and then, after a while, settle. The breath slows. This is not wasted time. It is the most direct way to learn what your body is doing when it holds itself up.

## Receiving a Push

Now we put the root to work. Plate 7.6 shows the same push given to a student standing in eight different stances.

![**Plate 7.6** The same forward push applied to the chest in eight stances. Notice how the line from the point of contact to the rear foot differs in each, and how the stances that place a foot behind the line of the push (Sanchin, zenkutsu) resist it most easily.](figures/p7_6.png)

Try this yourself with a partner. You will find that some stances resist a forward push far better than others. Zenkutsu dachi and Sanchin dachi, which place a foot behind the line of the push, are strong. Shiko dachi, seen from the front with the push arriving across the line of the feet, is surprisingly weak. Shiko dachi turned sideways to the push, frame (f), is strong again. Musubi dachi, with no depth at all, is weakest of all.

The lesson is that the root depends on the direction of the force. No stance is strong in every direction. Sanchin dachi comes closest, which is part of its genius.

### Absorbing and grounding

When a push arrives, you have a fraction of a second to organise your body so that the force travels to the floor. Plate 7.7 breaks this into stages.

![**Plate 7.7** Absorbing and grounding a push in Sanchin dachi. (a) The push approaches. (b) Contact. (c) The student sinks slightly, lowering the centre. (d) The body aligns so that a straight line runs from the point of contact to the rear foot. (e) The force is passed into the floor. (f) The student holds the posture without effort. (g) The partner's push tires; the student has not moved. (h) Release.](figures/p7_7.png)

The stages happen very quickly in a skilled student, almost simultaneously. In training, slow them down.

1. **Sink.** As the push arrives, let your weight drop a few millimetres. This lowers the centre and engages the legs.
2. **Align.** Allow the body to adjust, without leaning, so that the force has a straight path from the point of contact to the floor. Usually this means the rear leg takes more of the load.
3. **Ground.** Feel the push arrive in the sole of the rear foot. If you can feel it there, you have grounded it.
4. **Hold.** Breathe. Do not push back. Let your partner do all the work.

The hardest part for most students is the last instruction. When someone pushes you, the instinct is to push back. Resist that instinct. If you push back, you are meeting force with force, and the stronger person wins. If you ground the push, it does not matter who is stronger; the floor is stronger than both of you.

## Why Leaning Fails

Plate 7.10 compares two students receiving the same push. The rooted student hardly moves at all while the push is applied, and does not move when it is taken away. The leaning student holds their position too, at first, but when the partner suddenly removes the push, they fall forward.

![**Plate 7.10** Two responses to the same push. The solid line shows the partner's force rising, held and then suddenly removed. The rooted student (dashed line) barely moves throughout. The leaning student (dotted line) holds while the push is applied, then lurches forward when it is withdrawn.](figures/p7_10.png)

This is the key test of real rooting: **remove the push suddenly**. If the student stumbles, they were resisting by leaning, not rooting. It is a test I use constantly, and I recommend that you ask your partner to use it on you.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I can resist a slow push but not a fast one."**

*The problem.* You can ground a steady push, but a sudden shove knocks you back.

*Why it happens.* Rooting a slow push gives you time to sink and align. A fast push arrives before you have organised your body, so the root must already be there.

*The solution.* Build the root into your default posture through standing practice, so that you are already sunk and aligned before anything happens. Then train with pushes that gradually increase in speed: slow for a month, moderate for the next, and only then sudden. Keep the pushes to the chest and shoulders, never the head.
:::

## Balance on One Leg

Every step involves a moment on one leg, and every kick demands it. A root that disappears when one foot leaves the floor is no root at all. Plate 7.8 shows a single-leg balance progression.

![**Plate 7.8** Single-leg balance. (a) Stand. (b) Lift the knee slowly. (c) Hold with the knee high. (d) Front view. (e) Arms wide for balance. (f) Eyes closed (advanced). (g) Sinking on the standing leg. (h) A slow kick from the balance.](figures/p7_8.png)

The standing posture is the same as in a two-footed root: knee soft, weight over the middle of the foot, trunk upright, centre low. The difference is that the base is now tiny, so every tiny error shows. Begin with ten seconds on each leg and build to a minute. When that is easy, close your eyes; this removes vision from the balance system and makes the feet and inner ear work much harder. Have a wall or chair nearby when you first try it.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

Single-leg balance is one of the best exercises there is for older students. Falls are a leading cause of injury in later life, and balance declines steadily with age unless it is trained. Ten minutes a day of the progression in Plate 7.8, holding a chair at first if necessary, makes a measurable difference within weeks. I have seen students in their seventies who could not stand on one leg for five seconds go on to perform Sanseru, with its kicks, without wobbling.
:::

## Rooting Faults

Plate 7.9 shows eight faults that weaken the root, and the troubleshooting table below suggests corrections.

![**Plate 7.9** Faults that weaken the root. (a) Perched high, with straight legs. (b) Leaning into the anticipated push. (c) Toes lifted off the floor. (d) Weight on the balls of the feet. (e) Knees pushed outward. (f) Weight shifted to one side. (g) Chest lifted and back arched. (h) Sunk too low, so the legs are overloaded.](figures/p7_9.png)

| Symptom | Likely cause | Test | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pushed back easily | Perched; centre high | Partner push | Sinking drill; soften knees |
| Falls forward when push removed | Leaning to resist | Sudden-release test | Ground the push; do not push back |
| Wobbles sideways | Weight to one side; narrow stance | Side push | Re-centre; widen to measured stance |
| Calves cramp | Weight on balls of feet | Coin test (Chapter 4) | Settle onto heels and tripod |
| Feet feel numb or "absent" | Toes gripping; poor contact | Circle the foot | Spread toes; feet exercises |
| Lower back aches under push | Chest lifted; back arched | Shoulder press | Drop ribs; draw tailbone down |
| Legs shake in standing practice | Sunk too low; weight on heels | Mirror check | Rise a little; centre the weight |
| Root vanishes in steps | No balance on one leg | Single-leg test | Plate 7.8 progression |

## How the Root Connects to Everything Else

Rooting is not a separate skill from Sanchin tension or from breathing. It is what those skills are for. The chains of tension in Chapter 5 connect the hands to the feet. The breath and core pressure of Chapter 3 make the trunk a solid link in that chain. The stance of Chapter 4 provides the footings. The root is the result when all of them work together.

It also works in both directions. The line along which a push travels from your chest to your rear foot is the same line along which force can travel from your rear foot to your hand when you strike. In the next chapter, we turn the root around and use it to generate power.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Rooting means using the floor: the ground pushes back through aligned bones.
- A rooted body has good foot contact, aligned joints, steady connecting tension and a low centre.
- Rooting is not stiffness; the joints stay unlocked and adaptable.
- Sinking is relaxing downward, not just bending the knees.
- Weight shifting moves the root from foot to foot without leaning.
- Receive a push by sinking, aligning and grounding; never push back.
- The sudden-release test reveals whether you are rooting or leaning.
- Single-leg balance is essential for stepping, kicking and lifelong health.
:::

# Kakie: Testing Internal Skill Against a Partner

::: chapter-intro
Kakie is Goju-Ryu's pushing-hands exercise. Two partners keep their wrists in contact while pushing, yielding and circling, each trying to feel and use the other's force. It is the place where everything in this book meets: the root, the connected structure, the soft hand, the breath and the quiet attention of internal training. This chapter explains the basic pattern, the principles behind it, a progression from fixed drills to free practice, and the faults that turn kakie into a shoving match.
:::

I learned kakie from a woman who ran a small dojo above a bakery. She was barely five feet tall and well into her sixties. In our first session, she put the back of her wrist against mine and said, "Push me." I pushed. My hand slid off into empty air, I lurched forward and found her other palm resting on my ribs. "Again," she said. I pushed more carefully. The same thing happened, more slowly. After ten minutes I was sweating and she had not moved her feet.

What she was doing, I eventually understood, was listening. Through the small patch of skin where our wrists touched, she could feel exactly where my force was going and where my balance was. She gave way where I was strong and moved in where I was weak. She was not stronger than me. She was simply better informed.

That is the essence of kakie. In Japanese the word is usually written with characters meaning something like "hooking hands". Whatever its exact origin, it is a method for training the sixth internal skill from Chapter 13, sensitivity, and for testing all the others under pressure from a living, unpredictable partner.

## Why Kakie Matters

Kata trains you alone. Shime tests your structure while you stand still. Kakie tests your structure, timing and sensitivity while someone is actively trying to unbalance you. It is the bridge between solo practice and fighting.

It also reflects the way Goju-Ryu expects to be used. Goju-Ryu is a close-range art. Its techniques work best when you are already in contact with an opponent: after a block, in a grab, in the clinch. At that range, there is no time to see an attack and react. You must feel it. Kakie trains that feeling.

Finally, kakie is safe. It involves no strikes in its basic forms, so it can be practised hard, for long periods, by students of almost any age and fitness. Many older Goju-Ryu teachers make kakie the centre of their practice when hard sparring is no longer sensible.

## The Basic Pattern

Plate 14.1 shows the basic one-hand kakie cycle from the side, and Plate 14.2 from an angle.

![**Plate 14.1** The basic one-hand kakie cycle, seen from the side. (a) The partners make contact with the backs of their right wrists. (b) A pushes toward B's centre; B yields. (c) B turns the waist to guide the push aside. (d) B's hand circles over. (e) B pushes; A yields. (f) A turns. (g) A's hand circles under. (h) Contact again, and the cycle repeats.](figures/p14_1.png)

![**Plate 14.2** The same cycle in three-quarter view, showing how the contact point travels in a flat circle between the partners.](figures/p14_2.png)

1. **Position.** Face your partner, both in Sanchin dachi with the right foot forward, close enough that your forearms meet at about the middle of the space between you. Plate 14.3 shows the foot positions from above.
2. **Contact.** Raise your right hands and touch the backs of your wrists together, the arms bent, the elbows low. Rest your left hands lightly in front of your bodies, ready.
3. **A pushes.** Partner A turns the wrist and pushes slowly, with an open hand, toward B's chest or shoulder.
4. **B yields.** Partner B does not resist. B lets the arm bend, keeps contact and turns the waist slightly, so that A's push is guided away from the centre.
5. **B circles and pushes.** As A's push runs out, B's hand circles over A's wrist and B begins to push back along the same path.
6. **A yields.** A now yields, turns and circles under, and the cycle continues.

![**Plate 14.3** Foot positions for kakie, seen from above. One partner's feet are solid, the other's dotted. (a) Both partners right foot forward. (b) The wrists meet at the centre, marked ×. (c) One partner steps in while the other steps back. (d) The same with the left foot forward.](figures/p14_3.png)

At first, the cycle is slow, smooth and cooperative. Each partner gives the other a clear, steady push and yields cleanly. The aim is to learn the shape and to keep the contact light and continuous: neither pressing hard nor losing touch.

## The Principles of Kakie

Kakie can be practised for decades, and its depth comes from a handful of principles that sound simple and take years to master.

### Stick

Never lose contact. The wrists stay together through every push, yield and circle. If contact breaks, you have lost your source of information. The pressure at the contact point should be light, about the weight of a small apple resting on your wrist.

### Listen

Use the contact to feel what your partner is doing: the direction of their push, its strength, its timing and, most importantly, the state of their balance. A good kakie player knows when their partner is about to push before the push begins, because they feel the slight change in tension at the wrist.

### Yield

When pushed, do not resist with strength. Give way with the arm, then with the waist. Yielding is not collapsing. The structure stays connected and the root stays in the floor, but the point of contact moves out of the way of the force.

### Turn from the centre

The most important yield comes from the waist, not the arm. Plate 14.4 shows how a small turn of the hips can guide a strong push harmlessly past.

![**Plate 14.4** Listening and yielding. (a) The partners are in contact, listening. (b) B's push arrives. (c) A turns the waist. (d) The push slides past A's centre. (e) B over-extends. (f) A takes the centre line. (g) A returns the push into B's weakened structure. (h) Reset.](figures/p14_4.png)

If you yield only with the arm, the push follows you in and arrives at your body anyway. If you turn the waist, the line of the push moves away from your centre, and your partner pushes into empty space. As their push over-extends, their balance moves forward, and you are perfectly placed to take the centre line yourself.

### Root

All this yielding is only possible if you are rooted. If your stance is weak, a push will move your feet, however skilfully you turn. Kakie is therefore an excellent test of the rooting skills of Chapter 7. You will discover very quickly whether your root survives movement and pressure.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "Kakie turns into a pushing contest."**

*The problem.* You and your partner both push harder and harder, neither yielding, until one of you is shoved backward.

*Why it happens.* Under pressure, the instinct to resist force with force takes over. Each partner's resistance provokes more force from the other.

*The solution.* Agree to work at a fixed, low level of force: say, a quarter of your strength. Count the cycles aloud for the first few minutes to keep the rhythm slow. If either partner starts to push harder, stop, reset and begin again. Remember that the person who wins a pushing contest has learned nothing about kakie.
:::

## Finding the Weak Line

Every stance is strong in some directions and weak in others, as we saw in Chapter 7. In kakie, you are constantly searching for the direction in which your partner's root is weakest. Plate 14.10 shows the idea from above.

![**Plate 14.10** Off-balancing in kakie, seen from above. (a) Both partners balanced. (b) Partner A has moved most of the weight onto the front foot. (c) B leads A's push further forward, beyond the front foot. (d) The weak line of A's stance, at an angle between the feet.](figures/p14_10.png)

In Sanchin dachi, the weak lines run diagonally, at an angle between the two feet, rather than straight forward, back or to the side. A push along a weak line moves the partner's centre of mass outside their base, and they must step or fall. In kakie, you do not need to push hard along that line. You need to feel when your partner's weight is moving toward it and add a little to the movement at exactly the right moment.

This is what the Japanese call *kuzushi*, off-balancing. It is the foundation of every throw, takedown and joint lock in the classical kata, and kakie is where you learn to feel it.

## A Progression

Plate 14.7 shows a typical progression through the stages of kakie training.

![**Plate 14.7** A progression in kakie training, showing the approximate month in which each stage is introduced for a student training two or three times a week.](figures/p14_7.png)

### Stage one: the fixed pattern

Practise the basic cycle slowly and cooperatively, right hand and left hand, until it is smooth and continuous. Five minutes on each side.

### Stage two: slow free practice

The cycle becomes less predictable. Either partner may push at any point, in any direction toward the centre, but slowly and with light force. The other partner must feel the push and yield appropriately.

### Stage three: eyes closed

Practising with the eyes closed removes vision and forces you to rely entirely on touch. Plate 14.8 shows the drill.

![**Plate 14.8** Sticking hands with the eyes closed. The partner leads the contact (b) up, (c) down, (d) out, (e) in, (f) forward and (g) back, while the other follows without losing touch. (h) Still, listening for the next movement.](figures/p14_8.png)

One partner closes their eyes. The other moves the contact point slowly in any direction, and the first follows, keeping the pressure light and constant. Swap after two minutes. Then both partners keep their eyes closed and practise the basic cycle. Most students are astonished at how much more they feel.

### Stage four: adding steps

Plate 14.12 shows kakie with stepping. One partner advances with the crescent step while pushing; the other retreats while yielding, then they reverse.

![**Plate 14.12** Kakie with stepping. (a) Start. (b–d) A advances while B gives way, keeping contact. (e) The direction turns. (f–g) B advances while A gives way. (h) Back to the centre.](figures/p14_12.png)

Stepping adds a whole new layer of difficulty, because you must keep your root, your contact and your sensitivity while moving your feet. It is also much closer to real fighting.

### Stage five: two hands

In two-hand kakie, both partners' hands are in contact. Plate 14.9 shows the basic positions.

![**Plate 14.9** Two-hand kakie in three-quarter view. (a) Both wrists in contact. (b–c) Alternate pushes. (d–e) One hand high and one low. (f) Hands close. (g) Hands wide. (h) Settle.](figures/p14_9.png)

With two points of contact, you receive twice the information and face twice the problems. One hand may push while the other pulls, one may circle while the other stays still. The hands can also trap, pull and open lines, which leads naturally into the next stage.

### Stage six: light application

Once the cycle is smooth and sensitive, you can begin to introduce the techniques of the kata at low speed and force. Plate 14.5 and Plate 14.11 show two examples.

![**Plate 14.5** From kakie to control. (a) Contact. (b) A feels B's push. (c) A's hand turns over B's wrist. (d) A grasps the wrist. (e) A draws B in. (f) A's other hand moves to B's elbow. (g) A presses down. (h) B is controlled.](figures/p14_5.png)

![**Plate 14.11** From kakie to strike. (a) Contact. (b) A absorbs B's push. (c) A redirects it. (d) A traps B's arm. (e) A's other hand begins to strike. (f) A palm strike to the chest. (g) Hold. (h) Reset.](figures/p14_11.png)

In both, the technique is not forced. It appears because the partner's push has given you the opportunity. You feel the push, yield to it, and as their balance moves past its limit you take control of the arm or strike into the opening. These applications should be practised slowly and gently, with care for your partner's joints. The aim is to recognise the moment, not to hurt anybody.

### Stage seven: free kakie

Eventually, kakie becomes a free exercise in which both partners push, yield, trap, pull and off-balance at will, within agreed limits of force and without strikes. It is a game of skill, played at close range, in which the winner is the one who feels more and reacts more appropriately. Good free kakie between experienced students looks almost lazy from the outside. From the inside, it is intensely demanding.

## Faults in Kakie

Plate 14.6 shows eight common faults.

![**Plate 14.6** Faults in kakie. (a) Leaning into the push. (b) The elbow lifted, disconnecting the arm. (c) The hands clenched. (d) The arm locked straight. (e) Standing too tall. (f) Bending backward to escape a push. (g) Watching the hands. (h) Losing contact.](figures/p14_6.png)

| Fault | Effect | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Leaning in | Easily pulled forward | Keep the trunk upright; push from the root |
| Elbow lifted | Arm disconnects from back; easily pushed aside | Elbows heavy, as in Sanchin |
| Clenched hands | Tension blocks sensitivity | Open, alive hands; light contact |
| Arm locked straight | Cannot yield; elbow vulnerable | Keep a slight bend at all times |
| Standing tall | Root lost | Sink; knees bent and braced |
| Bending back | Balance lost; pushed over easily | Turn the waist instead of leaning |
| Watching the hands | Head drops; slow reactions | Eyes on partner's chest; feel, don't look |
| Losing contact | No information | Stick; follow the partner |
| Pushing hard | Contest replaces practice | Agree a low force level |

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I can't feel anything through my wrist."**

*The problem.* Your partner tells you to listen, but all you feel is a vague pressure. You have no idea what they are about to do.

*Why it happens.* Tension dulls sensitivity. If your arm, shoulder or hand is tense, the nerves are swamped by the signals from your own muscles. Pressing too hard has the same effect.

*The solution.* Lighten the contact until it is barely there. Do the body scan of Chapter 13 before you start, paying special attention to the shoulder and hand. Practise the eyes-closed drill. Most students begin to feel clear signals within a few weeks once their tension drops.
:::

## Kakie Etiquette

Because kakie is a partner exercise that can become competitive, its etiquette matters.

- Bow before and after, and agree the level of force before you begin.
- Never strike in kakie unless both partners have agreed to practise applications, and then only at the agreed level.
- Apply joint locks slowly and release them immediately when your partner taps.
- If you are much stronger or more experienced than your partner, work at their level. Your job is to help them learn.
- If you are pushed out of your stance, acknowledge it. The point of kakie is to discover your weaknesses, not hide them.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

Some of my most useful lessons in kakie came from beginners. A beginner pushes in unexpected directions, with odd timing, and does not follow the patterns that experienced students fall into. If you only practise kakie with people of your own level, you will become very good at a narrow game. Seek out partners who are different from you: heavier, lighter, younger, older, stiffer and softer.
:::

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Kakie is Goju-Ryu's pushing-hands exercise; it trains sensitivity and tests structure under live pressure.
- The basic cycle: contact, push, yield, turn, circle, push back.
- The principles: stick, listen, yield, turn from the centre, stay rooted.
- The strongest yield comes from turning the waist, not the arm.
- Every stance has weak lines; kakie teaches you to feel and use them (kuzushi).
- Progress from fixed patterns to free practice over many months, with eyes-closed and stepping stages.
- Keep force low; a pushing contest teaches nothing.
:::

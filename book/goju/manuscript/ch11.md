# Kote Kitae and Body Conditioning: Progressive Impact Training

::: chapter-intro
Impact conditioning is the part of Goju-Ryu that most fascinates outsiders and most worries doctors. Done properly, it toughens the skin, muscles and bones, teaches the body to accept contact calmly and makes your blocks and strikes far more effective. Done badly, it causes bruising, nerve damage and joint problems. This chapter explains how conditioning actually works in the body, then takes you through forearm, leg, body and makiwara conditioning step by step, with a cautious year-long progression and a clear set of warning signs.
:::

At my first proper Goju-Ryu summer course, a group of senior students from another dojo invited me to do *kote kitae*, forearm conditioning, with them. I was keen to impress. Twenty minutes later my forearms were swollen, purple from wrist to elbow, and so sore that I could barely hold a cup of tea that evening. I could not train properly for a week. One of the seniors came over to me the next morning, looked at my arms and said, quite kindly, "That's not conditioning. That's just damage."

He was right, and I have never forgotten it. Conditioning is a slow, patient process of adaptation. It works by asking the body to cope with slightly more than it is used to, then giving it time to rebuild stronger. Bruising, swelling and lasting pain are signs that you have asked too much. They do not speed the process up. They slow it down.

## How Conditioning Works

The body adapts to the loads placed on it. This principle, sometimes called *Wolff's law* for bone and more generally the principle of *progressive overload*, underlies all physical training. Muscles grow stronger when they are worked a little harder than usual. Bone becomes denser where it is loaded. Skin thickens where it is rubbed. Tendons and ligaments become stiffer and stronger, though much more slowly.

Impact conditioning applies this principle to contact. Four kinds of adaptation seem to be involved.

**Skin.** Repeated light friction and pressure make the skin tougher and less likely to split or bruise.

**Muscle.** Muscles that are repeatedly struck while tensed become better at contracting quickly to protect the tissues beneath. Tensed muscle also spreads the force of a blow over a wider area.

**Bone.** Bone responds to repeated, moderate loading by laying down more mineral and becoming denser and stronger. This takes months to years and needs moderate, regular stimulus, not occasional heavy battering.

**Nervous system.** Perhaps most important of all, the brain learns that contact is not dangerous. The flinch reflex dampens, and the sensation of impact, which at first feels alarming, becomes ordinary. Much of the "toughness" of a conditioned karateka is in the head.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

There is a popular idea that conditioning works by creating tiny fractures in the bone that heal stronger. This is at best a simplification. Bone does strengthen in response to load, but microfractures that are not given time to heal accumulate into stress fractures. Never try to "break down" a bone to build it up. Load it moderately and regularly, and let it adapt.
:::

### The rule of recovery

Adaptation happens during rest, not during training. The training session is the stimulus; the next day or two is when the body rebuilds. If you condition again before recovery is complete, you are adding new damage on top of old, and the tissues get weaker, not stronger.

For this reason, I recommend that impact conditioning of any one area is done no more than two or three times a week, with at least one full day between sessions, and that any session that leaves significant bruising is followed by a longer break.

## Kote Kitae: Forearm Conditioning

*Kote kitae*, "forearm forging", is the classic Goju-Ryu conditioning drill. Two partners face each other and strike their forearms together in a set pattern, usually three contacts: low, middle and high. Plate 11.1 shows the sequence from the side and Plate 11.2 from an angle.

![**Plate 11.1** Three-point forearm conditioning (sanbon kote kitae), seen from the side. (a) Ready. (b–c) The right forearms swing down and meet low, outer forearm to outer forearm. (d–e) They swing up and meet at the middle level. (f–g) They swing over and meet high. (h) Reset.](figures/p11_1.png)

![**Plate 11.2** The same sequence seen from a three-quarter angle, showing how the forearms meet near the centre line between the partners.](figures/p11_2.png)

1. **Position.** Face your partner in Sanchin dachi, right foot forward, close enough that your forearms meet in the middle when your arms are bent at the elbow at about ninety degrees.
2. **Low contact.** Swing your right forearm down and slightly outward, as in a downward block, so that the outer, fleshy side of your forearm meets the same part of your partner's forearm.
3. **Middle contact.** Swing the forearm up, turning it, so that the inner forearms meet at the middle level, as in an inward block.
4. **High contact.** Swing the forearm up and over, so that the outer forearms meet at head height, as in a rising block.
5. **Repeat.** Continue the cycle for a set number of repetitions, then change to the left arm.

### Where to make contact

The contact should be on the thick, muscular part of the forearm, between about a hand-width below the elbow and a hand-width above the wrist. Plate 11.11 shows the safe areas.

![**Plate 11.11** Conditioning surfaces. (a) The muscular part of the forearm. (b) The muscle of the thigh. (c) The abdomen. (d) Joints and the wrist bones must be avoided.](figures/p11_11.png)

Avoid the wrist, where small bones, tendons and nerves lie close to the surface, and the elbow. Avoid striking bone on bone along the sharp ridge of the ulna, the bone on the little-finger side of the forearm; that is painful and does little good. Turn the forearm slightly so that the muscle takes the contact.

### Footwork

Plate 11.12 shows the foot positions for partner conditioning from above.

![**Plate 11.12** Foot positions for partner forearm conditioning, seen from above. One partner's feet are solid, the other's dotted. (a) Facing in Sanchin dachi, right foot forward. (b) The forearms meet at the centre. (c) Changing sides. (d) Stepping closer for a shorter range.](figures/p11_12.png)

The partners stand offset, so that the forearms meet on the centre line between them. As the drill becomes familiar, they can step forward and back together with the crescent step, which turns kote kitae into a moving exercise that also trains timing and distance.

### Progression

**Weeks one to four.** Very light contact, a touch more than a tap. Ten repetitions of the three-point cycle on each arm. The aim is to learn the pattern and the contact points, not to condition.

**Months two to three.** Light contact. Twenty repetitions per arm. You should feel a mild sting that fades within a minute or two.

**Months four to six.** Light to moderate contact. Thirty repetitions per arm. A little redness that fades within an hour is acceptable; bruising is not.

**After six months.** Moderate contact, up to fifty repetitions per arm, two or three times a week. Many experienced students never go beyond this. There is no need.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My forearms bruise badly after kote kitae."**

*The problem.* After forearm conditioning, you have dark bruises that last several days.

*Why it happens.* The force is too great for your current level of adaptation, the contact is on bone rather than muscle, or you are not tensing the forearm at the moment of contact.

*The solution.* Stop forearm conditioning until the bruises have completely gone. When you resume, halve the force and the number of repetitions. Turn the forearm so that the muscle takes the contact. Tense the forearm slightly and breathe out at each contact. Ask your partner to match your level, not theirs. If you bruise very easily even with light contact, or the bruises are unusual, see a doctor; some medical conditions and medicines increase bruising.
:::

## Solo Forearm Conditioning

If you have no partner, you can condition the forearms against a padded post. Plate 11.4 shows the method.

![**Plate 11.4** Solo forearm conditioning on a padded post. (a) The arm draws back. (b) The outer forearm strikes. (c) Draw back. (d) The lower forearm strikes. (e) Rising. (f) The inner forearm strikes. (g) Repeat. (h) Guard.](figures/p11_4.png)

Wrap a post, a tree or a makiwara with a thick layer of rope, carpet or foam, and strike it lightly with the different surfaces of the forearm in turn, using the same blocking movements as in kote kitae. A padded post is less forgiving than a partner's arm, which gives slightly, so start even more gently. Many students find it helpful to strike a heavy bag first, which is softer still.

## Leg Conditioning

The thighs and shins are conditioned in a similar way, with a partner making light contact with the shin to the large muscles of the thigh. Plate 11.3 shows the drill.

![**Plate 11.3** Leg conditioning with a partner. (a) Ready. (b) The partner's knee lifts. (c) The shin makes light contact with the front of the thigh. (d) Reset. (e) The outer thigh. (f) The front thigh, from an angle. (g) Roles swap. (h) Bow and change.](figures/p11_3.png)

The receiving partner stands in Sanchin dachi, tensing the thigh and breathing out at each contact. The giving partner makes light, controlled contact with the lower shin or the top of the foot, never the knee or the toes. Start with five contacts per leg and very light force.

The knee joint, the inside of the knee and the area just above the kneecap must never be struck. A careless kick that lands on the knee can damage the ligaments or the cartilage, and these injuries can last for years.

## Body Conditioning

### Self-conditioning

The simplest body conditioning is done alone, by slapping and tapping your own muscles. Plate 11.5 shows a sequence.

![**Plate 11.5** Self-conditioning by slapping and tapping. (a–b) Slapping each forearm. (c) The chest. (d) Fists tapping the abdomen. (e) The ribs, with open hands. (f) The thighs. (g) The lower back, very lightly. (h) The shins.](figures/p11_5.png)

Breathe out and tense the area as you slap it. Work from the arms to the chest, abdomen, sides, thighs and shins. The force is light: this is as much about waking up the tissues and getting used to contact as about conditioning. It is an excellent way to finish Sanchin practice, and it is safe for nearly everyone.

### Abdominal conditioning with a partner

Plate 11.6 shows a progression for conditioning the abdomen with a partner. It follows the same principles as the abdominal shime of Chapter 6.

![**Plate 11.6** Abdominal conditioning with a partner. (a) The partner's palm rests on the abdomen. (b) It presses as the student exhales. (c) A fist rests on the abdomen. (d) A light push. (e) The upper abdomen. (f) A light tap. (g) The sides, with the palm only. (h) Recovery breathing.](figures/p11_6.png)

The partner always presses or strikes during the exhalation, when the abdomen is firm and the breath is controlled. The force begins as a press and progresses, over many months, to light and then moderate taps. A medicine ball dropped from a low height onto the tensed abdomen, while lying on the back, is a useful alternative; start from a few centimetres.

The same exclusions apply as for shime: no abdominal conditioning during pregnancy, after recent abdominal surgery, with a hernia, or for children.

## Makiwara Conditioning

In Chapter 8 we used the makiwara as a feedback tool for power. It is also the main tool for conditioning the striking surfaces of the hand, particularly the two large knuckles. Plate 11.7 shows a progression.

![**Plate 11.7** A makiwara progression. (a) Touching the pad to check alignment. (b) Pressing and holding for three seconds. (c) A light strike. (d) A medium strike. (e) The palm heel. (f) A close-range strike. (g) A rising strike. (h) Resting the hand.](figures/p11_7.png)

**Stage one: touch and press.** For the first two weeks, simply place your knuckles on the pad and press, checking that the wrist is straight and that the two large knuckles take the pressure. Hold for three seconds, release. Twenty presses per hand.

**Stage two: light strikes.** Strike lightly, ten to twenty times per hand, focusing on alignment and the lock at impact.

**Stage three: medium strikes.** Gradually increase the force over months, never to the point of splitting the skin or causing swelling. Thirty to fifty strikes per hand, two or three times a week.

**Stage four: variety.** Add the palm heel, the hammer fist, close-range strikes and rising strikes. Each conditions a different surface and a different angle.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My knuckles are swollen and stiff after the makiwara."**

*The problem.* The day after makiwara practice, your knuckles are puffy, and you find it hard to close your hand fully.

*Why it happens.* The joints of the knuckles are inflamed. This is the result of too many strikes, too much force or poor alignment so that force goes into the smaller knuckles.

*The solution.* Stop makiwara training until the swelling and stiffness have completely gone, usually a few days. When you resume, reduce the volume by half and check your fist formation (Chapter 5) and wrist alignment. Use the after-care routine below. Persistent swelling of the knuckles is a warning sign of joint damage. Do not train through it.
:::

## After-Care

Traditional Okinawan karateka used herbal liniments, known in Chinese martial arts as *dit da jow*, to rub into conditioned areas. Their effectiveness has not been well studied, and I do not recommend any particular product. What clearly does help is gentle massage, movement and time. Plate 11.9 shows a simple after-care routine.

![**Plate 11.9** After-care following conditioning. (a–b) Stroking each forearm firmly from the wrist toward the elbow. (c) Shaking the arms out. (d) Raising the arms above the head. (e) Pressing the palms together. (f) Stretching the wrists. (g) Gently closing and opening the fists. (h) Resting.](figures/p11_9.png)

Stroke the conditioned area firmly toward the heart, which helps fluid drain from the tissues. Move the joints gently through their full range. Raise the arms above the head for a minute to reduce swelling. Rest. If an area is particularly sore, a cold pack wrapped in a cloth for ten minutes can ease it.

## The Year-Long View

Plate 11.8 summarises a cautious year-long progression for the three main forms of impact conditioning.

![**Plate 11.8** A year-long progression for impact conditioning. Forearm conditioning begins first, at a light level. Makiwara work begins a month or two later. Leg and body conditioning begin last and progress most slowly. None goes beyond a moderate-to-firm level in the first year.](figures/p11_8.png)

Notice how slowly each line rises. By the end of a year, a student following this plan will be able to perform kote kitae at a moderate level, strike the makiwara firmly with sound hands and take light contact to the legs and body. That is a very good result. Students who try to reach it in a month usually spend the following months recovering from injuries instead.

## Faults and Warning Signs

Plate 11.10 shows eight common faults.

![**Plate 11.10** Faults in conditioning. (a) Contact on the wrist bones. (b) Contact at the elbow. (c) Flinching forward. (d) Being struck during the inhalation. (e) A bent wrist on the makiwara. (f) Leaning into the post. (g) A kick landing on the knee. (h) Training on hands that are already bruised.](figures/p11_10.png)

The following signs mean you must stop conditioning that area and allow it to recover. If they persist or are severe, see a doctor.

| Warning sign | Possible meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Bruising lasting more than a few days | Too much force | Rest; halve force when resuming |
| Numbness or tingling in the hand or fingers | Nerve irritation | Stop at once; seek advice if it persists |
| Swollen, stiff knuckles | Joint inflammation | Stop makiwara; rest until normal |
| Pain at a precise point on a bone | Possible stress fracture | Stop; seek medical advice |
| Split skin | Too much friction or force | Rest; cover; reduce volume |
| Pain in the wrist or elbow joint | Poor alignment; overload | Check technique; rest |
| Lumps or hard swellings | Haematoma or other injury | Seek medical advice |

### A word about long-term health

There are stories of old karate masters with enormous, misshapen knuckles and hands like gnarled roots. Some of these stories are true, and some of those hands were the result of decades of excessive conditioning that left their owners with arthritis and limited movement in old age. The aim is not to look like that. The aim is hands and arms that are strong, resilient and fully functional for the rest of your life. A conditioned hand should still be able to play the piano, button a shirt and hold a baby.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Conditioning works by progressive overload followed by recovery; damage slows it down.
- Skin, muscle, bone and the nervous system all adapt, at different speeds.
- Condition each area no more than two or three times a week, with recovery days between.
- In kote kitae, contact the muscular part of the forearm; avoid wrists, elbows and bone ridges.
- Never strike the knee joint in leg conditioning.
- Abdominal conditioning is always done on the exhalation, and never during pregnancy or with a hernia.
- Progress makiwara training from presses to light, then medium strikes over months.
- Stop at bruising, numbness, joint swelling or pain at a point on a bone.
:::

# Hojo Undo: The Traditional Tools and How to Use Them Safely

::: chapter-intro
Hojo undō, "supplementary exercise", is the strength-training tradition of Goju-Ryu. Its stone weights, clay jars and iron rings were designed to build exactly the kind of strength the kata demand: a crushing grip, strong wrists, robust shoulders and legs that stay rooted under load. This chapter explains the purpose of each tool, how to make or choose one, the core exercises step by step, how to progress safely, and what to do when something goes wrong.
:::

The first time I picked up a chi-ishi, I was rather offended by it. It looked like a garden tool, a short wooden handle with a lump of concrete on the end, and it weighed less than a bag of sugar. My teacher asked me to hold it out in front of me, handle upright, and slowly lower the weight forward until the handle was horizontal, then raise it again. By the fifth repetition my forearm was burning. By the tenth, I could not keep the handle steady. The bag of sugar had become very heavy indeed, because it was on the end of a lever forty-five centimetres long.

That is the genius of the old tools. They are cheap, simple and often light, but they load the body in awkward, specific ways that ordinary weights do not. They work the grip, the wrists and the small stabilising muscles of the shoulders, and they demand that you stay in a good stance while you use them. They build strength that belongs to karate, not just strength in general.

## The Purpose of Hojo Undo

Every tool in the hojo undō tradition answers a specific need of the art.

**Gripping and pulling.** Goju-Ryu is a close-range style full of grabs, pulls and joint locks. The jars, stone padlocks and chi-ishi develop the fingers, hands and forearms for this work.

**Wrist and forearm strength.** Blocking with the forearm, striking with the palm or wrist, and controlling an opponent's arm all demand strong, stable wrists.

**Shoulder stability.** The guard of Sanchin and the circling hands of Tensho put the shoulders under constant load. The chi-ishi and kongoken strengthen the deep muscles that hold the shoulder joint together.

**Leg strength and root.** Iron sandals, stance work while carrying jars and squats with the tan build the legs that a deep, stable root requires.

**Connection.** Perhaps most importantly, nearly every hojo undō exercise is performed in a karate stance with karate breathing. The tools teach you to stay connected and rooted while the body is loaded, which is exactly what fighting requires.

## Before You Start

Hojo undō is resistance training, and the usual rules of sensible resistance training apply.

- **Warm up.** Ten minutes of junbi undō (Chapter 2) and some light Sanchin breathing.
- **Start light.** Lighter than you think. The leverage of the chi-ishi and the grip demands of the jars catch everyone out.
- **Breathe.** Exhale on the effort, inhale on the return, never hold the breath.
- **Stay in your stance.** If the stance breaks, the load is too heavy.
- **Check the equipment.** Before every session, check that heads are firmly fixed to handles, that jar rims are not chipped and that there is clear space around you.
- **Stop at pain.** Muscle fatigue is expected. Sharp pain in a joint, tendon or the back is not.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

The traditional tools can be dangerous to the people around you. A chi-ishi head that comes off its handle in mid-swing becomes a missile. A dropped jar breaks into sharp pieces. Train with enough space, check your equipment every time, and never swing a tool near another person. In my dojo, nobody uses a chi-ishi within two metres of anyone else.
:::

## Chi-ishi: The Strength Stone

The *chi-ishi* is a weight on the end of a handle. Traditionally the weight was a stone with a wooden handle set into it; today most are made by setting a broom handle or length of dowel into concrete in a small plastic tub or tin. Some have a small knob at the far end of the handle as well.

### Making a chi-ishi

1. Cut a hardwood dowel or broom handle to about 45 cm.
2. Drive two or three long nails or screws through the bottom 5 cm of the handle, crossing each other, to anchor it in the concrete.
3. Place the handle upright in a small, round plastic container, about 10–12 cm across.
4. Fill the container with concrete, check that the handle is vertical, and leave it for at least a week to cure fully.
5. Sand the handle smooth, and wrap the grip with tape if you like.

A head of about one to one and a half kilograms is plenty for a beginner. Most experienced students use two to three kilograms. More is rarely needed, because the leverage multiplies the effect enormously.

### Basic chi-ishi exercises

Plate 10.1 shows the fundamental chi-ishi exercises, performed in shiko dachi with the other hand in the chamber.

![**Plate 10.1** Basic chi-ishi exercises in shiko dachi, in three-quarter view. (a) The chi-ishi hangs at the side. (b) It is raised. (c) It is held vertical in front. (d) The weight is lowered forward. (e) The handle is level. (f) It is raised back to vertical. (g) It is turned outward. (h) It is turned inward.](figures/p10_1.png)

**Forward lowering.** Hold the chi-ishi upright in front of you at chest height, elbow bent and low, weight at the top. Breathe out and slowly lower the weight forward by bending the wrist, until the handle is level or slightly lower. Breathe in and slowly raise it back to vertical. Eight to ten times per hand.

**Wrist turns.** From the upright position, slowly turn the handle outward to the side, then back to vertical, then inward across the body. The forearm rotates, the elbow stays still. Eight to ten each way.

These two exercises alone, done three times a week for six months, will transform the strength of your wrists and forearms.

### Shoulder and punching exercises

Plate 10.2 shows more advanced exercises that work the shoulder and the punch.

![**Plate 10.2** Chi-ishi exercises for the shoulder and the punch. (a) Chamber. (b) A slow punch with the handle upright. (c–d) Lifting the chi-ishi behind the shoulder and lowering it down the back. (e) Return. (f–g) The arm out to the side, the weight lowered outward. (h) Chamber.](figures/p10_2.png)

**The slow punch.** Hold the chi-ishi at the chamber, with the handle forward. Punch slowly, rotating the forearm as in Sanchin, so that the handle ends upright at full extension. Return slowly. This loads every link of the arm–back line of Chapter 5.

**Over the shoulder.** Lift the chi-ishi up beside the head and let the weight lower slowly down behind the back, with the elbow pointing up. Raise it again. This stretches and strengthens the back of the shoulder and the triceps. Go carefully; it is easy to lower too fast.

**To the side.** Hold the arm out to the side with the handle upright, then lower the weight outward until the handle is horizontal. This is hard work for the shoulder; start with very few repetitions.

### Progressing safely

Plate 10.10 shows a sensible year-long progression for the chi-ishi.

![**Plate 10.10** A year-long chi-ishi progression. The head weight (solid line) rises slowly from about one kilogram, with a lighter week every fourth week to allow recovery. Repetitions (dashed line) increase gradually alongside.](figures/p10_10.png)

The principle is slow, steady progress with regular easier weeks. You do not need several chi-ishi of different weights: holding the handle further up, nearer the head, makes the same chi-ishi much lighter. Plate 10.11 shows this, along with the common faults.

(e) Correct: elbow low and close. (f) Correct: a slow, controlled lowering. (g) Holding the handle closer to the head to reduce the load. ::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My elbow aches after chi-ishi."**

*The problem.* After chi-ishi practice, you feel an ache on the outer or inner side of the elbow, sometimes for days.

*Why it happens.* The tendons that attach the forearm muscles at the elbow are being overloaded, often by too much weight, too many repetitions or jerky movement. This is the same problem as "tennis elbow" or "golfer's elbow".

*The solution.* Rest the arm from chi-ishi for a week or two. When you return, choke up on the handle to halve the load, slow every movement to a count of four down and four up, and cut the repetitions. Add the slow wrist curls in Chapter 12. If the pain persists for more than a few weeks, see a physiotherapist.
:::

## Nigiri-game: The Gripping Jars

The *nigiri-game* are a pair of jars, held by their rims with the fingertips, that are carried while walking in Sanchin dachi or held in various positions. They build a powerful pinch grip, and they train the whole body to stay rooted and connected while the arms are loaded.

### Choosing jars

Traditional jars are earthenware with a narrow neck and a lip around the rim. Today many people use plastic or metal containers with a similar shape. The important thing is a rim you can grip securely between the fingertips and thumb. Start with empty jars, then add sand or water a little at a time over months. A beginner might start at under a kilogram per jar; strong, experienced students may work with three kilograms or more.

### Walking with the jars

Plate 10.3 shows the core exercise: walking forward in Sanchin dachi, using the crescent step, while carrying the jars.

![**Plate 10.3** Walking in Sanchin with the nigiri-game. (a) The jars are gripped by their rims at the sides. (b–f) The crescent step is made as in Chapter 4, with the stance and posture unchanged. (g) The foot lands. (h) The stance settles.](figures/p10_3.png)

1. Grip each jar by its rim with the fingertips and thumb. Do not hook the fingers inside the neck.
2. Stand in Sanchin dachi with the arms hanging at the sides, shoulders down.
3. Step forward with the crescent step, breathing in time with the movement.
4. Keep the posture exactly as it is without the jars: upright, knees braced, centre low.
5. Walk five to ten steps, turn and walk back.

As the grip tires, the temptation is to lean, lift the shoulders or hurry. Resist all three. If you cannot maintain your posture, put the jars down and rest.

### Arm positions

Once walking with the jars at your sides is comfortable, you can hold them in different positions to load the shoulders. Plate 10.4 shows eight.

![**Plate 10.4** Holding positions with the nigiri-game. (a) At the sides. (b) Forward and low. (c) Forward at shoulder level. (d) Out to the sides. (e) Overhead. (f) Behind. (g) In the Sanchin guard position. (h) Hugged close in front.](figures/p10_4.png)

The forward and sideways positions, (c) and (d), are very demanding on the shoulders. Hold them for only a few breaths at first. Position (g), in the Sanchin guard, is particularly useful, because it lets you practise the guard while the grip and forearms work.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I keep dropping the jars."**

*The problem.* Your grip gives way after a few steps and the jars slip.

*Why it happens.* The jars are too heavy for your current grip, or the rim is too smooth or too wide.

*The solution.* Empty the jars and start again with less weight. Chalk or rosin on the fingertips helps with smooth rims. Train the grip separately with the finger exercises in Chapter 12. Always train with the jars over a mat or grass, in case you drop them, and never wear sandals.
:::

## Ishi-sashi: The Stone Padlock

The *ishi-sashi* is a stone or iron weight shaped like an old-fashioned padlock, with a short handle on top. It can be used for slow punches, blocks and swinging movements, and it trains the grip and wrist in a slightly different way from the chi-ishi, because the weight hangs close to the hand. Plate 10.7 shows its use.

![**Plate 10.7** Exercises with the ishi-sashi. (a) Chamber. (b) A slow punch. (c) Return. (d) A rising block. (e) A block to the side. (f) The same with the other hand. (g) Both held in the Sanchin guard. (h) Rest.](figures/p10_7.png)

Many students use a kettlebell today in place of the ishi-sashi, which works very well. Hold it by the handle with the weight hanging below the fist or resting against the back of the forearm, and perform slow, controlled blocking and punching movements. Avoid fast swinging until you are very experienced; momentum makes the load on the wrist and elbow much greater than the weight suggests.

## Kongoken: The Iron Ring

The *kongōken* is a large, heavy oval of iron, introduced into Goju-Ryu by Miyagi after he saw a similar tool used by wrestlers in Hawaii, according to the usual account. It is used for lifting, pressing and twisting, and it develops the whole upper body, as well as working with a partner in some schools. Plate 10.5 shows basic exercises.

![**Plate 10.5** Exercises with the kongōken in shiko dachi. (a) Gripping it low. (b) Lifting. (c) At chest height. (d) Overhead. (e) Lowered behind the head. (f) Pressed out. (g–h) Twisting to each side.](figures/p10_5.png)

Kongōken are heavy, typically ten kilograms or more, and awkward. If you do not have one, a sandbag or a loaded rucksack held in the arms can substitute for many of the exercises. The twisting movements, (g) and (h), are excellent for the trunk muscles that drive the hip rotation of Chapter 8, but they must be done slowly and with the stance and abdomen firm.

## Tan: The Stone Barbell

The *tan* is a simple barbell: a wooden or metal bar with a weight at each end. Traditionally the weights were stones; today they are often concrete or ordinary weight plates. Plate 10.6 shows typical exercises.

![**Plate 10.6** Exercises with the tan in shiko dachi. (a) Grip. (b) Rolling the bar in the hands. (c) A curl. (d) At the chest. (e) Pressing overhead. (f) Holding it out in front. (g) Twisting. (h) Lowering.](figures/p10_6.png)

The rolling exercise in frame (b), in which the bar is rotated back and forth in the hands, is a superb forearm exercise. The twist in frame (g), with the bar held across the shoulders or in front, strengthens the trunk for rotational power. The tan is also used across the shoulders for squats in shiko dachi, which build the legs for the root. If you already train with a modern barbell, it will serve perfectly well.

## Tetsu-geta: The Iron Sandals

*Tetsu-geta* are heavy iron sandals strapped to the feet. They load the hip flexors, thighs and core during leg raises and slow kicks, and develop the strength and control for low, powerful kicking. Plate 10.8 shows the basic exercises.

![**Plate 10.8** Exercises with tetsu-geta, shown on the right foot. (a) Stand. (b–c) Lift the knee slowly. (d) A slow front kick. (e) Re-chamber. (f) The leg raised to the side. (g) The leg raised behind. (h) Lower.](figures/p10_8.png)

Iron sandals put considerable strain on the knee and the lower back if used carelessly. Never kick fast with them; every movement is slow and controlled. Start with ankle weights of half a kilogram to a kilogram, which are a safe, modern substitute, and progress very gradually. If you feel any strain in the knee or the lower back, stop.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My lower back aches after leg work with weights."**

*The problem.* After tetsu-geta or ankle-weight exercises, the lower back aches.

*Why it happens.* As the leg lifts, the weight pulls on the hip flexor muscles, which attach to the lower spine. If the abdomen is not firm, the pelvis tips and the lower back arches under the load.

*The solution.* Lighten the weight. Before each lift, engage the deep core as in Chapter 3 and keep the tailbone drawn down. Hold a wall or chair for balance so that you can concentrate on the trunk. Reduce the height of the lift. Strengthen the trunk with the exercises in Chapter 12 before returning to heavier work.
:::

## A Sample Hojo Undo Session

Here is a session I give to intermediate students, to be done two or three times a week, after a warm-up and on days that are not heavy partner or conditioning days.

| Tool | Exercise | Sets × reps | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chi-ishi | Forward lowering | 2 × 8 each hand | Slow, four counts down and up |
| Chi-ishi | Wrist turns | 2 × 8 each way | Elbow still |
| Chi-ishi | Slow punch | 2 × 6 each hand | Sanchin breathing |
| Nigiri-game | Walk in Sanchin | 3 lengths | Posture unchanged |
| Nigiri-game | Held in guard | 3 × 5 breaths | Shoulders down |
| Tan or barbell | Wrist rolls | 2 × 20 seconds | Both directions |
| Tan or barbell | Shiko dachi squats | 2 × 8 | Knees over toes |
| Ankle weights | Slow front kicks | 2 × 6 each leg | Core firm |

The session takes about thirty-five minutes. Finish with five minutes of Tensho or quiet breathing to bring the body back to a calm state.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Too heavy, too soon | Tendon pain at elbow and wrist | Start light; choke up; progress slowly |
| Jerky movement | Joint strain; little strength gain | Four counts down, four counts up |
| Holding the breath | Blood pressure spikes; dizziness | Exhale on effort, every repetition |
| Losing the stance | No connection; back strain | Lighter load until stance holds |
| Training every day | Overuse injury | Two or three times a week; easy weeks |
| Neglecting the weaker hand | Imbalance | Always start with the weaker side |
| Poorly made tools | Head detaches; injury | Check before every session |

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Hojo undō builds strength specific to Goju-Ryu: grip, wrists, shoulders, legs and connection under load.
- Always warm up, start light, breathe, stay in your stance and check your equipment.
- The chi-ishi uses leverage; a light head becomes very heavy at the end of a long handle.
- Nigiri-game train the grip and the root together; posture must not change when carrying them.
- The ishi-sashi, kongōken, tan and tetsu-geta each have modern equivalents that work equally well.
- Progress slowly with regular lighter weeks.
- Stop at sharp pain; tendon problems take far longer to heal than muscles.
:::

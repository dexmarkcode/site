# Posture, Alignment and the Architecture of Stance

::: chapter-intro
A Goju-Ryu stance is a piece of engineering. It must carry your weight, resist a push from any direction, let you move without warning and send force from the floor to your hands. This chapter explains how a well-built posture works, sets out the main stances of the style with their measurements, and shows you how to find and correct the alignment faults that undermine everything else.
:::

A few years ago a builder joined my Thursday evening class. He was a big, capable man who had spent thirty years putting up houses, and in his first lesson he watched me adjust a student's Sanchin stance with growing impatience. Afterwards he came over and said, a little sheepishly, "You're doing what I do. You're checking the footings and the plumb before you look at anything else." He was quite right, and I have borrowed his language ever since.

Every structure that has to bear a load is built from the ground up. You set the footings, you make sure the walls are plumb, and only then do you put on the roof. If the footings are poor, nothing you do higher up will save the building. If a wall leans, the load runs down it at an angle, and sooner or later something cracks. The human body is not a house, but in a stance it is asked to do the same job: carry loads safely down to the ground. The principles of good building apply surprisingly well.

## The Body as a Load-Bearing Structure

When you stand still, three things keep you upright. Your bones carry compression, your ligaments and tendons hold the joints together, and your muscles make constant, tiny adjustments to stop you toppling. The more of the load your bones carry, the less your muscles have to do. This is why good posture feels effortless and poor posture feels tiring. A slumped person is not resting. Their back muscles are working hard to stop the trunk folding forward.

For a karateka, there is a second reason to care. When someone pushes or strikes you, the force must go somewhere. If your skeleton is stacked so that the force runs straight down through your joints to the floor, you can absorb it with very little effort. If a joint is out of line, the force bends that joint, and your muscles have to fight it. They usually lose.

I teach this with three simple ideas.

**Stacking.** Each segment of the body should sit directly on the one below it, so that compression passes through bone rather than bending a joint. Head over chest, chest over pelvis, pelvis over feet.

**Footings.** The feet are the foundations. Their placement decides how stable the whole structure can be, and in which directions.

**Bracing.** Where a joint must be held at an angle, as the knees are in every Goju-Ryu stance, the surrounding muscles must brace it. Bracing is the subject of Part Two. In this chapter we concentrate on getting the shape right so that bracing has the best possible chance.

## Finding the Plumb Line

Stand sideways to a mirror in a relaxed, natural stance with your feet a little apart. If a plumb line were hung beside you, where would it fall? In a well-aligned body seen from the side, a vertical line passes close to the ear, the point of the shoulder, the hip joint, just in front of the centre of the knee, and just in front of the ankle bone. Plate 2.1 shows this, along with four very common departures from it.

![**Plate 2.1** The plumb line. (a)–(d) A well-aligned natural stance seen from the side, front, side with the key joints connected, and back. (e)–(h) Four common postural faults, each shown against the plumb line: the forward head, the sway-back, the slump and the rigid "parade-ground" posture.](figures/p2_1.png)

Look closely at the faults, because you will meet every one of them in Sanchin.

**The forward head.** Many people who work at desks carry the head a few centimetres in front of the shoulders. A human head weighs roughly as much as a bowling ball, and every centimetre it drifts forward increases the strain on the neck and upper back. In a stance, the forward head also pulls the chest forward and tips the weight towards the toes.

**The sway-back.** The hips drift forward of the ankles while the upper back leans back to compensate. It looks relaxed, even lazy, and it places the load on the ligaments at the front of the hip and the joints of the lower back.

**The slump.** The upper back rounds, the chest sinks and the head falls forward. Breathing becomes shallow, because the ribs cannot expand properly. We shall see in Chapter 3 why this matters so much.

**The parade-ground posture.** This is the over-correction. The chest is thrust up, the shoulders pinned back and the chin lifted. It looks impressive, but it is rigid and brittle, and it locks the lower back into an exaggerated curve. I see it most often in students who have been told to "stand up straight" too many times.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "When I stand straight, I feel as if I'm leaning backwards."**

*The problem.* You line yourself up with the mirror and it feels wrong, as if you are about to fall over backwards.

*Why it happens.* Your sense of vertical has adjusted to your habitual posture. If you normally lean slightly forward, true vertical feels like a backward lean. This is completely normal.

*The solution.* Trust the mirror and the camera, not the feeling, for the first few weeks. Practise the wall test described below twice a day for a minute. Within a month, the new alignment will begin to feel like home.
:::

### The wall test

This is the simplest posture check I know, and you can do it anywhere. Plate 2.9 shows the sequence.

![**Plate 2.9** The wall test. (a) Stand with the heels about five centimetres from a wall. (b) Notice which parts of the body touch the wall: ideally the buttocks, the upper back and, lightly, the back of the head. (c) Soften the knees without losing the contacts. (d) Step away and hold the alignment.](figures/p2_9.png)

1. Stand with your back to a wall, heels about five centimetres from the skirting board and feet hip-width apart.
2. Let your buttocks and upper back rest against the wall. Now notice the back of your head. If it touches only when you lift your chin, your head habitually sits forward.
3. Slide a hand behind your lower back. There should be room for your flat hand, no more. If you could fit a fist, your lower back is arched; if there is no room at all, it is flattened.
4. Soften your knees by a few centimetres and keep the contacts.
5. Step forward away from the wall, keeping everything exactly as it is. This is roughly the alignment you are aiming for in every Goju-Ryu stance.

## The Feet: Your Footings

The foot is a remarkable structure, a flexible arch of twenty-six bones that can become a rigid lever at will. Three points carry most of your weight: the heel, the ball of the foot behind the big toe and the ball behind the little toe. Teachers often call this the "tripod" of the foot.

When the weight is spread evenly between the three points, the arch is supported and the foot grips the floor naturally. When the weight rolls inward (pronation), the arch collapses and the knee tends to fall in after it. When the weight rolls to the outside edge (supination), the foot becomes unstable and the ankle is vulnerable.

::: drill
**Drill: Finding the tripod**

1. Stand barefoot in a natural stance. Close your eyes.
2. Rock your weight slowly forward onto the balls of the feet and back onto the heels, five times, getting smaller each time until you settle in the middle.
3. Rock from side to side, from the big-toe edge to the little-toe edge, five times, again getting smaller.
4. Settle where you feel the heel and the two balls of the foot pressing evenly. Gently spread your toes and let them rest on the floor without gripping.
5. Notice how the arch lifts slightly by itself. Stay for ten slow breaths.
:::

## The Natural Stances

Goju-Ryu uses a small number of stances, and most of them are compact. Before we look at the fighting stances, let us look at the natural stances that begin and end the kata. Plate 2.2 shows them from the front and from a three-quarter angle, and Plate 2.3 shows all the main stances from above.

![**Plate 2.2** The natural stances, from the front (a–d) and three-quarter view (e–h): musubi dachi (heels together, toes apart), heiko dachi (feet parallel, hip-width), hachiji dachi (feet hip-width, toes turned out) and uchi-hachiji dachi (feet hip-width, toes turned slightly in).](figures/p2_2.png)

**Musubi dachi.** The heels touch and the toes open to roughly a forty-five-degree angle. It is the stance of attention and of the bow.

**Heiko dachi.** The feet are parallel and about hip-width apart, measured from the centres of the heels. It is the most neutral of stances and the one I use for most breathing exercises.

**Hachiji dachi.** Like heiko dachi, but with the toes turned out. It is the usual ready stance (*yōi*) before many kata.

**Uchi-hachiji dachi.** The toes turn slightly inward. It sits halfway between the natural stances and Sanchin dachi, and it is useful for teaching beginners the feeling of the knees drawing gently inward.

![**Plate 2.3** Footprints of the main Goju-Ryu stances seen from above. The dotted line marks the centre line; the arrow shows the facing direction. In (g) and (h), the grey foot carries most of the weight and the grey circle marks the ball of the foot in contact with the floor.](figures/p2_3.png)

## Measuring a Stance to Your Own Body

One of the commonest questions I am asked is "How wide should my stance be?" The honest answer is that it depends on your body. Fixed measurements in centimetres make little sense when one student is a metre and a half tall and the next is nearly two metres. Goju-Ryu teachers traditionally measure stances with the student's own body, and I recommend the same.

| Stance | Width (side to side) | Length (front to back) | Weight distribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heiko / hachiji dachi | Hip-width, heel centre to heel centre | Feet level | 50/50 |
| Sanchin dachi | Shoulder-width, outside of shoulders | Front heel level with back toes | 50/50 |
| Shiko dachi | About two shoulder-widths | Feet level | 50/50 |
| Zenkutsu dachi | Hip- to shoulder-width | About one and a half shoulder-widths | 60 front / 40 back |
| Neko ashi dachi | Narrow, about a fist apart | About one foot-length | 10 front / 90 back |
| Kosa dachi | Crossed, back knee tucked behind front | Short | 90 front / 10 back |

You will notice that the ratios matter more than the absolute numbers. Chapter 4 gives a precise, step-by-step method for measuring Sanchin dachi, because there the details really matter.

## Shiko Dachi: The Wide Root

Shiko dachi is the wide, low stance of Goju-Ryu, and it appears in many of the classical kata. It looks rather like the stance of a sumo wrestler, and it shares a purpose with that stance: it is immensely stable from side to side and gives a powerful base for grappling and for techniques that drive downward. Plate 2.4 shows how to enter it.

![**Plate 2.4** Shiko dachi. (a) Begin in a natural stance. (b) Step out to about two shoulder-widths with the toes turned out. (c) Sink straight down. (d) The finished stance, with root lines into the floor. (e) From the side the trunk is upright. (f) Three-quarter view. (g) The knees track over the toes. (h) A deeper version used for strength training.](figures/p2_4.png)

1. Begin in hachiji dachi with your hands in a guard.
2. Step out sideways so that your feet are roughly two shoulder-widths apart, toes turned out at about forty-five degrees.
3. Sink straight down, as if sitting onto a stool directly beneath you. Do not tip forward.
4. Press your knees outward so that each one points in the same direction as its foot. Look down: you should see the knee directly above the second and third toes.
5. Keep the trunk upright, the tailbone pointing down and the weight evenly between both feet.

The depth of the stance varies. In kata, the thighs are usually somewhere between horizontal and a little above it. For strength training, you may go deeper, as in frame (h), but only once you can hold the shape with the knees tracking correctly.

### Why the knees must track

The knee is a hinge. It is designed to bend and straighten, with only a little rotation. When you sink into shiko dachi with the knees falling inward, the knee is asked to bend and twist at the same time, and the ligaments on its inner side take a load they were not built for. Over months and years, this is a common cause of knee pain in karateka. Plate 2.7 shows correct and incorrect knee tracking in four different stances.

![**Plate 2.7** Knee tracking. In each pair, the left drawing is correct and the right one shows the fault. (a–b) Shiko dachi with knees over toes, and with knees collapsing inward. (c–d) Sanchin dachi with knees drawn gently toward the centre over the feet, and with knees pushed outward. (e–f) Zenkutsu dachi, front knee over the foot and collapsing in. (g–h) A training squat, correct and with the knees falling in (valgus).](figures/p2_7.png)

Notice frame (c). In Sanchin dachi the knees are drawn *inward*, which seems to contradict everything I have just said. It does not, because in Sanchin the feet are also turned inward. The knee still points in the same direction as its foot. Chapter 4 explains this in detail.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My knees ache after shiko dachi."**

*The problem.* After holding shiko dachi or doing kata with deep stances, you feel an ache on the inner side of one or both knees.

*Why it happens.* Most often the knees are collapsing inward because the hip muscles that rotate the thigh outward are weak or because the feet are turned out further than the hips can comfortably allow.

*The solution.* Bring the feet in a little and reduce the toe-out angle until the knee can comfortably sit over the toes. Raise the stance. Strengthen the hips with the side-lying leg raises and clamshells in Chapter 12. Build depth slowly, over weeks. If the pain is sharp, persists for more than a few days or comes with swelling, see a physiotherapist.
:::

## Zenkutsu Dachi: The Forward Stance

Zenkutsu dachi is a long, forward-weighted stance used for driving attacks and for receiving force from the front. In Goju-Ryu it is generally a little shorter and higher than the long zenkutsu of some other styles, and the back foot is turned out slightly. Plate 2.5 shows it.

![**Plate 2.5** Zenkutsu dachi. (a–c) Stepping forward from heiko dachi into the stance. (d) The back leg is firm and nearly straight, forming a strong brace from the floor to the hip. (e) Front view. (f) View from behind. (g) Fault: leaning the trunk forward. (h) Fault: stance too short, so the front knee rises and the brace is lost.](figures/p2_5.png)

1. From heiko dachi, step forward with the left foot about one and a half shoulder-widths, keeping the feet roughly hip-width apart from side to side.
2. Bend the front knee until it is above the front foot. From the side, the shin is close to vertical.
3. Keep the back leg firm and almost straight, with the back heel on the floor and the back foot turned out slightly.
4. Keep the trunk upright, with the hips square or slightly angled depending on the technique.
5. Weight is roughly sixty per cent on the front foot and forty on the back.

The back leg is the secret of a strong zenkutsu dachi. It acts like a prop leaning against a wall, running in a straight line from the floor to the hip. When someone pushes you from the front, the force should travel straight down that line into the back foot. If you bend the back knee too much, the prop collapses.

## Neko Ashi Dachi: The Cat Stance

Neko ashi dachi, the "cat foot" stance, carries nearly all the weight on the back leg, with the front foot resting lightly on its ball. It is a stance for receiving and evading, for kicking with the front leg and for the circular hand techniques of kata such as Gekisai Dai Ni and Kururunfa. Plate 2.6 shows it.

![**Plate 2.6** Neko ashi dachi. (a) Natural stance. (b) Draw the weight back onto the rear leg. (c) Slide the front foot back until it rests on its ball. (d) The finished stance. (e) Front view. (f) Three-quarter view. (g) Roughly nine-tenths of the weight sits over the back foot. (h) Fault: the front foot is flat and heavy, so it cannot kick or move quickly.](figures/p2_6.png)

The key test of a good neko ashi dachi is simple. You should be able to lift the front foot from the floor at any moment without shifting your weight. If you cannot, you have too much weight on it.

The back leg works hard in this stance, with the knee bent well over the toes. Beginners often let the back knee collapse inward, which is uncomfortable and weak. Turn the back foot out slightly and press the back knee outward, in line with that foot.

## Moving Between Stances

A stance is not a place you go to and stop. In kata and in fighting, you flow between stances, and the transitions are where most of the problems appear. A stance that is perfect when you are standing still often falls apart in the moment you move into it.

The principle is this: **move from the centre, and keep the head at a constant height wherever the kata allows.** When you turn from shiko dachi into zenkutsu dachi, as shown in Plate 2.10, the hips lead, the feet pivot to allow the hips to turn, and the head stays level. If your head bobs up and down, you are standing up and sitting down again, which wastes time and telegraphs your movement.

![**Plate 2.10** Turning from shiko dachi into zenkutsu dachi. (a) Shiko dachi. (b) The weight begins to shift toward the side you will face. (c) The rear foot pivots. (d) The hips turn. (e) The front knee bends over the foot. (f) Zenkutsu dachi. (g–h) The two stances from the side.](figures/p2_10.png)

::: drill
**Drill: The glass of water**

Imagine that you are carrying a glass of water filled to the brim on the top of your head. Move slowly between heiko dachi, shiko dachi, zenkutsu dachi and neko ashi dachi in any order, keeping the imaginary glass level and not spilling a drop. Then try it with a real paper cup and a little water, outdoors. Your legs will burn, which is exactly the point.
:::

## Warming Up the Frame

Goju-Ryu classes traditionally begin with *junbi undō*, a set of preparatory exercises developed by Miyagi. Different schools have different versions, but they share a pattern: they work from the head to the feet, they warm each joint in turn, and they include stretching, strengthening and breathing. Plate 2.8 shows twelve exercises drawn from the common core.

![**Plate 2.8** Twelve preparatory exercises in the spirit of junbi undō. (a) Neck turns. (b) Neck bows. (c) Arm swings. (d) Opening the arms. (e) Trunk twists. (f) Forward fold. (g) Squat. (h) Knee circles. (i) Leg swings. (j) Hip opener in shiko dachi. (k) Lunge stretch. (l) Ankle rolls.](figures/p2_8.png)

Work through them slowly, about eight to ten repetitions each, breathing steadily. Do not force any stretch. The aim at the start of a session is to warm and mobilise, not to gain flexibility; deep stretching belongs at the end of a session when the muscles are already warm.

There is one exercise I would ask you to treat with care. The neck bows in frame (b) should be slow and gentle, and you should never roll the head round in a full circle with force. The neck is not built for it.

## Alignment Under Load

Everything so far has been about standing still. The real test of alignment is whether it survives pressure. Here are three simple tests you can do with a training partner. They introduce ideas that Part Two develops fully.

**The push test.** Stand in heiko dachi. Your partner places a flat hand on your sternum and pushes slowly and steadily backward, building over three seconds. A well-aligned stance passes the push down into the heels. A poorly aligned one buckles at the hips or the knees, or you take a step.

**The shoulder press.** Your partner stands behind you and presses straight down on your shoulders with both hands. If your stance is stacked, you will feel the pressure travel down into your feet, and you can hold it comfortably. If your head is forward or your lower back is arched, you will feel the strain in the neck or the back.

**The side nudge.** Your partner nudges you sideways at the hip, gently. Notice whether you wobble from the ankle, the knee or the hip. Each tells you something different about where your structure is weak.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I pass the tests when I'm concentrating but fail when I'm moving."**

*The problem.* Standing still and thinking about it, your posture holds. As soon as you move, or as soon as your mind is on something else, it collapses.

*Why it happens.* You are holding the posture with conscious effort rather than habit. The conscious mind can only attend to a few things at once.

*The solution.* Repetition, in many short bursts. Check your posture whenever you stand at a bus stop, wait for the kettle or queue in a shop. Use the glass of water drill daily. Ask a partner to test you at unexpected moments during kata practice, not only when you are ready.
:::

## A Troubleshooting Guide to Stances

| Symptom | Stance | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ache inside knee | Shiko, zenkutsu | Knee collapsing inward | Reduce toe-out; press knee over toes |
| Ache at front of knee | Any deep stance | Knee travelling far beyond toes, weight forward | Sit back; keep shin nearer vertical |
| Lower back ache | Shiko, neko ashi | Pelvis tipped forward, back arched | Drop the tailbone; lengthen the lower back |
| Wobble from front to back | Sanchin, shiko | Stance too short or too narrow | Measure to the body (Chapter 4) |
| Cannot lift front foot | Neko ashi | Too much weight forward | Sit back over the rear foot |
| Head bobs on transitions | All | Standing up between stances | Glass of water drill |
| Calves cramp | Neko ashi, Sanchin | Weight on the toes; gripping | Find the tripod; relax the toes |
| Feet turn as you step | Zenkutsu | Hips leading too early | Plant the foot, then turn the hips |

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- A stance is a load-bearing structure. Stack the segments, set the footings, then brace.
- From the side, the plumb line passes near the ear, shoulder, hip, front of the knee and front of the ankle.
- The common posture faults are the forward head, sway-back, slump and rigid parade-ground posture.
- The foot works best with weight spread over its tripod: heel, big-toe ball and little-toe ball.
- Measure stances against your own body, not in fixed centimetres.
- In every stance, the knee points in the same direction as its foot.
- Move from the centre and keep the head level through transitions.
- Test alignment under load, not only standing still.
:::

# Sanchin Dachi and the Crescent Step

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin begins with the feet. Before the tension, the breath or the testing, there is a small, peculiar stance that looks awkward to newcomers and turns out to be one of the most carefully designed structures in karate. This chapter builds Sanchin dachi from the ground up, measures it to your body, teaches the crescent step and the turn, and gives you a systematic way of finding and fixing the faults that creep into both.
:::

When I first stood in Sanchin dachi, I hated it. My feet felt pigeon-toed and silly, my knees ached within a minute, and when my teacher pushed lightly on my chest I stumbled backward as if I had been standing on ice. I remember thinking that a perfectly ordinary natural stance would have been far more stable. Then she took my shoulders, moved my back foot about four centimetres, turned my front foot a few degrees, pressed gently on my knees and said, "Now." She pushed again, harder. Nothing happened. I was rooted to the floor, and the push seemed to vanish into it.

That moment taught me something that this whole chapter is built around. Sanchin dachi only works when it is exactly right for your body. A few centimetres or degrees out, and it is weaker than a natural stance. Exactly right, and it is remarkably strong. That is why we spend so long on it.

## Why Sanchin Dachi Looks the Way It Does

The word *sanchin* is usually translated as "three battles" or "three conflicts". The three are often said to be the mind, the body and the breath, which must be brought into agreement. Some teachers point to the three points of the stance (two feet and the centre), others to three levels of the body. You will hear several explanations, and they need not compete. What matters for us is the job the stance does.

Sanchin dachi is designed to be stable in every horizontal direction at once while keeping the body compact and ready to move. Most stances are strong in one or two directions. Shiko dachi resists side-to-side pushes superbly but can be toppled forward or back. A long zenkutsu dachi resists pushes from the front but is weak to the side. Sanchin dachi gives up some of that specialised strength in exchange for all-round stability. It does this through four features.

**A short front-to-back offset.** One foot is a little in front of the other, so the base has depth as well as width.

**Shoulder-width spacing.** The feet are as wide as the outside of the shoulders, giving side-to-side stability without making the stance wide and slow.

**Toes turned inward.** Both feet turn in, the front foot more than the back. This brings the knees naturally toward the centre line.

**Knees drawn inward and the thighs braced.** The inward pressure of the knees closes the stance like a pair of brackets, and links the two legs into a single, connected base.

## Measuring Sanchin Dachi to Your Body

There is no single correct size for Sanchin dachi. There is a correct size for *you*, and it is set by your own body. Schools differ slightly on the details, but the method in Plate 4.1 is widely taught and works for almost everyone.

![**Plate 4.1** Measuring right-foot-forward Sanchin dachi, seen from above. (a) Begin in heiko dachi. (b) Set the feet at the width of the outside of your shoulders. (c) Slide the right foot forward in a small inward curve. (d) The heel of the front foot is level with the toes of the back foot. (e) Turn the back toes slightly inward. (f) Check the width against the shoulders. (g) Weight is shared equally. (h) The mirror image, left foot forward.](figures/p4_1.png)

1. **Set the width.** Stand in heiko dachi and widen the feet until the outside edges of the feet are roughly below the outside edges of the shoulders. Some teachers measure from the centre of each heel; the difference is small. If you have broad shoulders and narrow hips, err on the narrower side.
2. **Set the length.** Slide the right foot forward until its heel is level with the toes of the left foot. You can check this by drawing an imaginary line across the floor from the tips of the left toes: the right heel should sit on it.
3. **Turn the front foot in.** Pivoting on the heel, turn the front toes inward until the foot points about twenty to thirty degrees toward the centre line.
4. **Turn the back foot in.** Turn the back foot in a little, about ten to fifteen degrees. In some schools the back foot stays straight; follow your teacher.
5. **Bend the knees.** Soften the knees until you can no longer see your toes past them when you look down, and draw the knees gently toward each other so that each points over its own foot.
6. **Check the weight.** It is shared equally between the feet, and spread over the whole of each foot.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

The measurement "front heel level with back toes" gives a length of roughly one foot-length. In some lineages the stance is slightly longer, with the front heel a fist ahead of the back toes. If your teacher uses the longer version, use it. What matters is that you measure it the same way every time, so that you can compare one session with the next.
:::

### The feeling of the stance

When the measurements are right, the stance produces some distinctive sensations. Learn to recognise them, because they are your best guide when no teacher is watching.

- The inner thighs feel engaged, as if you were holding a large, soft ball between your knees.
- The weight feels evenly spread under the whole of both feet; neither the toes nor the heels are doing more than their share.
- The buttocks feel slightly drawn under and the lower back feels long rather than arched.
- The knees feel springy, not locked and not straining.
- When someone pushes you gently from any direction, you feel the push travel down into the floor rather than rocking you.

If any of these is missing, go back to the measurements before you start looking elsewhere. Nine times out of ten, the fault is in the feet.

## Entering Sanchin Dachi from Yoi

In the kata, you do not simply arrive in Sanchin dachi. You step into it from the ready position, and how you do so sets the tone for the whole form. Plate 4.2 shows one common version.

![**Plate 4.2** Entering Sanchin dachi from the ready position, seen from the front. (a) Yoi in hachiji dachi. (b) The arms cross in front of the body. (c) The right foot moves. (d) It travels in a small inward crescent. (e) The stance settles. (f) The arms rise. (g) The Sanchin guard. (h) Holding, with breath and root established.](figures/p4_2.png)

The details vary considerably between schools: some move the right foot first, some the left; some open the arms before the step, some after. Follow your own teacher. The principles that matter are these. The step is made with the knees already bent, so that the head does not rise and fall. The foot moves in a small curve, which we shall come to in a moment. The stance settles before the arms move, so that the arms have something solid to connect to. And the breath is natural and calm throughout; the first ibuki comes with the first technique, not before.

## The Crescent Step

Sanchin moves forward with a distinctive step in which the moving foot travels in a curve, first inward toward the standing foot and then outward to its new position. This is called the crescent step, because the path of the foot traces a crescent moon on the floor. Plate 4.4 shows it from above.

![**Plate 4.4** The crescent step seen from above, from right-foot-forward to left-foot-forward Sanchin dachi. (a) Starting stance. (b) The weight settles over the front foot without leaning. (c) The rear foot draws in toward the front foot. (d) It passes close to the standing foot. (e) It arcs outward and forward. (f) It lands with the toes turned in. (g) The new rear foot adjusts its angle. (h) The completed step, with the whole path of the moving foot shown.](figures/p4_4.png)

### Why step in a crescent?

Students often ask why we do not simply step straight forward. There are several good reasons, and they are worth understanding, because each one points to something you should feel as you step.

**It protects the groin.** As the moving foot passes close to the standing leg, the thighs close and the groin is covered at the moment when the body is least stable.

**It keeps the knees together.** The inward path keeps the inner thigh muscles engaged throughout the step, so the connection between the legs is never lost.

**It keeps the centre stable.** Because the foot passes under the body rather than swinging out to the side, the centre of mass does not have to shift sideways. A straight step from a shoulder-width stance makes the body sway from side to side like a walking duck.

**It hides intent.** The curved path is short and close, and it gives an opponent very little warning that you are moving.

**It trains the feet.** The sliding, curving movement teaches the feet to stay in light contact with the floor and to feel its surface, a skill we shall come back to in Chapter 7.

### The step, frame by frame

Plate 4.5 shows the crescent step from the side, and Plate 4.6 shows it from the front. Study both, because each shows something the other hides.

![**Plate 4.5** The crescent step from the side. The head stays at the same height throughout; the arms and trunk do not change shape; the moving foot glides close to the floor.](figures/p4_5.png)

![**Plate 4.6** The crescent step from the front. The moving foot draws in toward the centre line, passes the standing foot and arcs out again to shoulder width. The knees stay drawn in and the hips stay level.](figures/p4_6.png)

1. **Prepare.** In right-foot-forward Sanchin dachi, keep the weight centred. Do not shift your weight onto the front foot before you move; the step is made from a stable base.
2. **Draw in.** Allow the left foot to become light, then slide it forward and inward, close to the floor, toward the inside of the right foot. The left knee stays bent and drawn in.
3. **Pass close.** The left foot passes just beside the right ankle, almost brushing it. The thighs are close together.
4. **Arc out.** Continue the curve forward and outward until the left foot reaches its new position, shoulder-width from the centre line, with its heel level with the toes of the right foot.
5. **Land and turn in.** Place the foot with the toes already turned in. Do not land straight and twist; land in the final position.
6. **Settle.** Adjust the new rear foot if necessary, re-establish the even weight, and draw the knees gently inward. The step is complete only when the stance is completely settled.

::: drill
**Drill: Stepping on paper**

Place two sheets of A4 paper on the floor, one under each foot, in right-foot-forward Sanchin dachi. Make a crescent step, sliding the left foot on its sheet of paper. If the paper crumples, you are pressing down too hard or lifting and stamping. If it slides smoothly, your foot is skimming the floor correctly. Practise twenty steps forward and back. The paper will tell you more about your footwork than any mirror.
:::

### Keeping the head level

The head should not rise as you step. Students find this hard, because the natural tendency is to straighten the standing leg slightly as the moving foot passes. The cure is to keep the standing knee bent and pressed slightly forward over its foot, and to imagine that you are moving under a low ceiling that brushes the top of your head. If you film yourself from the side, put a strip of tape on the mirror or the wall at the height of your head and watch whether you rise above it.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I wobble when the back foot leaves the floor."**

*The problem.* As soon as your rear foot becomes light to step, you lose balance, usually toward the side of the moving foot.

*Why it happens.* Most often your weight is not truly central over the front foot's base before you move, so when the rear foot lifts, the body tips. Sometimes the standing knee is collapsing inward or outward, so it cannot support the body alone.

*The solution.* Practise the "half step": draw the rear foot in to touch the inside of the front ankle, pause for three slow breaths, then return it to its starting position. Repeat ten times on each side. When you can pause with complete stability, add the second half of the step. Strengthen the standing leg with the single-leg exercises in Chapter 12.
:::

## Turning in Sanchin

Sanchin includes a turn of half a circle, so that you face the direction from which you came. Schools differ in how they do this, but the version shown in Plate 4.7 and Plate 4.8 is common and illustrates the principles well.

![**Plate 4.7** The Sanchin turn seen from above. (a) Right-foot-forward stance. (b) The left foot crosses behind the right and is placed with the toes pointing back. (c) The toes of the left foot now point to the rear. (d) Pivoting on both feet, the body turns. (e) The body now faces the rear. (f) The width is adjusted. (g) The weight settles evenly. (h) The completed turn.](figures/p4_7.png)

![**Plate 4.8** The Sanchin turn with the figure, seen from the original front. The foot moves first, then the hips turn, and the arms and upper body follow the hips. The guard keeps its shape throughout, and the head stays at the same height.](figures/p4_8.png)

1. **Move the foot.** From right-foot-forward Sanchin dachi, move the left foot in an arc across and behind the right, placing it so that its toes point toward the rear and the feet are about shoulder-width apart.
2. **Turn the hips.** Pivoting on the balls of both feet, turn the hips through half a circle to face the rear. The hips lead; the shoulders and arms follow a fraction later.
3. **Arrive in the stance.** You are now in Sanchin dachi facing the opposite direction. Adjust the feet to the correct width, length and angle.
4. **Settle and breathe.** Re-establish the even weight and the knee pressure before the next technique.

The most important principle is that the guard keeps its shape. The arms do not swing round the body like a door. The whole body turns as a unit around a vertical axis through the centre, with the hips initiating. If your arms flop or your guard opens during the turn, slow the turn down until you can keep it closed.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "After turning, my stance is always too long or too short."**

*The problem.* You complete the turn and find that your feet are badly placed, so that you have to shuffle to correct the stance.

*Why it happens.* The moving foot is being placed by guesswork rather than by a consistent reference.

*The solution.* Use the standing foot as a reference. Decide exactly where the moving foot goes in relation to it: for example, "the left big toe just behind the right heel, a shoulder-width to the side." Practise placing the foot to that reference twenty times slowly, without turning, before adding the pivot. Film the turn from above if you can, with the phone on a high shelf or a stepladder.
:::

## Faults in Sanchin Dachi

Plate 4.9 shows eight faults with the figure, and Plate 4.10 shows eight faults in the footprints. Between them they cover nearly every problem I see in students' Sanchin stances.

![**Plate 4.9** Eight common faults in Sanchin dachi. (a) Stance too long. (b) Too wide. (c) Too narrow. (d) Back foot turned out. (e) Weight back on the rear foot. (f) Weight forward. (g) Knees pushed outward. (h) Standing too tall with the knees nearly straight.](figures/p4_9.png)

![**Plate 4.10** Faults in Sanchin dachi seen from above. (a) Too long. (b) Too wide. (c) Feet on one line, like a tightrope walker. (d) Back toes turned out. (e) Front toes pointing straight ahead. (f) Toes turned in too far. (g) No front-to-back offset. (h) Heels lifted, weight on the balls of the feet.](figures/p4_10.png)

The troubleshooting table below groups these faults by what you feel, which is usually the first clue you get.

| What you feel | Likely fault | Test | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easily pushed backward | Stance too short, or weight back | Partner push to the chest | Lengthen to "heel to toes"; centre weight |
| Easily pushed sideways | Feet on one line, or too narrow | Partner push to the shoulder | Widen to shoulder width |
| Ache on outer knee | Toes turned in too far | Look down: knee inside the foot | Reduce toe-in until knee is over the foot |
| Ache on inner knee | Knees pushed outward, toes in | Knee outside the foot | Draw knees in over the feet |
| Calves cramping | Heels lifted, weight on toes | Slide paper under the heel | Settle onto the whole foot |
| Lower back aches | Pelvis tipped forward | Wall test (Chapter 2) | Draw tailbone down gently |
| Thighs burn quickly | Standing too low | Compare with Plate 4.3 | Rise a little; build depth slowly |
| No feeling in inner thighs | Feet too wide, knees out | Ball-between-knees check | Narrow stance; press knees in |

### The weight under the foot

Many of these faults show up first as a change in how the weight sits under the foot. Plate 4.11 compares four patterns.

![**Plate 4.11** How the weight sits under the foot. (a) Spread over the whole foot, as it should be in Sanchin dachi. (b) On the balls of the feet only. (c) On the heels only. (d) The tripod of heel, big-toe ball and little-toe ball, the reference for even weight.](figures/p4_11.png)

::: drill
**Drill: The coin test**

Stand in Sanchin dachi barefoot and ask a partner to try to slide a coin under the edges of your feet: under the heel, the outer edge, the inner arch, the balls and the toes. In a well-settled stance the coin will pass only under the arch. If it slides under your heel, your weight is too far forward. If it slides under the toes, your toes are lifting or clawing. If it slides under the outer edge, your weight has rolled inward.
:::

## Practising the Stance on Its Own

Sanchin dachi is worth practising without the arms or the breath at first, for short periods, several times a day. Here is a simple progression that I give to new students.

**Week one.** Measure the stance carefully on each side. Hold it for thirty seconds, relax, and repeat five times on each side. Check it in the mirror from the front and from the side.

**Week two.** Hold for one minute at a time, and add the crescent step: ten steps forward, turn, and ten steps back, slowly.

**Week three.** Add the arms in the Sanchin guard (Chapter 5 explains them fully). Keep the breath natural. Hold each stance for three slow breaths before stepping.

**Week four.** Ask a partner to push you gently from the front, back and sides, in each stance. Correct whatever the pushes reveal.

Only when the stance passes these gentle pushes reliably are you ready to add the tension and breathing of the full kata. Many students resist this; they want to get on with "proper" Sanchin. I understand the impatience. But a strong tension built on a poor stance only locks the fault in place. A good stance with modest tension is far better than a poor stance with great tension.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sanchin dachi trades specialised strength for all-round stability.
- Measure it to your body: outside-of-shoulder width, front heel level with back toes, toes turned in, knees drawn in over the feet, weight shared equally.
- Learn the feelings of the correct stance: engaged inner thighs, even weight, long lower back, springy knees.
- The crescent step protects the groin, keeps the knees connected, keeps the centre stable and hides intent.
- Keep the head at a constant height when stepping and turning.
- In the turn, the hips lead and the guard keeps its shape.
- Most Sanchin faults begin in the feet. Check the measurements first.
:::

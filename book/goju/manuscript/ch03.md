# Breath as Technique: Ibuki, Nogare and the Diaphragm

::: chapter-intro
Goju-Ryu is named after a line about breathing, and the breath is its most distinctive feature. This chapter explains how breathing actually works, why the hard, audible breath of Sanchin and the quiet breath of Tensho each do their particular job, and how to train both safely. It ends with the problems that stop most students breathing well, and the cures for each.
:::

The first thing visitors notice about a Goju-Ryu class is the noise. In Sanchin, twenty people breathing out together through tightened throats sounds rather like the sea dragging back over shingle. It is unmistakable, and it puzzles people. I have been asked whether we are trying to frighten an opponent, whether it is a kind of meditation or whether it is just for show.

It is none of those things, though it has something in common with all of them. The breath in Goju-Ryu is a technique in its own right, exactly like a punch or a block. It has a correct form, it can be done well or badly, it can be tested and it can be trained. It also has effects on the body that reach well beyond the lungs: on posture, on stability, on the ability to take a blow and on the state of the mind.

Breathing is also the place where I see the most harm done by poor teaching. Students who hold their breath while straining, who force air into the chest or who blow out so hard that they become dizzy are not training breath; they are training a bad habit, and some of them are putting their health at risk. So we shall go slowly, start with the mechanics and build up.

## How Breathing Works

You breathe with a sheet of muscle called the diaphragm. It sits beneath the lungs and above the stomach and liver like a dome, or an upturned bowl, attached all round the bottom edge of the rib cage and to the front of the spine. When the diaphragm contracts, the dome flattens and moves downward, the space in the chest enlarges, and air is drawn into the lungs. When it relaxes, the dome rises, and the air flows out.

As the diaphragm presses down, it pushes on the organs of the abdomen. They have nowhere to go except outward and downward, so the belly and the lower back expand slightly and the floor of the pelvis is pressed gently down. This is why the belly moves when you breathe well. It is not the belly that breathes; it is the diaphragm, and the belly is simply making room.

Plate 3.1 shows this in schematic form. The diaphragm has partners. The muscles of the abdominal wall wrap round the trunk like a corset; the muscles of the pelvic floor form a sling across the bottom of the pelvis; and the deep muscles of the spine run up the back. Together with the diaphragm, they form a sealed container. When all of them work together, they raise the pressure inside the abdomen. That pressure is the single most important thing to understand about Goju-Ryu breathing.

### Intra-abdominal pressure

Imagine an empty drinks can. You can crush it easily by standing on it. Now imagine the same can, sealed and full of fizzy drink. It will hold your full weight. The thin walls are the same; what has changed is the pressure inside.

Your trunk behaves in a similar way. When the diaphragm, the abdominal wall and the pelvic floor all contract together, the abdomen becomes a pressurised cylinder that stiffens the spine from within and makes the whole trunk much harder to bend or compress. Weightlifters use this every time they lift something heavy. So do Goju-Ryu karateka in Sanchin. It is the main reason a well-trained student can take a hard strike to the abdomen without harm, and the main reason a correctly held Sanchin posture feels so solid.

There is a catch. If you raise that pressure by closing your throat and holding your breath, you also raise the pressure in your chest, which squeezes the great veins returning blood to the heart and causes a sharp rise and then fall in blood pressure. This is called the Valsalva manoeuvre. A brief, moderate version is normal in heavy lifting, but repeated, prolonged, forceful breath-holding is exactly what we must avoid. People with high blood pressure, heart problems, glaucoma or a history of stroke are particularly at risk.

The Goju-Ryu solution is elegant. In Sanchin breathing, you never hold your breath. You breathe *out* slowly, against a partly narrowed throat, while the abdominal muscles hold the pressure. Air is always moving. The pressure in the abdomen stays high, but the pressure in the chest never builds to dangerous levels. This is why the correct Sanchin breath is audible: the sound is the air escaping, and the sound tells you and your teacher that you are not holding your breath.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

I tell every new student the same thing: *if I cannot hear you, I assume you are holding your breath.* A silent Sanchin is not a refined Sanchin. Until you have trained for many years and your teacher tells you otherwise, keep the breath audible.
:::

## The Four Breaths of Goju-Ryu

Different schools use different words, but most Goju-Ryu teachers recognise four distinct ways of breathing. Plate 3.2 shows their rhythms as simple wave patterns, with inhalation as a rising line and exhalation as a falling one.

![**Plate 3.2** The rhythms of four kinds of breathing. Natural breathing is a steady, even wave. The Sanchin breath (ibuki) has a short inhalation and a long, controlled exhalation under tension, shaded grey. The Tensho breath is slow and unbroken. Nogare is quiet and controlled, often with a brief pause before the release.](figures/p3_2.png)

**Natural breathing.** Quiet, relaxed breathing through the nose, low in the body, without effort. This is the foundation of everything else and the breath you should return to whenever you are not deliberately doing something different.

**Ibuki** (the "hard" or "dynamic" breath). Used in Sanchin. A short, full inhalation through the nose, followed by a long, forceful, audible exhalation through the mouth against a narrowed throat, while the abdomen is held firm. This is *go*, the hard breath.

**The Tensho breath.** Slower, smoother and less forceful than ibuki, it flows continuously with the movement of the hands. Tension rises and falls with the breath like a wave rather than being held at a constant level.

**Nogare** (the "soft" or "escaping" breath). A quiet, controlled breath, often used to recover between techniques or as a gentle form of Sanchin breathing for students who cannot safely use ibuki. This is *ju*, the soft breath.

To these we can add the **kiai**, the short, sharp exhalation that accompanies a decisive technique, which is really a compressed form of ibuki lasting only a fraction of a second.

## Step One: Learning to Breathe Low

Before any of the specialised breaths, you need to be able to breathe naturally with the diaphragm. Many adults have lost this. Years of sitting, stress and tight waistbands teach people to breathe high in the chest, lifting the shoulders and ribs with every breath and barely moving the belly at all. High chest breathing is inefficient, it tenses the neck and shoulders, and it is completely incompatible with Sanchin.

Plate 3.3 and Plate 3.7 show how to check where you breathe.

![**Plate 3.3** Abdominal breathing with the hands on the belly, standing (a–d) and in seiza (e–h). On inhalation the belly fills gently forward and to the sides under the hands; on exhalation it draws softly back. The chest stays almost still.](figures/p3_3.png)

::: drill
**Drill: Hands on the belly**

1. Stand in heiko dachi, or sit in seiza or on a firm chair. Place your open hands on your abdomen, just below the navel, fingertips almost touching.
2. Breathe in slowly through your nose. Let the belly fill out under your hands, as if a balloon were inflating gently inside it.
3. Breathe out slowly through your nose. Let the belly draw softly back.
4. Keep your shoulders and chest still. If it helps, imagine that the breath begins in your hands.
5. Continue for two minutes, then rest. Repeat twice a day for a week before you move on.
:::

![**Plate 3.7** Three hand positions for checking the breath. (a–b) Hands on the lower ribs: the ribs should widen to the sides on inhalation. (c–d) Hands on the belly: the belly should fill forward. (e–f) Hands on the lower back: the back should widen slightly against the hands.](figures/p3_7.png)

When you can breathe into the belly easily, move your hands to the lower ribs and then to the lower back. A complete, healthy breath expands the trunk in all directions: forward at the belly, sideways at the lower ribs and slightly backward at the waist. Many students breathe only into the front of the belly. Learning to widen the ribs and the back as well gives a more even, stable pressure all round the spine, which is exactly what Sanchin needs.

### Why the nose?

In almost all Goju-Ryu breathing, you breathe in through the nose. The nose warms, filters and moistens the air. Nasal breathing also tends to be slower and deeper, and it encourages the diaphragm to work. Breathing in through the mouth, by contrast, tends to be quick and high in the chest. Some schools breathe out through the nose in Tensho and nogare; almost all breathe out through the mouth in Sanchin ibuki.

## Step Two: Coordinating Breath and Movement

Once you can breathe low, the next step is to link the breath to movement. Plate 3.6 shows a simple exercise that I use with every beginner.

![**Plate 3.6** Breathing with the arms. Inhale slowly as the open hands rise out to the sides and overhead (a–e); exhale slowly as they float down again (f–h). The breath and the arms should begin and finish together.](figures/p3_6.png)

The rule is simple: the breath and the movement must begin together and end together. If the arms arrive at the top and you are still breathing in, slow the arms down. If you run out of breath halfway up, slow the breath down. This sounds trivial, and it is not. Most students find that their breath and their movement are slightly out of step, and the effort of bringing them into line is itself excellent training for the attention.

## Step Three: Ibuki, the Sanchin Breath

Now we come to the breath that gives Goju-Ryu its sound. Plate 3.4 shows one cycle of ibuki as it is used in the Sanchin punch.

![**Plate 3.4** One cycle of the Sanchin breath (ibuki), seen from the side. (a) Chamber. (b) A short, full inhalation through the nose. (c–d) The exhalation begins, audible and slow, as the fist moves out. (e) Full extension with the abdomen held firm. (f) The breath finishes, not completely emptied. (g) A fresh inhalation as the fist returns. (h) Guard.](figures/p3_4.png)

### The inhalation

Breathe in through the nose, quickly but not violently, taking about one to two seconds. The breath goes low: you should feel the belly, the lower ribs and the lower back all widening. Do not lift the shoulders or the chest. Toward the end of the inhalation, let the pelvic floor and the lower abdomen firm gently, so that the air feels as though it settles into the lower belly. Many teachers describe this as "sending the breath to the tanden", the point a few finger-widths below the navel.

### The exhalation

Now breathe out through the mouth. The throat is narrowed, rather as if you were whispering the sound "haaa" with a slight grip in the throat. The lips are slightly parted. At the same time the abdominal wall stays firm and slightly expanded; it does not collapse inward. The result is a long, steady, audible stream of air, lasting four to six seconds at first and much longer with training.

The exhalation is not a push. Students often think they must force the air out as hard as they can, and they go red in the face, the veins stand out on their necks and they finish dizzy. That is wrong and potentially harmful. The air flows out because the diaphragm gradually releases while the abdomen holds its shape. The narrowed throat controls the rate, like a hand on a garden tap.

### Leave something in the tank

Do not empty your lungs completely. Stop the exhalation when you have breathed out about three-quarters of the air, while the abdomen is still firm. If you breathe out to the very last drop, the trunk loses its pressure, the posture collapses and the next inhalation becomes a gasp.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I feel dizzy or light-headed during Sanchin."**

*The problem.* After a few movements of Sanchin, you feel dizzy, your vision blurs or tingles at the edges, or your face feels hot and tight.

*Why it happens.* Usually one of two things. Either you are holding your breath or straining against a closed throat, raising the pressure in your chest; or you are breathing too hard and too fast, blowing off too much carbon dioxide.

*The solution.* Stop and rest, sitting down if necessary. When you resume, make the breath quieter and slower, and keep it moving all the time. Reduce your tension to about half of what you were using. Count the exhalation and make sure it never stops. If dizziness happens again, or comes with chest pain, a severe headache or visual disturbance, stop training and see a doctor before you continue.
:::

### Building the breath gradually

Ibuki is a demanding skill, and like any strength it should be built progressively. Plate 3.9 shows a twelve-week ladder that many of my students have followed. The inhalation stays short; the exhalation lengthens week by week. Do not move up a rung until the current one feels comfortable and controlled for a full set of Sanchin.

## Step Four: Nogare, the Soft Breath

If ibuki is the voice of *go*, nogare is the voice of *ju*. It is a quieter, softer breath, still low in the body and still controlled, but without the forceful narrowing of the throat. The sound, if there is one, is soft. Plate 3.5 shows it accompanying a soft, open-hand movement of the kind found in Tensho.

![**Plate 3.5** The soft breath with an open hand, seen in three-quarter view. The inhalation is quiet and slow as the hand gathers (a–c); the release is long and smooth as the palm presses forward and turns (d–f); the hand returns as a new breath begins (g–h).](figures/p3_5.png)

Nogare has three important uses.

**As a recovery breath.** After a hard technique or a demanding sequence, a few cycles of nogare bring the breathing back under control quickly without losing your guard.

**As the breath of Tensho.** In Tensho, tension rises and falls with each movement, and the breath must rise and fall with it. Ibuki is too abrupt for that. Chapter 9 explains the Tensho breath in detail.

**As a safe alternative to ibuki.** For students who have high blood pressure, glaucoma, a heart condition, a hernia or who are pregnant, nogare allows them to practise Sanchin with moderate tension and no risk of straining. I have taught Sanchin in this way to students in their eighties. It is not a lesser version. It is simply the *ju* side of the same kata.

## Step Five: The Kiai

The kiai is a short, sharp exhalation with a sound, timed to coincide with a decisive technique. In films it is a scream. In good karate it is something more compact: a sudden contraction of the abdomen that drives a burst of air out through the throat, producing a short, deep sound from low in the body. Plate 3.10 shows the timing.

![**Plate 3.10** The kiai with a punch in zenkutsu dachi. The body drives and accelerates in silence (a–d); at the instant of impact the abdomen contracts sharply and a short exhalation escapes with the sound (e); the tension is held for a fraction of a second (f) and released (g–h).](figures/p3_10.png)

The kiai does several things at once. It locks the trunk at the moment of impact, so that the force of the punch is not lost through a soft body. It protects the body, because a contracted abdomen absorbs a counter-strike far better than a relaxed one. And it has a powerful effect on the mind: on yours, by focusing your intent, and on your opponent's, by startling them.

The commonest mistake is to make the sound with the throat rather than the belly. A throat kiai is high-pitched and thin, and it does nothing for the body. Put your hand on your abdomen and say "Ha!" sharply. You should feel the abdominal wall jump inward under your hand. That is the movement you want, and the sound should come from it.

## Breathing Faults and Their Cures

Breathing faults are hard to see in yourself, because you cannot watch your own breath. Plate 3.8 shows seven common faults alongside the correct posture, and the troubleshooting table that follows gives you a cure for each.

![**Plate 3.8** Breathing faults in Sanchin. (a) The chest heaves and the chin lifts. (b) The breath is held. (c) The belly is pushed out and the back arches. (d) The trunk collapses forward on the exhalation. (e) The body rises on the inhalation. (f) The jaw is clenched and the chin raised. (g) The shoulders lift. (h) Correct: low, stable and centred.](figures/p3_8.png)

| Fault | How to spot it | Why it matters | Cure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest breathing | Shoulders and collarbones rise on inhalation | Tension in neck; no abdominal pressure | Hands on belly drill; slow nasal inhalation |
| Breath holding | Silence, red face, neck veins prominent | Dangerous rise in blood pressure | Keep the breath audible; reduce tension |
| Belly pushed out | Lower back arches, ribs flare | Loss of spinal stability | Widen the ribs and back, not just the front |
| Collapse on exhale | Chest sinks, head drops | Posture and pressure lost together | Keep abdomen firm; stop at three-quarters |
| Rising on inhale | Knees straighten, head lifts | Root and stance lost | Keep knees bent; breathe down, not up |
| Clenched jaw | Teeth grit, chin juts | Tension spreads to neck and head | Lips slightly apart; tongue resting on palate |
| Lifting shoulders | Shoulders creep toward ears | Arms disconnect from trunk | Draw shoulder blades down; see Chapter 5 |

### Checking with a partner

Because these faults are so hard to feel, a partner is invaluable. Plate 3.12 shows a simple partner check.

![**Plate 3.12** Checking the breath with a partner. (a) The partner places a flat hand lightly on the student's lower abdomen. (b) On inhalation, the hand should be pushed gently forward. (c) With the hand on the upper chest, there should be very little movement. (d) The same check in three-quarter view.](figures/p3_12.png)

The partner places one hand lightly on your lower abdomen and the other, if you like, on your upper chest. As you breathe, they report what they feel. The lower hand should move; the upper hand should be almost still. During the exhalation of ibuki, the lower hand should feel the abdomen stay firm and full rather than sinking away. Swap roles afterwards. You will learn as much from feeling someone else's breath as from having your own checked.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My breath runs out before the movement finishes."**

*The problem.* In Sanchin, you reach the end of your exhalation while your arm is still moving out, and you either gasp or hold the last bit of the movement without breath.

*Why it happens.* Either the exhalation is too forceful, the movement is too slow for your current capacity, or you are not filling the lungs fully on the inhalation.

*The solution.* First, check the inhalation: is it low and full, widening the belly, ribs and back? Second, narrow the throat a little more so that the air escapes more slowly. Third, shorten the movement for now, and work up the breath ladder in Plate 3.9. Never "cheat" by holding the breath to finish the movement.
:::

## The Pressure Partnership

We said earlier that the diaphragm, the abdominal wall and the pelvic floor work as a team. Plate 3.13 shows the four pressures in action.

![**Plate 3.13** The partnership that controls pressure in the trunk. (a) The diaphragm presses down. (b) The abdominal wall presses in. (c) The pelvic floor lifts. (d) All three in balance, the state we aim for in Sanchin.](figures/p3_13.png)

The pelvic floor is often forgotten in karate teaching, but it matters. If the diaphragm presses down hard while the pelvic floor is relaxed, the pressure has to go somewhere, and it pushes downward on the organs of the pelvis. Over years, this can contribute to problems such as stress incontinence, particularly in women after childbirth and in older students of both sexes. The traditional instruction to "close the anus" or "draw up the lower belly" in Sanchin is, in modern terms, an instruction to engage the pelvic floor so that it meets the pressure from above.

::: drill
**Drill: The gentle lift**

1. Sit on a firm chair. Breathe naturally into the belly.
2. On an exhalation, gently draw up the muscles you would use to stop the flow of urine, as if lifting a small marble from the chair. Use about a quarter of your maximum effort.
3. Hold the lift lightly for three to five seconds while you continue to breathe out, then release completely on the inhalation.
4. Repeat ten times. Over the weeks, add the gentle lift to your Sanchin exhalation.
:::

## Breath and the Mind

Every Goju-Ryu class ends, and many begin, with a short period of quiet sitting called *mokuso*, which means something like "silent thought". Plate 3.11 shows the posture.

![**Plate 3.11** Mokuso, quiet sitting. (a) Seiza from the front, hands resting together in the lap. (b) From the side, with the tanden marked. (c–d) The very small rise and fall of the trunk with natural breathing.](figures/p3_11.png)

Sit in seiza or on a chair, back upright, hands resting in the lap. Close your eyes or lower them to the floor in front of you. Breathe naturally through the nose, low in the body, and let your attention rest on the breath as it moves in and out. When your mind wanders, as it will, bring it gently back. That is all.

Mokuso is not mystical. It is a way of settling the nervous system before training and of absorbing the lesson afterwards. Slow breathing with a longer exhalation than inhalation reliably calms the body; you can feel the heart rate slow within a minute or two. Over months, students who practise mokuso seriously tend to find that they stay calmer under pressure, both in the dojo and outside it. In Part Five we shall see how this calm attention becomes the basis of what the old teachers called internal energy.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Breathing is driven by the diaphragm; the belly moves to make room.
- The diaphragm, abdominal wall, pelvic floor and deep back muscles form a pressurised cylinder that stiffens the trunk.
- Never hold your breath under strain. In Sanchin the breath is always moving, which is why it is audible.
- Ibuki: short, low nasal inhalation; long, audible, controlled exhalation through a narrowed throat with the abdomen firm. Stop at about three-quarters.
- Nogare: the soft, quiet breath, used for recovery, for Tensho and as a safe form of Sanchin breathing.
- The kiai is a sharp abdominal contraction at impact, not a shout from the throat.
- Breathe into the belly, the sides of the ribs and the lower back.
- Engage the pelvic floor gently to meet the pressure from above.
:::

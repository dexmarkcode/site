# The Way of Hard and Soft

::: chapter-intro
Goju-Ryu means "hard–soft style". Before you tense a single muscle in Sanchin or lift a stone weight, you need to understand what those two words are really asking of you, where the art came from, and how this textbook is built to help you solve the problems that every serious student meets. This chapter lays that groundwork and gives you a method you will use in every chapter that follows.
:::

The first Goju-Ryu class I ever watched was held in a church hall with a cracked floor and a radiator that clanked whenever the heating came on. Nobody in the room looked like a martial artist from the films. There was a retired postman, two nurses who had come straight off a shift, a teenager who could not keep still and a quiet woman in her sixties who turned out to be the most senior student there. They spent forty minutes doing what looked like very little. They stood in a narrow, pigeon-toed stance, breathed loudly through their mouths, and pushed their arms out so slowly that it was almost painful to watch. Then the teacher walked along the line and struck each of them on the shoulders, the stomach and the thighs, and not one of them moved.

I went home puzzled and slightly unsettled. I had expected kicks and shouting. What I had seen was something closer to a laboratory: people testing their own bodies against pressure, again and again, and looking for the weak point each time. It took me years to understand that the quiet hall had shown me the whole of Goju-Ryu in miniature. The hardness was there, plain to see in the tension and the striking. The softness was there too, hidden inside the breathing and in the way the senior student let the blows sink into her and simply disappear.

This book is about both halves. It is a textbook, which means it is meant to be used rather than admired. It is also a problem-solving book. Almost every section ends with the faults that students actually make, the reasons they make them and what to do about it. If you train alone, those sections are your teacher's eye. If you train in a dojo, they will help you understand the corrections you are already being given.

## What "Hard and Soft" Really Means

The name comes from a line in an old Chinese text that Okinawan karateka called the *Bubishi*, a collection of fighting principles, medical notes and techniques that circulated in handwritten copies. One of its eight precepts of the fist reads, in the usual translation, *"The way of inhaling and exhaling is hardness and softness."* Miyagi Chōjun, the founder of Goju-Ryu, took the words *go* (hard) and *ju* (soft) from that line when he gave his system a name.

Notice what the precept actually says. It does not say "some techniques are hard and others are soft". It ties hardness and softness to breathing. That small detail matters enormously, and it is the first idea I want you to carry with you. In Goju-Ryu, hard and soft are not two boxes into which we sort techniques. They are two phases of a single cycle, like the in-breath and the out-breath. One gives birth to the other.

A punch shows this neatly. The fist leaves the hip relaxed, travels quickly because nothing is fighting it, and only at the moment of contact does the body lock into a hard, connected structure. Then it softens again so that it can move on. Plate 1.9 shows the cycle frame by frame. If you are hard all the way through, you are slow and you tire quickly. If you are soft all the way through, you have speed but nothing behind it. The skill is in the timing of the change.

![**Plate 1.9** Hard and soft inside a single punch, seen from the side. The fist travels relaxed (b–c), the whole body locks for an instant at impact (d), holds briefly (e), then releases so that the arm can return and the guard can re-form (f–h). Tick marks show where tension is held.](figures/p1_9.png)

### Go: hardness as structure

Beginners often think hardness means muscle. It does not, or at least not mainly. Hardness in Goju-Ryu is the ability to arrange your skeleton so that force passes through it to the ground without collapsing a joint. Muscle tension is the glue that holds the arrangement together for the instant it is needed. A strong person in a poor posture is weak. A slight person in a well-aligned posture can be very difficult to move.

You will see this most clearly in Sanchin, the kata at the heart of the style. Sanchin builds hardness by asking you to hold a precise shape while a partner or teacher tests it. When the shape is right, the test feels almost easy. When something is out of line, even by a few centimetres, you feel it immediately as a wobble, a strain in the lower back or a knee that wants to give way.

### Ju: softness as adaptation

Softness is not weakness or floppiness. It is the ability to yield, redirect, absorb and change. A soft block does not meet the attack head-on; it rides along it and turns it. A soft body does not brace against a push; it lets the force in and sends it down into the floor. Softness is also sensitivity: the capacity to feel what a partner is about to do through the point where your arms touch.

Tensho, the second of the two "breathing" kata, is the great teacher of softness. Its open hands circle, hook and press in slow, continuous movements, and it is extremely hard to do well because there is nowhere to hide. Plate 1.3 sets some typical hard and soft shapes side by side so you can begin to see the difference with your own eyes.

![**Plate 1.3** Hard and soft shapes compared. The left-hand drawing of each pair shows a closed, braced, "go" expression; the right-hand drawing shows the open, yielding, "ju" counterpart. In practice, each contains the seed of the other.](figures/p1_3.png)

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

When students ask me whether they are "a hard person" or "a soft person", I tell them that everyone starts out on one side of the line. Strong, impatient students tend to be all *go*. Gentle, cautious students tend to be all *ju*. Your training will always ask you for the half you find difficult. If Sanchin feels natural to you and Tensho feels awkward, spend more time on Tensho, and the other way round.

## Where Goju-Ryu Came From

You do not need to be a historian to train well, but a little history saves a great deal of confusion. Many of the arguments you will read online about "the right way" to do Sanchin or the "real meaning" of a kata go back to gaps in the historical record. Knowing where those gaps are will help you hold your opinions lightly.

### Okinawa and Fujian

Okinawa, the largest island of the Ryūkyū chain, sat for centuries between China and Japan, trading with both. Okinawan nobles, officials and merchants travelled to Fuzhou in Fujian province on the Chinese coast, where the Ryūkyū kingdom kept a trading house. Through these contacts, Chinese boxing methods reached the island and mixed with local fighting practice, which Okinawans called simply *te* ("hand") or *tōde* ("Chinese hand").

### Higaonna Kanryō

The central figure in Goju-Ryu's early story is Higaonna Kanryō (1853–1915), a native of Naha. He travelled to Fuzhou as a young man and stayed there for a long period, studying with a teacher whom Okinawan tradition calls Ryū Ryū Ko. Scholars still debate exactly who this teacher was and how long Higaonna stayed. What is not in doubt is the result. Higaonna returned to Naha with a body of training that was markedly different from the karate taught in Shuri and Tomari. It used short, rooted stances, close-range hand techniques, grappling and joint attacks, a powerful method of breath control, and a demanding programme of strength conditioning with improvised tools.

Higaonna's karate was known as *Naha-te*. He was, by every account, a severe teacher. Students often spent years on Sanchin before they were taught anything else.

### Miyagi Chōjun

Miyagi Chōjun (1888–1953) began training with Higaonna as a teenager and became his foremost student. He came from a prosperous family, which gave him the freedom to train intensively for many years. He also made his own journeys to China after Higaonna's death, to research the roots of what he had learned.

Miyagi did three things that shape everything in this book. First, he systematised the training. He organised the preparatory exercises (*junbi undō*) and the strength methods (*hojo undō*) into a coherent curriculum, much of which survives in recognisable form today. Second, he created new kata to fill gaps in the teaching. Tensho, developed in the early 1920s from a Chinese form, gave students a soft counterpart to Sanchin. Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni, created around 1940, gave beginners a way in that did not require years of Sanchin first. Third, he gave the style its name. When a student, Shinzato Jin'an, was asked at a demonstration in Japan what school he practised, he had no answer, and Miyagi chose "Goju-Ryu" from the *Bubishi* precept quoted above. The name was in use by the early 1930s.

### After Miyagi

Miyagi taught a number of students who became important teachers in their own right, and from them the style spread across Okinawa, mainland Japan and the world. Plate 1.6 shows a simplified family tree. It is deliberately incomplete. Every lineage chart you will ever see has been drawn by somebody with a point of view, and this one is no exception. Its purpose is only to show you how the main names fit together, so that you can follow the discussion in other books.

![**Plate 1.6** A simplified lineage of Goju-Ryu. Dashed lines indicate relationships that are uncertain or that continue through many later teachers. Only a small selection of the many important figures in the style is shown.](figures/p1_6.png)

Today you will meet Goju-Ryu under several organisational names, and the details of the kata differ from group to group. The hand might open a little earlier in one school; the step in Sanchin might be longer or shorter in another. Do not let this worry you. The principles in this book were chosen because they are common ground. Where schools differ in a way that matters to the principle, I will tell you, and I will explain why each version makes sense.

## The Kata of Goju-Ryu

A kata is a set sequence of movements practised alone. In Goju-Ryu the kata are the textbook of the style, and every other kind of training either prepares you for them or tests what you have learned from them. Plate 1.4 shows a representative posture from each of the twelve classical kata most schools teach. These drawings are simplified. They are not meant to teach you the kata, only to give you a feel for the family and its characteristic shapes.

![**Plate 1.4** Representative postures from the twelve classical kata. Each drawing is simplified to show the characteristic stance and hand shape rather than an exact moment in any one school's version.](figures/p1_4.png)

The kata fall into three groups, and it helps to know them.

**The breathing kata.** Sanchin and Tensho are the root. They are slow, short, performed with deliberate breath control and either constant tension (Sanchin) or flowing, changing tension (Tensho). Almost everything this book has to say about rooting, tension and internal energy is taught first through these two forms.

**The introductory kata.** Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni were designed by Miyagi as a public, accessible introduction to karate. They use longer stances, simple blocks and punches, and they teach the beginner how to move.

**The classical kata** (sometimes called *kaishu kata*, "open-hand kata"). Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseru, Seipai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei came down from Higaonna's Chinese training. They contain the close-range grappling, joint attacks, throws and strikes of the system, buried in sequences that can take years to understand.

This textbook is not a kata manual. It concentrates on the qualities that make every kata work: the posture, the breath, the tension, the root and the conditioned body. When you have those, any kata your teacher shows you will come to life far more quickly.

## The Conditioning Tradition

If you visit a traditional Goju-Ryu dojo in Okinawa, you will notice a rack of strange tools along one wall. There will be stone weights on wooden handles, earthenware jars with narrow necks, heavy iron rings, a striking post wrapped in rope or leather, and perhaps a pair of iron sandals. These are the tools of *hojo undō*, "supplementary exercise", and they are as much a part of Goju-Ryu as the kata. Plate 1.5 introduces them.

![**Plate 1.5** The principal tools of hojo undō, drawn to show their basic form. Each is covered in detail, with safe progressions, in Part Four.](figures/p1_5.png)

The tools exist because Higaonna and Miyagi wanted a body that could do what the kata asked: grip and hold an opponent, absorb blows, strike without injury, and keep its structure under pressure. They had no gymnasiums or machines. They had stones, jars and timber, and they were imaginative about how to use them.

The phrase "iron body" in the title of this book refers to this tradition and to its partner, *kote kitae* or body conditioning by impact. I want to say clearly, here at the start, that iron body training done badly causes real injuries. Done well, progressively and with patience, it builds a robust, resilient body that serves you well into old age. Part Four takes you through it step by step, and it begins from a very gentle starting point.

## Internal Energy: Setting Expectations

The subtitle promises internal energy training, and you may be wondering what I mean by that. Some books on this subject speak about *ki* (Chinese *qi*) as a mysterious force that can be projected across a room. This is not one of those books.

In this textbook, "internal" refers to things you cannot easily see from the outside: the timing of tension and relaxation, the coordination of breath with movement, the connection between the feet and the hands, the quality of attention, and the sensitivity that lets you feel a partner's intention through contact. These are real, trainable skills. They have a basis in physiology and biomechanics, even if we do not yet understand every detail. They also produce sensations that the old teachers described with words like *ki*, and those words remain useful as a way of pointing your attention. Part Five explores all of this carefully. For now, it is enough to know that nothing in this book asks you to believe anything you cannot test for yourself.

## Etiquette as Training

Every Goju-Ryu class begins and ends with formal courtesy. It is tempting to see the bowing and kneeling as old-fashioned ceremony, but I would encourage you to treat it as training. The standing bow (*ritsu-rei*) and the kneeling bow (*za-rei*) are excellent posture exercises, and the formal opening of the kata is a small lesson in breath and calm.

### The standing bow

Stand in *musubi dachi*, heels together and toes turned out at about forty-five degrees, with open hands resting lightly on the fronts of the thighs. Plate 1.1 shows the sequence from the side.

![**Plate 1.1** The standing bow, ritsu-rei. The body hinges from the hips with a straight back; the head stays in line with the spine rather than dropping forward. Pause briefly at the lowest point, then rise at the same speed.](figures/p1_1.png)

1. Stand tall. Let the crown of the head lift and the chin draw slightly in.
2. Hinge forward from the hip joints, keeping the spine long, to roughly thirty degrees.
3. Keep the eyes lowered but aware. Traditionally, you do not stare at the floor.
4. Pause for a breath, then return to upright at the same pace.

The common fault is to bow from the neck, folding the head forward while the back stays upright. Another is to round the back like a question mark. Both are posture habits that will turn up again in your Sanchin. If you can hinge cleanly at the hips here, you have already learned something about how the pelvis and the spine relate.

### Seiza and the kneeling bow

Formal sitting, *seiza*, is hard on Western knees and ankles. Plate 1.8 shows how to get into it in a controlled way and how to perform *za-rei*, the kneeling bow.

![**Plate 1.8** Entering seiza and performing za-rei. Kneel on the left knee first, then the right; sit back onto the heels with the back straight; for the bow, place the hands on the floor in front and hinge forward from the hips. Rise in the reverse order.](figures/p1_8.png)

If you cannot sit in seiza without pain, do not force it. Place a folded towel between the calves and thighs or sit on a low block. Many respected teachers have had to modify their seiza after knee injuries. Your knees must carry you through decades of training; an aching ego is a far smaller price than a damaged cartilage.

### The formal opening

Most Goju-Ryu kata begin with a short formal opening. The details differ between schools, so follow your own teacher, but the version in Plate 1.2 is widely used and teaches the essentials.

![**Plate 1.2** A typical formal opening (yōi). From musubi dachi the open hands cross in front of the body, rise slightly with an inhalation, lower with an exhalation, and close into fists as the feet open to a natural stance.](figures/p1_2.png)

Treat the opening as a deliberate change of state. As the hands rise, breathe in through the nose and let the attention settle into the lower abdomen. As they lower, breathe out and let the shoulders drop. When the fists close, you are ready. I tell my students that the kata starts here and not with the first technique, and I mark them on it.

## How to Use This Textbook

This book is organised in five parts of three chapters each. Each part builds on the one before it, so it is worth reading in order the first time.

- **Part One** gives you the foundations: this orientation, then posture and stance, then breathing.
- **Part Two** is devoted to Sanchin: its stance and step, its tension, and the testing method called *shime*.
- **Part Three** develops rooting and power, and introduces Tensho and the soft hand.
- **Part Four** covers the iron body methods: the traditional tools, impact conditioning and the care of hands, joints and connective tissue.
- **Part Five** deals with internal energy, partner sensitivity through *kakie*, and building a lifelong programme.

### Reading the plates

This textbook contains a large number of line drawings arranged in plates. Each plate shows a sequence or a comparison, with the frames lettered (a), (b), (c) and so on. Plate 1.7 explains the conventions.

![**Plate 1.7** Conventions used in the plates. The same posture can be shown from the front, side, three-quarter or back. Dashed lines below the feet indicate rooting; a ringed dot on the lower abdomen marks the tanden (the body's centre); short radiating ticks show held tension; solid arrows show direction of movement.](figures/p1_7.png)

The figures are drawn in a simple, clean style on purpose. Detailed anatomy would only hide the shape you need to see. The near-side limbs are drawn in white and the far-side limbs are given a light grey wash, so you can always tell which arm or leg is nearer to you. The black belt and the lapels of the jacket help you read the angle of the hips and chest.

When you study a sequence, do not just look at it. Stand up and walk through it, slowly, frame by frame. Pause in each frame and compare your body with the drawing. Then ask yourself what changed between this frame and the last one, and where in your body that change began. That last question is the most important habit you can form. In Goju-Ryu, movement almost always begins in the centre and travels outward, and the plates are drawn to help you see that.

### The problem-solving sections

Throughout the book, you will find boxes headed **Problem and Solution**. Each describes a fault that I have seen many times, explains the cause and gives you a correction to try. Most chapters also contain a troubleshooting table and a set of review questions. Answers to the review questions are at the back of the book.

Here is the first problem box, and it concerns the most common difficulty of all.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I can't tell whether I'm doing it right."**

*The problem.* You are training at home, perhaps between classes or with no class nearby, and you have no idea whether your posture is correct.

*Why it happens.* Your sense of where your body is in space (proprioception) is easily fooled. Most people believe they are standing straight when they are leaning several degrees forward.

*The solution.* Use three tools. First, a mirror placed to your side rather than in front, so that you can check your profile. Second, a phone camera on a shelf, recording from the front and the side; watch the footage at the end of each session. Third, a wall test: stand with your heels a short distance from a wall and check whether your buttocks, upper back and head touch it in the order you expect. Each chapter suggests specific checks of this kind.
:::

### Common beginner faults

Plate 1.10 shows eight faults that turn up in almost every beginner's Sanchin. You will meet each of them again later in the book, with full explanations. For now, simply look at them and see whether any of them seem familiar. Most of us have at least three.

![**Plate 1.10** Eight common faults in the basic posture. (a) Leaning forward; (b) leaning back; (c) knees locked straight; (d) feet set too wide; (e) head dropped; (f) chin lifted; (g) hips twisted off-line; (h) elbows flared away from the ribs.](figures/p1_10.png)

| Fault | What you feel | Likely cause | First correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaning forward | Weight in the toes, calves working hard | Trying to "attack" with the body | Feel the weight over the middle of the foot; lengthen the back of the neck |
| Leaning back | Pressure in the lower back | Over-tucking the pelvis or pushing the belly forward | Tuck less; let the ribs settle down over the pelvis |
| Knees locked | Jarring through the hips when tested | Standing too tall, tension in the thighs | Soften the knees until you feel the thigh muscles share the load |
| Feet too wide | Knees cannot squeeze inward | Copying a wide stance from another style | Measure your stance (Chapter 4) |
| Head dropped | Neck strain, poor balance | Watching the floor | Look at a point at eye height |
| Chin lifted | Tightness at the base of the skull | Over-correcting a dropped head | Imagine a thread lifting the back of the crown |
| Hips twisted | Uneven pressure under the feet | Stepping off-line | Square the belt knot to the front |
| Elbows flared | Shoulders rise, arms tire quickly | Using the shoulders instead of the lats | Keep a fist's width between elbow and ribs |

## A Problem-Solving Method for Your Training

Over the years I have found that students improve fastest when they approach their faults in a consistent way. The method I teach has four steps, and you will see it running through every chapter of this book. I call it **Notice, Name, Test, Adjust**.

**Notice.** Pay attention to what actually happens, not to what you think should happen. Where does the effort show up? Where do you wobble? Where do you feel strain or pain? Write it down if you can.

**Name.** Give the problem a precise name. "My Sanchin is bad" is not a name. "My front knee drifts outward when I step" is. A named problem can be solved; a vague feeling cannot.

**Test.** Find a simple test that exposes the problem reliably. In Goju-Ryu the tests are usually physical: a push, a press on the shoulders, a tap on the stomach, a balance check. If you cannot find a test, you have not yet named the problem precisely enough.

**Adjust.** Make one change at a time and test again. If you change three things at once and the problem goes away, you will not know which change mattered, and the problem will come back.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I keep fixing one thing and breaking another."**

*The problem.* Every time you correct one part of your posture, another part goes wrong. You straighten your back and your knees lock. You soften your knees and your hips swing back.

*Why it happens.* The body is a connected system. A change at one joint is felt at every other joint. Beginners try to hold many corrections consciously at once, and the attention cannot manage it.

*The solution.* Use the Notice, Name, Test, Adjust method, and make only one change per practice block of five to ten minutes. Allow the correction to become habit before adding another. Accept that you will feel worse for a while. Improvement in Goju-Ryu is often uncomfortable before it becomes natural.
:::

## Planning Your Practice

Many people come to Goju-Ryu with a busy life and only a few hours a week to give it. The good news is that the core of the system rewards short, regular practice far more than long, occasional sessions. Twenty focused minutes a day will take you further than a single three-hour session at the weekend.

Plate 1.11 shows one way to shape a week. It is not a prescription; Chapter 15 gives you complete programmes for different levels and ages. What it shows is the balance. Breath and Sanchin appear every day, even if only for ten minutes. Kata, conditioning and partner work rotate so that the body has time to recover between harder sessions. One day is left for rest, with only gentle breathing practice.

![**Plate 1.11** The shape of a balanced training week for an intermediate student. Breathing and Sanchin appear daily; strength conditioning and partner work alternate to allow recovery.](figures/p1_11.png)

### Keeping a training journal

I ask all my students to keep a training journal, and I would ask the same of you. It need not be elaborate. After each session, write the date, what you practised, one thing that went well, one problem you noticed (named precisely, as above), and how your body felt. Over months, the journal becomes a remarkable record. It shows you patterns you would never otherwise see: that your knees ache when you train on consecutive days, or that your Sanchin is always steadier in the mornings. Appendix B includes a simple template.

### Safety first, always

Some of the training in this book is demanding. Before you begin, please read the safety notes at the front of the book. In particular, if you have high blood pressure, heart disease, a hernia, glaucoma, a history of stroke, or if you are pregnant, the hard breathing and tension methods in Parts Two and Three must be modified, and you should speak to your doctor before starting them. The iron body work in Part Four should be approached gradually, and stopped immediately if you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling.

None of this is meant to frighten you. Goju-Ryu has been practised safely by children, the elderly and everyone in between for well over a century. It has survived because, taught sensibly, it makes people stronger and healthier. The sensible teaching is the part that matters.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- *Go* (hard) and *ju* (soft) are two phases of one cycle, tied to breathing, not two categories of technique.
- Hardness is mainly skeletal alignment, held together by well-timed muscular tension.
- Softness is adaptability: yielding, redirecting, absorbing and sensing.
- Goju-Ryu developed from Higaonna Kanryō's study of Fujian boxing and was organised and named by Miyagi Chōjun.
- Sanchin and Tensho are the root kata; conditioning (*hojo undō*) is an integral part of the system.
- "Internal energy" in this book means trainable skills of timing, breath, connection and sensitivity.
- Use the method **Notice, Name, Test, Adjust**, one change at a time.
:::

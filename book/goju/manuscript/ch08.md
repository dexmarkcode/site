# Generating Power: Hip, Wave and the Kinetic Chain

::: chapter-intro
A rooted body can absorb force. This chapter turns the root around and uses it to produce force. It explains how power travels from the floor through the legs, hips and trunk to the hand, why the order of that journey matters, how Goju-Ryu generates power at very short range, and how to diagnose and fix the faults that leak power away before it reaches the target.
:::

There is a moment in every student's training that I look forward to. It usually comes after two or three years. They are hitting the makiwara or a pad, as they have done hundreds of times, and suddenly the sound changes. Instead of a slap, there is a deep, dull thud, and the pad holder takes a half step back with a look of surprise. The student looks surprised too. "I didn't hit any harder," they say. And they are right. They did not hit harder. They hit with more of themselves.

This chapter is about that change. Power in karate does not come mainly from the arm. It comes from the ground, is gathered and multiplied by the legs and hips, transmitted by the trunk and delivered by the arm. The arm is the last link, and the smallest. When all the links work together, in the right order, a modest effort produces a startling result.

## The Kinetic Chain

Sports scientists use the phrase *kinetic chain* to describe the way the segments of the body work together to produce fast, powerful movement. A tennis serve, a javelin throw, a golf drive and a karate punch all follow the same pattern: the movement begins in the large, heavy segments near the ground and the centre, and travels outward to the small, light segments at the end. Each segment speeds up, reaches its peak and then slows down, handing its momentum on to the next. Plate 8.10 shows this pattern for a punch.

![**Plate 8.10** The proximal-to-distal sequence in a punch. Each curve shows the speed of one part of the body over time. The legs and hips move first, then the trunk, then the shoulder and upper arm, and finally the forearm and fist, which reach the highest speed just before impact.](figures/p8_10.png)

The order matters enormously. If the arm moves first, it reaches the target before the hips have done anything, and the body's mass contributes nothing. If everything moves at once, the segments interfere with each other, and the result is stiff and slow. The art is to start from the ground and let each segment begin as the one before it is reaching its peak, like a wave passing along a whip.

Plate 8.1 shows the chain in a reverse punch from zenkutsu dachi.

![**Plate 8.1** The kinetic chain in a reverse punch, seen from the side. (a) The stance is set. (b) The rear foot presses into the floor. (c) The rear knee and hip extend. (d) The hip turns. (e) The chest follows the hip. (f) The arm extends, carrying the momentum of the whole body. (g) At impact the body locks briefly. (h) Recovery. Dashed arrows show the wave of movement rising from the floor.](figures/p8_1.png)

1. **The foot presses.** Power begins with the rear foot pressing down and back into the floor. The floor pushes back, and this push is the raw material of the punch.
2. **The leg drives.** The rear knee and hip extend slightly, converting the floor's push into a forward and rotational drive at the pelvis.
3. **The hips turn.** The pelvis rotates toward the target. This is the engine of the punch.
4. **The trunk follows.** The chest and shoulders rotate a fraction after the hips, stretching the spiral chain across the front of the body like an elastic band, and then releasing it.
5. **The arm extends.** The punching arm travels last, carried by the momentum of the turning trunk, and extends to the target.
6. **The body locks.** At the instant of impact, everything locks for a fraction of a second, as described in Chapter 3 and Chapter 5. This turns the moving body into a solid mass behind the fist.
7. **Release.** The body relaxes immediately so that it can move again.

## The Hips: Engine of the Body

Goju-Ryu teachers talk constantly about the hips. "Use your hips" is probably the commonest correction in any dojo. But what does it mean? Plate 8.2 shows the relationship between the hips and the shoulders, seen from above, during a punch.

![**Plate 8.2** The hips (solid line) and shoulders (dashed line) seen from above during a reverse punch. (a) Set. (b–d) The hips begin to turn and lead the shoulders. (e) The hips stop. (f) The shoulders catch up and pass them. (g) Impact. (h) Return to square. The arrow shows the direction of the target.](figures/p8_2.png)

The important thing to notice is that the hips lead and the shoulders follow. For a brief moment, frames (c) and (d), the hips are turned further toward the target than the shoulders. This twist stretches the muscles that connect the pelvis to the ribcage, the oblique muscles of the abdomen and the deep muscles of the back. Like any stretched muscle, they then contract with extra force. When the hips stop, frame (e), the shoulders whip round past them.

This "lead and follow" is the source of most of the power in a punch. Without it, the hips and shoulders turn together as one block, and the stored elastic energy is lost.

### Not too far

Beginners who have just discovered the hip often overdo it. They twist the hips so far that the stance collapses, the back foot spins and the punch ends up off-line. The hips in Goju-Ryu turn through a relatively small angle, often no more than twenty to thirty degrees. Power comes from the speed and timing of the rotation, not its size.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "When I use my hips, my back foot spins."**

*The problem.* As you rotate your hips into the punch, your rear heel lifts and the foot spins outward, and the punch loses power.

*Why it happens.* The hips are rotating further than the stance allows, or the rear knee is collapsing inward, so the rotation has nowhere to go but out through the foot.

*The solution.* Reduce the size of the hip turn. Press the rear heel firmly into the floor and keep the rear knee in line with the foot. Practise the turn slowly in front of a mirror, checking that the rear heel never lifts. Then add speed gradually.
:::

## Hip Rotation in Sanchin Dachi

In Sanchin dachi, the hips cannot turn much because the stance is short and the knees are braced inward. Yet Goju-Ryu punches from Sanchin dachi are powerful. How? Plate 8.3 shows the answer.

![**Plate 8.3** A punch from Sanchin dachi using a small, sharp hip rotation, seen from the front. (a) Chamber, hips turned slightly away. (b–c) The hip begins to turn toward the target. (d–e) The fist follows the hip. (f) Impact as the hip arrives. (g) Hold. (h) Return.](figures/p8_3.png)

The rotation is small, perhaps fifteen degrees either side, but it is very fast. Because the stance is braced, the rotation does not leak away through the legs; the floor holds the feet and knees firm, and all the turning force goes up into the trunk. This is a different kind of power from the long, sweeping drive of zenkutsu dachi. It is compact, sudden and well suited to the close range at which Goju-Ryu prefers to fight.

The braced legs act like the stock of a rifle pressed against the shoulder. The recoil has somewhere to go.

## Dropping Power

Not all power comes from rotation. Some comes from gravity. When you drop your body weight suddenly, the falling mass can be added to a downward strike. Plate 8.4 shows a hammer-fist strike from shiko dachi that uses this principle.

![**Plate 8.4** A downward hammer-fist strike using dropping power. (a) The striking arm rises. (b) The student steps out. (c–d) The body begins to sink. (e) The fist falls with the body. (f) Impact as the stance arrives. (g) The root absorbs the reaction. (h) Guard.](figures/p8_4.png)

The key is that the body and the fist arrive together. As the stance sinks into shiko dachi, the fist falls at the same time, and the moment the feet settle is the moment the fist lands. If the stance arrives first, the dropping weight has already been absorbed by the legs. If the fist arrives first, the weight has not yet started to fall.

This dropping power is a feature of many of the classical kata, particularly Saifa, Seiyunchin and Suparinpei, and it is one reason the low stances of these kata matter.

## Close-Range Power: The Elbow

Goju-Ryu is a close-range art, and at close range the elbow is one of the most powerful weapons the body has. Plate 8.5 shows a horizontal elbow strike.

![**Plate 8.5** A horizontal elbow strike at close range, seen in three-quarter view. (a) Guard. (b) The arm folds. (c) The elbow comes forward. (d) The hip drives. (e) The elbow strikes across. (f) The body locks. (g) Release. (h) Guard.](figures/p8_5.png)

The elbow strike shows the kinetic chain at its most compact. The arm itself barely moves; the elbow is carried by the turning trunk, which is driven by the hip, which is driven by the floor. If you try to strike with the arm alone, it feels weak. If you let the hip throw the elbow, it feels as though the whole body has landed on the point of the elbow.

## Kicking Power

The same principles apply to the legs, but the chain is shorter, because the kicking leg is itself the first link. Plate 8.6 shows a front kick from Sanchin dachi.

![**Plate 8.6** A front kick from Sanchin dachi, seen from the side. (a) Stance. (b) The knee rises. (c) The kick is chambered. (d) The hip pushes forward. (e) The ball of the foot strikes. (f) The leg re-chambers. (g) It lowers. (h) The stance is restored.](figures/p8_6.png)

The kick begins with the knee, which rises to point at the target. The hip then pushes forward slightly as the lower leg snaps out, so that the body's mass is behind the foot at impact. The standing leg stays bent and rooted, and the trunk stays upright. Goju-Ryu kicks are usually aimed low, at the knees, groin or abdomen, and they are fast and direct rather than high and spectacular.

The re-chamber, frame (f), is as important as the kick. A leg left hanging after a kick can be caught. Bring the knee back as fast as it went out.

## Stepping Power

When you step and punch at the same time, the body's forward momentum can be added to the punch. Plate 8.7 shows a stepping punch.

![**Plate 8.7** A stepping punch, seen from the side. (a) Zenkutsu dachi. (b) The rear foot pushes off. (c) The foot passes under the body. (d) It reaches forward. (e) It lands. (f) The punch arrives as the weight settles. (g) The root absorbs the reaction. (h) A straight line runs from the fist to the rear foot.](figures/p8_7.png)

The timing here is critical. The punch must arrive at the moment the front foot settles and the weight transfers into it, frame (f). If the punch arrives while you are still travelling, there is no root behind it; the reaction of the impact simply pushes you backward. If it arrives after the weight has settled, the forward momentum has already been absorbed by the stance. The fist and the foot must land together, or the fist a fraction after the foot.

Frame (h) shows the final check: a straight line from the fist through the shoulder and hip to the rear foot. This line is the same line along which a push was grounded in Chapter 7. The root that absorbs force is the root that delivers it.

## Training on the Makiwara

The makiwara, a flexible striking post, is the traditional tool for developing power in karate. Its great virtue is that it pushes back. A heavy bag absorbs your punch; the makiwara returns the force, so you feel immediately whether your structure holds at the moment of impact. Plate 8.8 shows the basic method.

![**Plate 8.8** Striking the makiwara from zenkutsu dachi. (a) Set the distance so that the arm is slightly bent at contact. (b) The hip starts. (c) The fist travels. (d) Contact. (e) Drive through, bending the post. (f) Recover. (g) Reset. (h) Next strike.](figures/p8_8.png)

1. **Distance.** Stand so that your fist reaches the pad with the arm still slightly bent. The final extension of the arm happens as the post bends.
2. **Strike.** Use the full kinetic chain: foot, leg, hip, trunk, arm. Strike with the two large knuckles.
3. **Drive.** Push the post back a few centimetres, keeping the wrist straight and the structure locked.
4. **Recover.** Let the post push your fist back as you relax.

Start very gently, with ten light strikes per hand, and increase slowly over months. Chapter 11 gives a full conditioning progression for the makiwara, including care of the hands. For now, use it as a feedback tool. If the post pushes you back, your root is weak. If your wrist bends, your alignment is off. If the sound is a slap rather than a thud, your body is not behind the fist.

## Short-Range Power

Goju-Ryu contains a skill that impresses everyone who feels it: the ability to generate strong power over very short distances, sometimes with the fist already touching the target. Plate 8.11 shows a training drill for this.

![**Plate 8.11** Short-range power with a partner holding a pad or forearms against the chest. (a) The fist rests against the target. (b) The body sinks slightly. (c) The feet press into the floor. (d) The hip snaps. (e) The wave reaches the fist. (f) The partner is moved. (g) Relax. (h) Reset.](figures/p8_11.png)

There is nothing magical about this. With no room to accelerate the arm, the power must come entirely from the kinetic chain: a sudden press of the feet, a sharp turn and slight drop of the hips, and an instantaneous lock of the trunk and arm. The whole body moves only a centimetre or two, but it moves as one unit, very fast. The partner feels it as a sudden, heavy shove rather than a strike.

Practise this gently, with the partner holding a thick pad against their chest, and never to the head or throat. The skill grows from the root, the hip and the lock. If those are not yet secure, short-range power will not work, however hard you try.

::: drill
**Drill: The wall shove**

Stand in Sanchin dachi facing a wall, your fist resting against a cushion held against it. Without drawing the fist back, sink, press your feet into the floor, turn your hips sharply and lock. You should feel the wall push back into your feet. Twenty repetitions on each side. When you can feel the reaction arrive in your rear foot every time, you have the beginnings of short-range power.
:::

## Where Power Leaks

Most problems with power are not a lack of strength but a leak somewhere in the chain. Plate 8.9 shows eight common leaks.

![**Plate 8.9** Power leaks. (a) Punching with the arm only, no hip. (b) Leaning the trunk into the punch. (c) The rear heel lifting. (d) Over-rotating the hips. (e) The elbow flaring out. (f) A short, bent arm at impact. (g) Rising up out of the stance. (h) Pulling the head and shoulders back.](figures/p8_9.png)

| Leak | What happens | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Arm only | Body mass not involved; weak, slappy punch | Start from the foot; let hip lead |
| Leaning in | Root lost; pushed back on impact | Keep trunk upright; drive from rear leg |
| Heel lifts | Floor push lost | Press heel down; reduce hip turn |
| Over-rotation | Stance collapses; punch off-line | Small, fast hip turn |
| Elbow flares | Arm disconnects from back | Elbow brushes ribs (Chapter 5) |
| Bent arm at impact | Elbow absorbs force | Correct distance; full extension |
| Rising up | Root lost; power goes upward | Keep head level; knees bent |
| Pulling back | Body moves away from punch | Commit the centre toward the target |

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My punches feel strong in the air but weak on the pad."**

*The problem.* Your kata techniques look and feel powerful, but when you hit a pad or makiwara, the impact is disappointing and you feel jarred.

*Why it happens.* Striking the air never tests the lock at impact. On a target, any soft link in the chain, usually the wrist, elbow or trunk, gives way and absorbs the force.

*The solution.* Strike a target regularly, gently at first. Focus on the lock: breathe out sharply and tighten the abdomen at the moment of impact, and keep the wrist straight. Use the wall shove drill to feel the reaction travel to the feet. Most students find their power on the pad doubles within a couple of months.
:::

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Power travels from the floor, through the legs, hips and trunk, to the hand.
- The order is from the large central segments outward; each starts as the previous one peaks.
- The hips lead and the shoulders follow, stretching and releasing the trunk.
- In Sanchin dachi, the braced stance lets a small, fast hip turn generate strong power.
- Dropping power adds body weight to downward strikes; the stance and fist arrive together.
- In stepping techniques, the fist lands as the weight settles.
- The makiwara tests whether the structure holds at impact.
- Most weak techniques are caused by leaks in the chain, not lack of strength.
:::

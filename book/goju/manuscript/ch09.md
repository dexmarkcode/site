# Tensho and the Soft Hand

::: chapter-intro
If Sanchin is the hard root of Goju-Ryu, Tensho is its soft flower. Created by Miyagi Chōjun as a counterpart to Sanchin, it teaches the open hand, circular movement, flowing tension and the ability to receive force rather than block it. This chapter explains the principles of Tensho, breaks down its key hand movements step by step, shows how the soft hand works against a real attack, and sets out the faults that turn Tensho into a stiff imitation of Sanchin.
:::

I once watched an old teacher perform Tensho at a seminar in a sports hall. He was in his late seventies, slightly stooped, and he moved so slowly that a few of the younger people at the back began to fidget. Then he invited one of them, a large, fit young man, to throw a punch at him, as hard as he liked. The punch came. The old man's hand rose, met the arm, seemed to stick to it and turned. The young man found himself stumbling past, off balance, with the old man's other palm resting gently against the side of his head. There was no impact at all. Nobody fidgeted after that.

That is Tensho in action. Its slowness in the kata hides a skill that is quick, subtle and extraordinarily effective at close range: the ability to meet force softly, join with it, redirect it and counter in the same movement. It is the *ju* to Sanchin's *go*, and a Goju-Ryu karateka who has only one of them has only half the art.

## The Origins and Purpose of Tensho

The name *tenshō* is usually translated as "rotating palms" or "turning hands". Miyagi developed it in the early 1920s, basing it on a Chinese form often identified as Rokkishu, a set of six hand techniques recorded in the *Bubishi*. His aim, as far as we can tell from his students' accounts, was to give Goju-Ryu a soft, open-handed partner to Sanchin.

Tensho shares Sanchin's stance, its step and its slow pace. But almost everything else is different.

| Feature | Sanchin | Tensho |
|---|---|---|
| Hands | Mostly closed fists | Open hands throughout |
| Movement | Mostly straight lines | Circles, arcs and spirals |
| Tension | High and held | Flowing; rising and falling with each movement |
| Breath | Ibuki: forceful, audible | Softer, smoother, continuous |
| Character | Hard (*go*) | Soft (*ju*) |
| Main lesson | Structure and connection | Sensitivity, adaptability, redirection |

The two kata are often practised one after the other, Sanchin first, so that the student experiences both ends of the hard–soft spectrum in a single session.

## Tension That Flows

In Sanchin, the tension is built up and held, with brief peaks and releases. In Tensho, the tension moves continuously, rising as the hand presses out and falling as it gathers back, like a wave on the shore. Plate 9.7 compares the two patterns.

![**Plate 9.7** Tension in Tensho compared with Sanchin. In Tensho (solid line), tension rises and falls smoothly and continuously with each movement. In Sanchin (dotted line), it builds steadily to a sharp peak and drops back.](figures/p9_7.png)

This flowing tension is harder than it looks. Most students find that their tension either stays high all the time, which turns Tensho into a stiff Sanchin with open hands, or vanishes altogether, which turns it into a vague, floppy dance. The skill is in the gradient: smooth, unbroken change from soft to firm and back.

I compare it to the way you press the accelerator of a car. Nobody stamps on the pedal and lifts off sharply when they are driving well. They squeeze it on and ease it off, so smoothly that the passengers hardly notice. Tensho asks for the same quality in your muscles.

## The Shapes of the Open Hand

Tensho uses several shapes of the open hand, each with a particular purpose. Plate 9.5 shows the main ones.

![**Plate 9.5** Shapes of the open hand used in Tensho and the classical kata. (a) The open hand. (b) The soft, slightly cupped hand used for receiving. (c) The palm heel (shōtei). (d) The "tiger's mouth" formed between the thumb and index finger. (e) The "crane's beak", fingertips gathered. (f) The "bear hand", fingers folded at the middle joints. (g) The knife edge of the hand. (h) The half fist (hiraken).](figures/p9_5.png)

In Tensho, the hand is never limp. Even when it is soft, the fingers are lightly together, the thumb is held close and there is a gentle sense of life in the hand, as though it were about to catch something. When the hand presses, the force goes through the heel of the palm, with the fingers pointing up or to the side and the wrist bent back.

### Preparing the wrists

Tensho places unusual demands on the wrists. They bend back sharply when the palm presses, and they turn constantly. Before you practise Tensho, warm them thoroughly. Plate 9.11 shows a simple sequence.

![**Plate 9.11** Warming the wrists and hands. (a–d) The palms turn slowly from facing down to facing up. (e–g) The wrists flex downward, return to neutral and flex upward. (h) The hands close gently into fists.](figures/p9_11.png)

Ten slow repetitions of each movement are enough. If your wrists are stiff or have been injured, do not force them back; Tensho can be performed with a less extreme bend until the wrists loosen with practice.

## The Rising and Pressing Palm

The first hand movement of Tensho, and the one repeated most often, is a rising, turning palm that presses forward and then down. Plate 9.1 shows it in detail.

![**Plate 9.1** The rising and pressing palm, in three-quarter view. (a) The open hand rests at the side of the body. (b) It rises along the centre line. (c) The wrist bends back. (d) The palm becomes vertical. (e) It presses forward through the heel of the palm. (f) The hand turns over. (g) It presses down. (h) It returns along a circular path.](figures/p9_1.png)

1. **Rise.** From the chamber at the side of the chest, the open right hand rises along the centre line in front of the body, palm turned toward you. Breathe in quietly.
2. **Turn.** As the hand reaches chest height, the wrist bends back and the palm turns to face forward.
3. **Press.** The palm presses forward slowly through its heel, the fingers pointing up. Breathe out smoothly. Tension rises steadily, reaching its peak as the arm approaches full extension. The elbow stays slightly bent and pointing down.
4. **Turn over.** The hand turns so that the palm faces down.
5. **Press down.** The palm presses down to the level of the lower abdomen.
6. **Return.** The hand draws back along a curved path to the chamber, relaxing as it goes. A fresh breath begins.

Throughout, the elbow stays close to the body, as in Sanchin. The arm–back line of Chapter 5 is just as important here. The difference is that the force now passes through the palm and changes direction continuously.

### What the movement means

Each part of this movement can be applied. The rising hand can lift an incoming punch or deflect it upward. The pressing palm can push an attacker's arm or body away, or strike with the heel of the palm. The downward press can trap an arm or press down an attempt to grab or kick. Taken together, the movement teaches the hand to stay in contact with an opponent while it changes direction, which is the heart of the soft hand.

## Mawashi Uke: The Circular Block

*Mawashi uke*, the "turning" or "circular" block, is perhaps the most famous technique in Goju-Ryu. It appears at the end of Tensho, as well as in Sanchin and many of the classical kata. Plate 9.2 shows it from the front and Plate 9.3 from the side.

![**Plate 9.2** Mawashi uke, seen from the front. (a) The hands begin apart, one high and one low. (b) They begin to circle. (c) They cross in front of the body. (d) The circle is completed, the positions reversed. (e–f) The hands draw in to the chest, palms forward. (g) A double palm push. (h) The finished "tiger's mouth" position.](figures/p9_2.png)

![**Plate 9.3** The same sequence of mawashi uke seen from the side, showing how the hands stay in front of the body and how the double push is delivered from the root.](figures/p9_3.png)

The two hands move together in a large circle, rotating around a point in front of the chest, like two hands on a steering wheel turning in opposite directions. As they circle, one sweeps down and the other sweeps up, so that together they cover high and low lines at once. They then draw back to the chest, turn and push out together, the upper hand at face height and the lower at belly height, with the palms forward and the thumbs and fingers spread to form the "tiger's mouth".

Plate 9.6 shows the paths of the hands in this and the other Tensho movements, simplified into four basic shapes.

![**Plate 9.6** The paths of the soft hand. (a) The full circle of mawashi uke. (b) The arc of the rising palm. (c) The wave of the hooking hand. (d) The spiral of the turning wrist.](figures/p9_6.png)

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My mawashi uke looks like windmills."**

*The problem.* Your hands circle in large, separate arcs that look impressive but feel disconnected, and the final push has no power.

*Why it happens.* The circles are being driven from the shoulders rather than the centre, and the hands are travelling too far from the body.

*The solution.* Make the circle smaller, roughly the width of your shoulders, and keep it in front of your chest. Feel the movement begin from a slight turn of the hips and a sink of the stance, so that the arms follow the centre. Keep the elbows low and heavy throughout. The final push should feel like the Sanchin double push from Chapter 5, rooted in the rear foot.
:::

## Kake Uke: The Hooking Hand

Another key soft-hand technique, though it belongs more to the classical kata and to partner work than to Tensho itself, is *kake uke*, the hooking block. It shows the principle of the soft hand very clearly. Plate 9.4 shows it.

![**Plate 9.4** Kake uke, the hooking block, in three-quarter view from neko ashi dachi. (a) Ready. (b) The open hand rises inside the line of the attack. (c) It meets the attacking arm. (d) The wrist hooks around it. (e) It leads the arm outward. (f) It draws the arm in. (g) The other hand counter-punches. (h) Hold.](figures/p9_4.png)

The hand does not knock the attacking arm away. It meets it, hooks round it with the bent wrist, and leads it, so that the attacker's own force carries them slightly off balance. The hooking hand then draws the arm in, pulling the attacker onto the counter-punch. Reception and counter are one movement.

## The Soft Hand Against a Real Attack

Plate 9.9 shows the soft hand applied against a punch with a partner.

![**Plate 9.9** The soft hand receiving a punch. (a) The partners face each other. (b) The punch comes. (c) The defender's open hand meets the punching arm. (d) It sticks to the arm and turns. (e) It leads the punch past. (f) The defender counters. (g) Check. (h) Reset.](figures/p9_9.png)

There are four principles here, and you will meet them again in Chapter 14.

**Meet, do not clash.** The hand rises to meet the attack early, before it has gathered full speed, and at an angle rather than head-on.

**Stick.** Once contact is made, the hand stays in contact. It does not bounce off. Through the contact, you feel where the attacker's force is going.

**Lead.** You do not reverse the attack. You add a little to it, in a direction that takes it past your body. The attacker's own momentum does most of the work.

**Counter in the same breath.** As the attack passes, you are already in position, at close range, with the attacker slightly off balance. The counter follows naturally.

::: drill
**Drill: The sticking hand**

Stand facing a partner, both in Sanchin dachi, with your right forearm touching the outside of their right forearm at wrist height. Your partner slowly moves their arm in any direction, forward, back, up, down or in circles. Your job is to keep your forearm in light contact with theirs at all times, without pushing or pulling. Close your eyes after the first minute. Swap roles. This simple drill is the foundation of the sensitivity training in Chapter 14.
:::

## The Downward Press

Tensho also contains a double downward palm press, in which both hands press down slowly in front of the body as the stance sinks. Plate 9.8 shows it.

![**Plate 9.8** The double downward press, seen in three-quarter view. (a) The palms are at chest height. (b) They turn to face down. (c) The press begins with the exhalation. (d) The stance sinks a little. (e–f) The palms press down to the level of the hips. (g) The root absorbs the reaction. (h) The hands rise with a fresh breath.](figures/p9_8.png)

This movement shows clearly how the soft hand connects to the root. The palms do not press with the strength of the arms. As the breath goes out, the stance sinks slightly, and the hands sink with it, carried by the weight of the body. The feeling is of pressing something down into the floor. Applied, the movement can trap a grab, crush a kick or press an opponent's arms down to open a line for a strike.

## The Wrist-Top Block

The Goju-Ryu classical kata contain a block made with the top of the bent wrist, called *kōken uke*. It belongs naturally alongside the soft hand, because it uses the same bent wrist and the same rising, curving path. Plate 9.12 shows it.

![**Plate 9.12** The wrist-top block (kōken uke). (a) Ready. (b) The wrist bends with the fingers pointing down. (c) It rises in an arc. (d) The top of the wrist lifts the attacking arm.](figures/p9_12.png)

The bent wrist is surprisingly strong and gives a smooth, rounded surface that slides easily under an attacking arm. It is one of the techniques that the iron-body hand conditioning of Part Four is designed to support.

## Breathing in Tensho

The breath in Tensho is softer than ibuki. In many schools, the inhalation is through the nose and the exhalation through the mouth with a soft sound, as in nogare; in others, both are through the nose. Follow your teacher. What matters is that:

- the breath is continuous, never held;
- each exhalation matches one pressing movement, and each inhalation one gathering movement;
- the breath and the tension rise and fall together, so that the end of the exhalation coincides with the peak of the press;
- the breath stays low, in the belly, ribs and back, as in Chapter 3.

Because the breath is gentler, Tensho is the kata I give first to students who cannot safely use ibuki. With nogare breathing and moderate tension, it is suitable for nearly everyone.

## Faults in Tensho

Plate 9.10 shows seven common faults and the correct posture.

![**Plate 9.10** Faults in Tensho. (a) The palm not vertical, so the force goes through the fingers. (b) The hand pressing off the centre line. (c) The elbow lifting. (d) Leaning after the hand. (e) The arm locked straight. (f) The eyes following the hand, dropping the head. (g) The stance lost, knees straightening. (h) Correct.](figures/p9_10.png)

| Fault | Effect | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Palm not vertical | Force through fingers; wrist weak | Bend the wrist back; press through the heel |
| Hand off the centre line | Movement weak; guard opened | Rise and press along the centre line |
| Elbow lifts | Arm disconnects from back | Elbow heavy and pointing down |
| Leaning after the hand | Root lost | Let the hand go out; keep the centre back |
| Arm locked straight | Elbow joint loaded | Stop just short of full extension |
| Watching the hand | Head drops; posture collapses | Eyes forward, hand in peripheral vision |
| Tension always high | Tensho becomes stiff Sanchin | Let the tension fall on every gathering movement |
| Tension absent | Movement vague; no connection | Build tension steadily through each press |

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "Tensho bores me."**

*The problem.* You understand Tensho intellectually, but in practice it feels slow, pointless and dull, and your mind wanders.

*Why it happens.* Without a partner or an application in mind, the slowness of Tensho can feel empty. Many students, especially strong and active ones, have never been asked to pay attention to small changes in their own bodies.

*The solution.* Practise the sticking hand drill and the soft-hand reception with a partner for a few weeks. Then return to Tensho and imagine the partner's arm against your hand in every movement. Also try performing Tensho with your eyes closed, attending only to the rise and fall of tension and breath. Most students find that once they have felt the soft hand work, Tensho becomes fascinating.
:::

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Tensho, created by Miyagi Chōjun, is the soft counterpart to Sanchin: open hands, circular movement, flowing tension.
- Tension in Tensho rises and falls smoothly with each movement, like a wave.
- The pressing palm works through its heel, with the wrist bent back and the elbow heavy.
- Mawashi uke circles both hands around a point in front of the chest and finishes with a rooted double push.
- The soft hand meets, sticks, leads and counters.
- The breath is softer than ibuki, continuous and synchronised with the tension.
- Tensho is suitable for nearly every student and is the safe alternative for those who cannot use hard breathing.
:::

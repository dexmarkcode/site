# Sanchin Tension: Chains, Locking, Release and Timing

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin tension is the most misunderstood skill in Goju-Ryu. Done well, it links the whole body into one connected structure that can deliver and absorb force. Done badly, it is a red-faced strain that teaches nothing but rigidity. This chapter explains what the tension is, which muscles do the work and in what order, how to grade it, when to release it, and how to build it safely over months rather than days.
:::

There is a student in my dojo, a schoolteacher in her fifties, who came to Goju-Ryu after many years of yoga. In her first months of Sanchin she was the most tense person in the room. Her jaw was clamped, her fists were white, her neck stood out like rope, and when I pressed on her shoulders she shook. She was trying very hard, and the harder she tried, the weaker she became.

What finally changed things for her was a single sentence. I asked her to use half as much effort and to put it in different places: less in her hands and neck, more in her back, her inner thighs and her lower belly. Within a fortnight she was steadier than she had been in a year. "It's not about squeezing," she told me afterwards, "it's about connecting." I could not have put it better, and that is the theme of this chapter.

## What Sanchin Tension Is, and What It Is Not

When you tense a muscle without moving the joint it crosses, the contraction is called isometric. Sanchin uses isometric tension, and it also uses tension during slow movement, which is a kind of resisted movement. Both have a long history in strength training, and both build strength, stability and control.

But Sanchin tension is more than isometric exercise. Its purpose is not to make individual muscles stronger, though it does that. Its purpose is to make the body behave as a single connected unit, so that force applied anywhere is carried through the whole frame to the ground, and force generated from the ground can reach the hands without leaking away through a soft joint.

Think of a ladder. Leaning against a wall, a ladder can carry a person's weight easily, because the load travels straight down its rails into the floor. Now loosen every rung so that the rails can wobble independently. The same wood, the same weight, but the ladder will fold. Sanchin tension tightens the rungs. It does not need to be enormous. It needs to be in the right places and present at the right moments.

Sanchin tension is **not**:

- maximal effort all the time;
- gripping the fists and the jaw;
- holding the breath;
- shaking (a little trembling in early training is normal, but violent shaking means the load is too high);
- a test of who can strain hardest.

## The Sanchin Guard

Before we can talk about where the tension goes, we need the shape that holds it. Sanchin is performed with a characteristic guard in which the elbows are low and close to the body, the forearms rise at an angle and the fists sit about level with the shoulders. Plate 5.1 builds it step by step.

![**Plate 5.1** Building the Sanchin guard, seen from the front. (a) Arms hanging. (b) The elbows bend. (c) The forearms rise with the hands open. (d) The elbows settle a fist-width from the ribs. (e) The hands are open. (f) The fists close. (g) The guard is set, with tension at the elbows. (h) Checking the line of the forearm.](figures/p5_1.png)

1. Stand in Sanchin dachi with the arms hanging naturally.
2. Bend the elbows and bring the forearms up in front of you, hands open.
3. Allow the elbows to settle downward and slightly inward, until there is about the width of a fist between each elbow and the side of your ribs.
4. Raise the hands until the fists, when closed, are roughly level with the shoulders and about shoulder-width apart.
5. Close the fists firmly but not crushingly, the thumbs locked over the first two fingers.
6. Let the shoulders settle down and slightly back, so that the shoulder blades sit flat on the ribcage.

Plate 5.2 shows the reference measurements and four common faults.

![**Plate 5.2** Measuring the guard. (a) The fists are about shoulder height. (b) The elbows sit a fist-width from the ribs. (c) The forearm rises at an angle from the elbow. (d) Three-quarter view. Faults: (e) elbows flared outward; (f) fists too high, lifting the shoulders; (g) fists too low; (h) fists too close together.](figures/p5_2.png)

### Forming the fist

A loose or badly formed fist will spoil the guard and injure your hand when you strike. Plate 5.11 shows the traditional method.

![**Plate 5.11** Forming the fist, seen from the side. (a) Open hand. (b) The fingertips curl to the base of the fingers. (c) The fingers roll into the palm. (d) The fingers are rolled tight. (e) The thumb locks across the first two fingers. (f) The striking surface, the two large knuckles of the index and middle fingers, seen from the front.](figures/p5_11.png)

Curl the fingertips first, then roll the fingers into the palm, and only then fold the thumb across the index and middle fingers. Never tuck the thumb inside the fist; it will break when you hit something. In Sanchin, the fist is firm, but the forearm muscles should not be so tight that they cramp. Grip as if holding a small bird that you do not want to crush and do not want to let go.

## The Chains of Tension

The single most useful idea I can give you about Sanchin tension is that it runs in chains. Muscles do not work alone. They are linked by connective tissue, called fascia, into long continuous lines that run from the feet to the hands. When one link in the chain contracts, it pulls on the next. Sanchin trains you to feel and use these chains as wholes.

Researchers and manual therapists describe these lines in slightly different ways, and you do not need to learn their anatomical names. For a karateka, the following eight chains are the ones that matter. Plate 5.3 shows them with dotted lines on the figure.

![**Plate 5.3** Eight chains of tension in Sanchin. (a) The arm–back line, from the forearm through the elbow and the large muscles of the back to the hip. (b) The front line through the abdomen to the thighs. (c) The back line from the head down the spine to the heel. (d–e) The two spirals, crossing the trunk from each shoulder to the opposite hip and knee. (f) The inner leg line through the adductors and the knees. (g) The deep core around the tanden. (h) All chains together.](figures/p5_3.png)

### The arm–back line

This is the chain that connects your arms to your trunk, and it is the one most beginners miss. When the elbows press gently down and in, the large, flat muscles of the back (the *latissimus dorsi*, or "lats") contract and draw the upper arm toward the hip. The serratus muscles on the sides of the ribs hold the shoulder blades flat. Together, these muscles lock the arm into the trunk.

You can feel this easily. Stand in the guard and ask a partner to push your fist straight back toward your shoulder. If you resist with the shoulder and the front of the arm, you will feel strain in the shoulder joint and the push will move you. Now press the elbow down toward your hip as though squeezing a book against your side. The resistance suddenly comes from your back and the push travels into your body rather than into your shoulder. That is the arm–back line.

### The front and back lines

The front line runs from the top of the chest through the abdominal muscles to the front of the thighs and the shins. The back line runs from the base of the skull down the long muscles of the spine, through the buttocks and hamstrings, to the calves and heels. In Sanchin, these two lines are held in balance, so that the trunk is neither pulled forward nor arched backward. If the front line dominates, you curl forward; if the back line dominates, your lower back arches and your chest lifts.

### The spirals

Two spiral chains cross the body diagonally, each from one shoulder, across the front of the trunk, to the opposite hip, and on down the leg. These are the chains that turn the body, and they are the chains that a twisting punch uses. In Sanchin they are held in tension at the same time, which locks the trunk against twisting. Later, in Chapter 8, we shall release one spiral while engaging the other to generate rotational power.

### The inner leg line

The adductor muscles on the inside of the thighs draw the knees toward each other. This is what closes Sanchin dachi and turns the two legs into a single base. It is the source of the "ball between the knees" feeling described in Chapter 4.

### The deep core

Beneath all these sits the deep core: the diaphragm above, the pelvic floor below, the deep abdominal muscle (the *transversus abdominis*) around the front and sides, and the small, deep muscles of the spine at the back. This is the pressurised cylinder of Chapter 3. Every other chain anchors to it, which is why Goju-Ryu teachers insist that tension begins in the tanden.

## The Order of Engagement

You cannot tense eight chains at once by willpower alone. The attention cannot manage it, and the result is a general, undirected squeeze. Instead, build the tension in a consistent order, from the ground upward and from the centre outward. I teach this order:

1. **Feet.** Spread the weight over the tripod of each foot and let the toes rest on the floor.
2. **Inner legs.** Draw the knees gently toward each other until you feel the inner thighs engage.
3. **Pelvis and buttocks.** Draw the tailbone gently down and tighten the buttocks slightly, so the pelvis is held level.
4. **Deep core.** Engage the pelvic floor and the lower abdomen, as in Chapter 3, so that the tanden feels full and firm.
5. **Back.** Press the elbows gently down toward the hips, engaging the lats, and let the shoulder blades settle flat.
6. **Arms.** Finally, firm the forearms and close the fists.

When you can run through this sequence slowly, spend ten seconds on each step, then five, then two. After some months it becomes a single, smooth wave of engagement that rises from the floor to the fists in the time it takes to breathe in.

::: drill
**Drill: The rising wave**

1. Stand in Sanchin dachi in the guard, relaxed, breathing naturally.
2. Inhale. As you breathe out slowly with ibuki or nogare, engage the six links in order, one per second: feet, inner legs, pelvis, core, back, arms.
3. Hold the completed tension for the rest of the exhalation.
4. Inhale and release everything to a light base tone.
5. Repeat five times. Then try to make the wave smoother, so the links blend into each other.
:::

## Grading the Tension

Here is a truth that took me years to understand: **Sanchin is not performed at maximum tension.** If you are at one hundred per cent all the time, you have nothing in reserve, you cannot feel anything, and you cannot adjust. You also tire within a minute and train yourself to be stiff.

Instead, think of tension as a dial with several settings. Plate 5.7 shows how the tension rises and falls during one Sanchin punch, alongside the breath.

![**Plate 5.7** Tension and breath during one Sanchin punch. A light base tone is held throughout. Tension rises steadily as the fist moves out with the exhalation, peaks briefly at full extension (grey band), then falls back to base tone as the fist returns and the next breath is drawn in.](figures/p5_7.png)

I use four settings.

**Base tone (about a quarter).** The stance is set, the chains are connected, but the muscles are not working hard. This is how you stand between techniques. You could hold it for many minutes.

**Moving tension (about half).** As the arm moves, the body works harder, but not so hard that movement becomes jerky. The tension is smooth and even.

**Peak (close to full, briefly).** At the moment of full extension, or the moment of impact in a real technique, everything locks for an instant. This is the hardest the body gets, and it lasts no more than a second or so.

**Release (back towards base tone).** Immediately after the peak, the tension drops, though never to nothing. The structure stays connected.

These percentages are approximate. Nobody can measure them precisely by feel. What matters is that you recognise distinct levels and move between them deliberately. Plate 5.8 shows the levels marked on the figure across one punch.

![**Plate 5.8** Tension levels during a Sanchin punch, seen from the front. Tick marks show where tension is concentrated. (a–b) Base tone. (c) Softening slightly to let the arm move. (d) Moving tension. (e) Peak: the whole body locks, including both knees and the striking arm. (f) Releasing to about three-quarters. (g) Returning at half. (h) Back to base tone.](figures/p5_8.png)

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "I shake violently when I tense."**

*The problem.* As you build tension in Sanchin, your arms, legs or whole body start to tremble, sometimes so much that a partner can see it.

*Why it happens.* Some trembling is normal when muscles are working near their limit; it is the nervous system struggling to coordinate many muscle fibres at once. Violent shaking means you are working far too hard for your current strength, or the load is in the wrong muscles.

*The solution.* Reduce the tension to about half. Use the rising wave drill to put the tension in the large muscles of the back, hips and legs rather than the arms and neck. Over weeks, increase the tension a little at a time. The trembling will reduce as your nervous system learns to recruit the muscles smoothly.
:::

## The Sanchin Punch, Step by Step

With the stance, guard, breath and chains in place, we can now put them together in the Sanchin punch. Plate 5.4 shows it from a three-quarter angle and Plate 5.5 from the side.

![**Plate 5.4** The Sanchin punch in three-quarter view. (a) Guard. (b) The right fist draws back to the chamber at the side of the chest with an inhalation. (c) It travels forward with the exhalation. (d) Full extension with peak tension. (e) The hand opens. (f) It grasps. (g) It pulls back to the chamber. (h) It returns to the guard.](figures/p5_4.png)

![**Plate 5.5** The same sequence seen from the side, showing the path of the fist close to the body and the constant height of the head.](figures/p5_5.png)

1. **Chamber.** From the guard, draw the right fist back along the side of the body to rest at the side of the chest, palm up. Breathe in through the nose as you do so. The left fist stays in the guard.
2. **Travel.** Begin the exhalation. The fist moves forward slowly and steadily, staying close to the body, the elbow brushing the ribs. The fist begins to turn over as the elbow leaves the body.
3. **Extension.** The arm arrives at full extension in front of the solar plexus, palm down. The elbow is not locked straight; it is fully extended but held by the muscles around it. Tension peaks.
4. **Open and grasp.** The fist opens, then the hand turns and closes as if grasping an opponent's sleeve or wrist.
5. **Pull back.** The hand draws back to the chamber, pulling against imagined resistance, with a fresh inhalation.
6. **Guard.** The fist returns to the guard, and the cycle continues with the other hand.

The details of steps 4 and 5 vary considerably between schools. Some open and grasp on every punch; others only on certain repetitions; others not at all. Follow your teacher. The principles of the chains and the grading of tension apply whatever the details.

### The elbow and the ribs

The most important point in the punch is the path of the elbow. As the fist travels, the elbow stays close to the body, brushing the ribs, for as long as possible. This keeps the arm–back line engaged right up to the moment the arm extends. If the elbow swings outward early, the arm disconnects from the back and the punch is carried by the shoulder alone.

::: problem
**Problem and Solution: "My shoulder aches after Sanchin."**

*The problem.* After Sanchin practice, the front of one or both shoulders aches, sometimes for a day or more.

*Why it happens.* Almost always, the arm is being held up and pushed out by the muscles at the front of the shoulder instead of being connected through the arm–back line. Often the shoulders are also lifting toward the ears.

*The solution.* Lower the tension and concentrate on pressing the elbow down toward the hip throughout the punch. Check the shoulder blades with the back-view check in Plate 5.9. Strengthen the back with the exercises in Chapter 12. If the ache persists more than a few days, see a physiotherapist.
:::

## Setting the Shoulder Blades

The shoulder blades are the foundation of the arms. If they float, ride up or wing away from the ribs, the arms cannot connect to the trunk however hard you tense. Plate 5.9 shows the correct setting and four faults, mainly from behind.

![**Plate 5.9** Setting the shoulder blades, seen mainly from behind. (a–b) Correct: the blades sit flat and slightly down. (c) Fault: the blades pinched together. (d) Fault: the upper back rounded. (e) Fault: the elbows lifted. (f) Correct: the lats engaged, linking elbow to hip. (g) From the side, the shoulder sits low. (h) Fault: the shoulder hunched forward.](figures/p5_9.png)

The instruction I give is: *shoulders down, blades flat, elbows heavy.* Imagine the shoulder blades sliding down your back into your back pockets. Do not pinch them together hard; that is the parade-ground fault of Chapter 2 and it locks the upper back. Simply let them settle and lie flat.

## Closing Movements

Sanchin ends with a series of open-hand movements in which the hands push forward, circle and push again. These movements change the quality of the tension: the hands are open, the fingers are firm, and the force is directed through the heel of the palm. Plate 5.6 shows one common version.

![**Plate 5.6** A typical closing sequence of Sanchin, seen in three-quarter view. (a) The open hands draw back to the sides of the chest. (b) The palms turn forward. (c) They push out together. (d–e) The hands circle, one rising and one falling. (f) A double push with the palms. (g) Return. (h) Close.](figures/p5_6.png)

Here the chains of tension are the same, but the end point is the palm instead of the knuckles, and the circling movements require one spiral to lengthen while the other shortens. They are a bridge between the hard tension of Sanchin and the flowing tension of Tensho, which we meet in Chapter 9.

## Building Tension Safely: Isometric Holds

Sanchin itself is the best training for Sanchin tension. But some students benefit from supplementary isometric holds that let them feel the chains clearly without the complexity of the kata. Plate 5.10 shows eight that I use.

![**Plate 5.10** Isometric holds for learning Sanchin tension. (a–b) Pressing the palms against a wall in Sanchin dachi. (c) A high press. (d) Pressing outward against a door frame. (e) A partner pressing against the guard. (f) Self-resisted pressure. (g) A shiko dachi hold. (h) A plain Sanchin hold with root and centre.](figures/p5_10.png)

Hold each for three to five breaths with nogare or gentle ibuki, at no more than half your maximum effort. Rest for twice as long as you held. Do two or three rounds. As the weeks pass, increase the effort, not the duration. Long, maximal holds are not necessary and raise the blood pressure more than short, moderate ones.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

If you have high blood pressure, keep isometric work to short holds at low intensity, never hold your breath, and check with your doctor first. Interestingly, there is research suggesting that regular, moderate isometric training, such as hand-grip and wall-sit exercises, can help to lower resting blood pressure over time in some people. That is not a licence to strain. It is a reminder that the dose makes the difference.
:::

## A Twelve-Week Tension Progression

| Weeks | Tension | Breath | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Base tone only (about a quarter) | Natural | Shape of stance and guard |
| 3–4 | Base tone, rising to half on punches | Nogare | Arm–back line; elbows heavy |
| 5–6 | Half, rising to three-quarters at extension | Nogare or gentle ibuki | Rising wave order |
| 7–8 | As above, with brief peak | Ibuki, 6–8 second exhale | Inner leg line and deep core |
| 9–10 | Full grading: base, moving, peak, release | Ibuki, 8–10 second exhale | Smooth transitions between levels |
| 11–12 | Full grading under light partner testing | Ibuki | Holding connection when touched |

Progress through the table only as fast as your body allows. If you develop aches, trembling or dizziness, drop back two weeks.

## A Troubleshooting Guide to Tension

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Red face, neck veins prominent | Tension in throat and neck; breath held | Relax jaw; keep breath audible; reduce effort |
| Fists cramp | Gripping too hard | "Small bird" grip; tension from back, not hand |
| Shoulder ache | Arm–back line not engaged | Elbows heavy; press toward hips |
| Lower back ache | Back line dominant; pelvis tipped | Draw tailbone down; balance front and back lines |
| Knees ache | Inner leg line pushing knees off line | Check knees over feet (Chapter 4) |
| Exhausted in one minute | Constant peak tension | Use base tone; peak only briefly |
| Can't feel anything | Too much tension everywhere | Halve the effort; rising wave drill |
| Partner's push moves you | Chains not connected | Test each chain separately (Chapter 6) |

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sanchin tension connects the body into one unit; it is not maximal squeezing.
- Build the guard carefully: elbows a fist from the ribs, fists at shoulder height, shoulders down.
- Tension runs in chains: arm–back, front, back, two spirals, inner leg and deep core.
- Engage in order: feet, inner legs, pelvis, core, back, arms.
- Grade the tension: base tone, moving tension, brief peak, release.
- In the punch, the elbow brushes the ribs for as long as possible.
- Set the shoulder blades flat and down; keep the elbows heavy.
- Build tension over weeks and months, increasing effort rather than duration.
:::

# Beyond the First Textbook

::: chapter-intro
You already know how to stand, how to breathe and how to perform the first kata of the system. This chapter explains what is different about the work ahead, why so many capable students stall at this point, and how to approach the classical kata so that they become a fighting method rather than a collection of shapes.
:::

The first time I watched a senior instructor perform Suparinpei, I was a green belt with about two years of training behind me. I remember very little about the kata itself. What I remember is the feeling of looking at something I could not read. I could see stances I recognised and techniques I had been taught, but I could not see why they were arranged the way they were, why some movements were slow and others explosive, or why the performer seemed to be listening to something I could not hear. It was like watching someone read aloud in a language I had only just begun to learn. I knew some of the words. I did not know what the sentences meant.

That experience is common, and it is not a sign that anything is wrong. It is a sign that you have reached the edge of what basic training can teach you. The first stage of Goju-Ryu is about building a vocabulary: the stances, the blocks, the strikes, the breathing, the basic patterns of movement. The second stage, which is the subject of this book, is about learning to read and eventually to write in that language. It is slower work, and it asks different things of you.

## What the first textbook gave you

Let us be clear about the ground you are standing on, because everything in this volume assumes it.

By the end of the first volume you were expected to be able to stand correctly in the basic stances of the system: *musubi dachi* and *heiko dachi* for formal positions, *sanchin dachi* as the root stance, *shiko dachi* as the low, wide, stable stance, *zenkutsu dachi* as the long forward stance and *neko ashi dachi* as the short, mobile cat stance. You learned the basic blocks, including *jodan age uke*, *chudan uke*, *gedan barai* and the circular *mawashi uke*, and the basic strikes and kicks. You learned the preparatory exercises, *junbi undo*, which warm and condition the body before training. You learned *Gekisai Dai Ichi* and *Gekisai Dai Ni*, the two kata composed by Chojun Miyagi around 1940 as an accessible introduction to the system. And you began the long work of Sanchin.

If any of that ground feels shaky, I would encourage you to go back and firm it up before going much further. There is no shame in it. Senior students in every serious dojo spend part of every week on basics, and the best of them spend a great deal. The faults that show up in advanced kata are, nine times out of ten, faults in the basics that have been carried forward and magnified.

## Heishu and kaishu

Goju-Ryu kata are traditionally divided into two groups. The *heishu* kata, meaning "closed hand", are Sanchin and Tensho. The *kaishu* kata, meaning "open hand", are all the rest.

It is natural to assume that these names refer to the shape of the hands. That is partly true. Sanchin is performed largely with closed fists, and Tensho, despite its soft, flowing hand movements, is grouped with it. But the deeper meaning lies elsewhere. The heishu kata are closed in the sense that they are concerned with the inside of the body: with breathing, with tension and relaxation, with the alignment of the skeleton and the strengthening of the muscles that hold it together. They train the engine. The kaishu kata are open in the sense that they face outwards. They are concerned with the opponent, with distance, angle and timing, with the application of the engine to the problem of fighting.

The two groups are not separate. A good kaishu kata is full of Sanchin, and a good Sanchin is full of the potential to fight. But the distinction helps you to understand what you are practising at any moment. When you perform Sanchin, your attention is directed inwards, to the quality of your structure and breath. When you perform Seiyunchin, your attention must be directed outwards, to an imagined opponent who is pulling, pushing and striking you, even while your body continues to draw on everything Sanchin has given it.

::: note
**Note: Gekisai and the classical kata**

Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni are sometimes listed among the kaishu kata and sometimes treated as a separate introductory group, along with the *Fukyu* or "promotional" kata used in some schools. Strictly speaking, they are modern compositions rather than classical forms handed down from Kanryo Higaonna. In this book, when I speak of "the classical kaishu kata", I mean the eight older forms: Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseru, Sepai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei.
:::

## The eight classical kata

The table below sets out the eight classical kaishu kata in the order in which they are most often taught. The grade at which each is introduced varies considerably between associations, so treat the right-hand column as a rough guide only.

| Kata | Common interpretation of the name | Character | Usually introduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saifa | "Smash and tear" or "destroy and defeat" | Escapes from grabs; short, explosive, circular | Green or brown belt |
| Seiyunchin | "Control, pull and fight" or "calm before the storm" | No kicks; deep shiko dachi; pulling and unbalancing | Brown belt |
| Shisochin | "Fight in four directions" | Open hands; shoulder power; elbow attacks | Brown belt or first dan |
| Sanseru | "Thirty-six" | Joint kicks; double punches; close grappling | First or second dan |
| Sepai | "Eighteen" | Circular, deceptive; throws and redirections | Second dan |
| Kururunfa | "Hold long, strike suddenly" | Neko ashi dachi; breaking balance; whole-body throws | Second or third dan |
| Seisan | "Thirteen" | Fast and close; short power; knees and grabs | Third dan |
| Suparinpei | "One hundred and eight" | The longest and most complete form | Third dan and above |

: **Table 1.1** The eight classical kaishu kata of Goju-Ryu.

You will notice that four of the names are numbers. Thirteen, eighteen, thirty-six and one hundred and eight are all significant in Buddhist numerology, and one hundred and eight in particular is associated with the number of earthly desires or worldly passions that must be overcome. Thirty-six is three times twelve, eighteen is three times six, one hundred and eight is three times thirty-six. Various ingenious theories link these numbers to the number of techniques in each kata, to points on the body, or to the number of opponents a fighter might face. None of these theories can be proved. What we can say is that the names came to Okinawa from southern China with the forms themselves, and that they reflect the cultural world in which the kata were made. You do not need to solve the riddle of the numbers to train well. It is enough to know that the people who made these kata thought of them as complete and meaningful wholes, not as arbitrary sequences.

The non-numerical names are also uncertain. They are Okinawan or Japanese readings of Chinese characters whose original pronunciation and meaning have been debated for a century. The interpretations in the table are the ones most widely given in Goju-Ryu dojo today. They are useful as reminders of each kata's character, which is the main reason to know them.

## What changes in the second stage

When students move from the basic kata to the classical forms, three things change at once. Understanding them will save you a great deal of frustration.

### From shapes to problems

In the first stage of training you are, quite properly, learning shapes. The teacher shows you a stance and you try to reproduce it. The teacher shows you a block and you try to copy its path and its finishing position. You are judged largely on whether your shapes match the model.

In the second stage, the shapes are still important, but they are no longer the point. Each movement in a classical kata is an answer to a problem. Someone has grabbed your wrist. Someone is pushing into you. Someone has seized your clothing with both hands and is trying to pull you off balance. The shape you make is the solution, and its details are determined by the problem it solves. A movement that looks odd as a shape often makes perfect sense once you know the problem.

This means that you cannot really learn a classical kata well without some understanding of its applications. That is why this book discusses applications alongside the sequences, rather than leaving them all to the end. You do not need to know every application before you learn the form, but you should never perform a movement for very long without asking what problem it might solve.

### From technique to timing

The basic kata are performed mostly at a steady rhythm: block, strike, step, block, strike. The classical kata are not. They contain slow movements performed with deep breathing and full-body tension, explosive bursts of two or three techniques performed almost as one, pauses, sudden changes of direction and long passages of continuous, flowing motion. The rhythm is part of the meaning.

A common sign of a student who has learned a classical kata as a sequence of shapes is that they perform every movement at the same speed and with the same emphasis. The kata becomes flat. The experienced eye can see immediately that the performer does not know which movements are preparations and which are finishing techniques, which are blocks and which are grips, which are meant to be fast and which are meant to be slow. Learning the rhythm of a kata is as important as learning its sequence, and it usually takes much longer.

### From external to internal

The third change is the hardest to describe. In the first stage, most of your attention is on the outside of your body: where your hands are, where your feet are, whether your back is straight. In the second stage, an increasing proportion of your attention moves inside: to the connection between your feet and your hands, to the moment when your breath and your technique arrive together, to the quality of tension in your back as you pull, to the feeling of your weight dropping through your stance.

The Okinawans have a word for the kind of power that results when all of this comes together: *chinkuchi*. We shall look at it closely in the next chapter. For now, it is enough to say that you cannot see it in a mirror. You have to feel it, and then a teacher has to tell you whether what you feel is real.

## The common structure of the classical kata

Every one of the eight classical kata shares a basic structure. Understanding it will help you to learn new forms more quickly and to see the relationships between them.

Each kata begins with a formal opening. You stand in musubi dachi, bow, announce the name of the kata and move to the ready position, *yoi*, usually with the open hands crossed in front of the lower abdomen. This is not a ritual to hurry through. It is the moment in which you settle your breath, focus your attention and set your structure. A kata that begins carelessly rarely recovers.

![**Figure 1.2** The formal opening: (1) musubi dachi, hands at the sides; (2) the bow; (3) standing upright, announcing the kata; (4) the hands rise and cross; (5) the ready position, yoi, in heiko dachi; and the formal closing: (6) the hands lowered; (7) return to musubi dachi; (8) the final bow.](figures/fig1_2.png)

The body of the kata then follows. In most of the classical forms the opening section contains a short series of movements repeated three times, often moving forward or to the diagonals, which establishes the kata's character. Saifa repeats its tearing release three times. Seiyunchin repeats its descent into shiko dachi three times. Shisochin, Sanseru, Seisan and Suparinpei all open with three steps forward in sanchin dachi or a related stance. This opening is the kata's statement of intent.

The middle of the kata develops its themes, usually moving in several directions, and often contains the most characteristic techniques. The end of the kata nearly always brings you back close to your starting point and finishes with the same technique: a step back into neko ashi dachi with the circular, two-handed *mawashi uke*, in which the hands rotate around each other and push forward together in the shape of a tiger's mouth, *tora guchi*.

![**Figure 1.3** The closing mawashi uke, shown in eight stages from the front. The hands begin crossed (1), the leading hand circles up as the rear hand circles down (2–5), the wrists turn (6), and both palms push forward together in tora guchi (7–8) as the weight settles back into neko ashi dachi.](figures/fig1_3.png)

This shared ending is not a coincidence. Mawashi uke is one of the signature techniques of Goju-Ryu. It can deflect almost any incoming attack, high or low, and turn the deflection immediately into a two-handed strike or push. By ending every kata with it, the system reminds you that the art finishes with the defence that turns into an attack.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Rushing the closing**

*The fault.* The student performs the kata well until the last sequence, then hurries through mawashi uke as though it were an afterthought, with shallow breath, a weak stance and hands that wave rather than push.

*The cause.* Fatigue, relief and the belief that the "real" kata is over.

*The correction.* Practise the closing sequence on its own, ten times at the start and end of each session, with full Sanchin breathing and complete focus. Then, when you perform the whole kata, decide in advance that the closing mawashi uke is the most important movement in it. Treat it as the final sentence of a speech, the one the audience will remember.
:::

## The stalled student

I want to return now to the student I described in the preface, the capable brown belt or new black belt who has begun to stall. In my experience, this stall usually has one of four causes. Diagnosing which one applies to you is the first step to getting moving again.

### Kata blur

The first cause is what I call *kata blur*. The student has learned four or five forms, and they have begun to merge. Movements from Seiyunchin creep into Shisochin. The opening of Sanseru gets confused with the opening of Seisan. The student knows that something is wrong but cannot pin down what, and so they lose confidence in all their kata at once.

Kata blur is almost always caused by learning forms as long, undifferentiated strings of movement. The cure is to break each kata into *movement groups*: short passages of three to eight movements that share a direction, a rhythm or a purpose. Give each group a name. It does not matter what you call it, so long as the name means something to you. "The three tears", "the long pull", "the turn and kick", "the double palms". Once a kata is a sequence of eight or ten named groups rather than a string of sixty movements, it becomes far easier to remember and far harder to confuse with another kata.

This book describes each classical kata in movement groups for exactly this reason. You may find that your own groups are slightly different from mine. That is fine. The act of grouping is what matters.

::: drill
**Drill: Group by group**

Choose one kata. Write down its movement groups, either from this book or from your own understanding, and give each a short name. Then, on the dojo floor, perform the kata one group at a time, pausing for a full breath in the finishing position of each group and saying its name silently to yourself before you continue. Do this three times. Then perform the kata straight through.

Repeat the drill once a week for a month with each kata you know. Kata blur rarely survives it.
:::

### The plateau of shapes

The second cause is the one I described above: the student has learned the classical kata as shapes and is still trying to improve them as shapes. They polish their stances, sharpen their hand positions and correct their angles, and for a while they get better. Then they stop getting better, because there is only so far you can go by polishing the outside of a movement whose inside you do not understand.

The remedy is to start asking what each movement is for. You do not need a complete and authoritative answer. Any plausible answer will do to begin with, because a movement performed with a purpose is better than a movement performed without one, even if the purpose is not quite right. As your understanding grows, your answers will improve and so will your kata.

### Copying instead of learning

The third cause is subtler. Many students learn their kata largely by watching a senior student or instructor and copying them. This is unavoidable and, up to a point, desirable. But it has a hidden cost. Every performer has mannerisms: a particular way of turning the head, an exaggerated pause, a hand position that suits their body but not yours. Students who copy closely absorb all of these along with the substance, and they often cannot tell which is which.

The result is a kata that looks like someone else's and does not quite fit the student's own body. It cannot be improved by copying more closely, because the model itself is the problem.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The borrowed kata**

*The fault.* Your kata closely resembles your senior's, including details that feel awkward or unnatural to you. When you try to make it your own, you feel that you are doing it wrong.

*The cause.* You learned by imitation and absorbed another person's mannerisms along with the form.

*The correction.* Watch at least three different competent performers of the same kata, ideally from your own association. Note what all three do in the same way; that is the substance of the form. Note what they do differently; those are individual interpretations or mannerisms. Ask your teacher about any differences that puzzle you. Keep the substance exactly and allow the rest to settle naturally into your own body over time.
:::

### Training without testing

The fourth cause is the most serious. The student practises their kata diligently but never tests what they have learned. They do not do Sanchin testing, or they do it only lightly. They do not practise applications against a resisting partner. They do not do kakie or any other form of close-range pressure training. Their kata are, in effect, unchecked hypotheses.

Untested training tends to drift. Without the corrective feedback of a partner who pushes back, stances become higher and narrower, tension becomes theatrical rather than functional, and applications become choreography. The student feels that they are improving because the kata feel more comfortable, but comfort is not the same as competence.

The remedy is to build testing into your regular training: Sanchin testing (Chapter 4), kakie (Chapters 5 and 17) and resisted application drills (Chapter 18). None of these needs to be brutal. All of them need to be honest.

## How to learn a new classical kata

When you begin a new classical kata, it helps to think of the learning as taking place in four overlapping stages. These are not strict phases. You will move back and forth between them for years. But keeping them in mind will stop you from trying to do everything at once, which is the surest way to learn nothing well.

### Stage one: the sequence

First, learn the order of the movements. At this stage you should not worry much about the quality of your stances, the precision of your techniques or the rhythm of the kata. You are simply trying to remember what comes next. Use movement groups. Walk through the kata slowly, even at half the depth of the real stances, until you can perform it from beginning to end without hesitating.

Most students try to skip this stage or hurry through it. They want their new kata to look good from the first day. The result is that they spend weeks trying to perform beautiful stances while also trying to remember the sequence, and they do neither well. Accept that your kata will look rough for a while. Get the sequence first.

### Stage two: the shape

Once you can perform the sequence without thinking, begin to refine the shapes. Lower the stances to their proper depth. Clean up the paths and finishing positions of the techniques. Check your direction and your embusen, the floor pattern of the kata. This is the stage at which detailed corrections from your teacher are most useful, and also the stage at which drawings like those in this book can help you, because you can compare your shapes with the model at leisure.

![**Figure 1.4** The four stages of learning a kata, shown with the same movement from Saifa. (1–2) Stage one: the sequence walked through in high stances with approximate hand positions. (3–4) Stage two: the same movement with correct stance depth and clean finishing shape. (5–6) Stage three: the movement with power, the body dropping and the breath released at the moment of focus. (7–8) Stage four: the movement applied against a partner's grab.](figures/fig1_4.png)

### Stage three: the power

When the shapes are reasonably clean, begin to put power into them. This means working on timing, on the connection between your stance and your technique, on breathing and on the use of tension and relaxation. It also means working on the rhythm of the kata, its slow passages and its fast ones, its pauses and its bursts.

Many students reach stage three and stay there, refining their power for years. That is not a bad place to be. A kata performed with real power, real breathing and real rhythm is a fine thing. But it is not yet complete.

### Stage four: the meaning

The final stage is the study of application. You begin to test your understanding of each movement against a partner, first cooperatively and then with increasing resistance. You discover which of your interpretations work and which do not. You find that some movements have several possible applications and that the kata begins to look different depending on which ones you have in mind.

The study of application changes the kata itself. Once you have felt what it is like to break a partner's grip with the opening movement of Saifa, you will never perform that movement in quite the same way again. Your hands will be more precise, your body will turn more decisively, and your timing will reflect the real demands of the technique. This is why the old saying holds that a kata is never finished.

::: drill
**Drill: The four-stage check**

After each training session in which you work on a new kata, ask yourself one question for each stage and answer it honestly in a training notebook.

1. *Sequence:* Can I perform the whole kata without hesitation?
2. *Shape:* Which one movement is furthest from the correct shape?
3. *Power:* Which one movement lacks the most power or the right timing?
4. *Meaning:* Which one movement do I not understand at all?

Pick one answer from questions 2 to 4 and make it the focus of your next session.
:::

## Three ways to perform a kata

Classical Goju-Ryu kata are practised in at least three different ways, and a complete training programme uses all of them.

The first is the **slow, tense performance**, in which the kata is performed at a reduced speed with strong Sanchin breathing and deliberate tension through the whole body. This builds strength, structure and awareness. It shows up weak links in your stance and posture with brutal clarity. It is tiring and it should not be done every day, but done once or twice a week, it is one of the most valuable forms of training in the system.

The second is the **performance at speed**, with the rhythm and power that the kata is meant to have. This is the version you will perform at gradings and demonstrations. It develops timing, coordination and explosive power. Its danger is that, done exclusively, it can encourage you to sacrifice structure for speed.

The third is the **performance with a partner**, in which you work through the kata's applications in sequence, or at least the parts of the kata whose applications you have learned. This is sometimes called *bunkai kumite* and it is discussed at length in Part Six. It connects the solitary kata to the real problems it was designed to solve.

![**Figure 1.5** Three ways of performing the same movement. (1–3) The slow, tense performance: the punch extends gradually with full tension and audible breath. (4–6) The performance at speed: the punch is fired from a relaxed start and tensed only at the moment of impact. (7–8) The performance with a partner: the punch follows a grip-breaking movement that frees the hand.](figures/fig1_5.png)

There is a fourth way of practising that the old teachers used constantly and that modern students often neglect: **practising single movements in isolation**. Take one movement group from a kata and repeat it twenty or thirty times, on both sides if it makes sense to do so, until it becomes as natural as a basic technique. This is how the classical movements become part of your fighting vocabulary rather than remaining locked inside the kata. Many of the drills in this book are built on this principle.

## A note on the floor pattern

Each kata follows a particular floor pattern, or embusen. For the basic kata, the embusen is usually a simple line or cross. The classical kata have more varied patterns, with diagonals, turns of ninety degrees and one hundred and eighty degrees, and sections that move forward and back along the same line.

The embusen is not merely decorative. It tells you something about the kind of fighting the kata was designed to train. A kata that moves repeatedly to the diagonals, like Saifa, is concerned with stepping off the line of an attack. A kata that turns to all four directions, like Shisochin, is training you to deal with threats from any side. A kata that moves forward and back along a single line with frequent short steps, like Seisan, is concerned with close-range pressure.

![**Figure 1.6** Simplified floor patterns of the eight classical kata, seen from above, with the starting point marked by a small circle and the opening direction by an arrow: (1) Saifa; (2) Seiyunchin; (3) Shisochin; (4) Sanseru; (5) Sepai; (6) Kururunfa; (7) Seisan; (8) Suparinpei.](figures/fig1_6.png)

When you learn a new kata, sketch its floor pattern in your training notebook. This simple exercise helps the sequence lodge in your memory and gives you a way to check that you are finishing in the right place. Most of the classical kata finish on or very close to the spot where they began. If you finish a long way from your starting point, you have taken a wrong step or a wrong-sized step somewhere along the way.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Drifting off the line**

*The fault.* You finish the kata some distance to one side of, or in front of, your starting point.

*The cause.* Usually a stance that is too long or too short, a turn that is under-rotated or over-rotated, or a step that travels diagonally when it should travel straight.

*The correction.* Place a small marker, such as a coin or a piece of tape, at your starting point. Perform the kata one movement group at a time and look down at the end of each group to check your position against your expected position. When you find the group in which the drift occurs, slow it down and examine each step. Correct the error and repeat the group ten times before moving on.
:::

## The role of the teacher and the role of the book

I said in the preface that this book is a companion to instruction, not a replacement for it. I want to be specific about what that means, because the line between the two is not always obvious.

A book can give you information. It can tell you the sequence of a kata, describe the correct shape of a stance, explain the principles behind a technique and suggest drills and corrections. It can do this more patiently and more systematically than most teachers have time for in a crowded class. It can be consulted again and again.

A book cannot see you. It cannot tell you that your left shoulder is a little higher than your right, that your weight is drifting onto your heels in neko ashi dachi, or that your breath is arriving a fraction of a second after your technique. It cannot put a hand on your back during Sanchin and feel whether the muscles there are working. It cannot adjust its explanation when it sees that you have misunderstood it.

These things are the teacher's work. The most productive way to use this book is therefore to read a section, try to apply it, and then take what you have discovered to your teacher. Ask them whether you have understood it correctly. Ask them to watch you and tell you what they see. Where their teaching differs from what is written here, follow their teaching, and if you are curious, ask them why the difference exists. You will often learn more from the answer than from either source alone.

## Patience and the long view

The material in this book will take years to absorb. That can feel daunting, particularly to adults who are used to making steady and measurable progress in other areas of their lives. In karate, progress at this stage is rarely steady and rarely measurable. You will have months in which nothing seems to change, followed by a single class in which something suddenly makes sense and three or four other things fall into place along with it.

The students who do well at this stage are not always the most talented. They are the ones who keep turning up, keep asking questions, keep testing what they think they know and keep their sense of humour when it turns out to be wrong. If you can manage those four things, the rest will follow.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- The heishu kata, Sanchin and Tensho, train the body from the inside. The kaishu kata apply that body to the problems of fighting.
- The eight classical kaishu kata are Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseru, Sepai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei. The order of teaching varies between associations.
- The second stage of training shifts the focus from shapes to problems, from technique to timing, and from external form to internal connection.
- Every classical kata shares a common structure: a formal opening, a body with a characteristic opening sequence, and a closing mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.
- Students usually stall because of kata blur, a plateau of shapes, copying instead of learning, or training without testing. Each has a specific remedy.
- Learn a new kata in four overlapping stages: sequence, shape, power and meaning.
- Practise kata slowly with tension, at full speed, with a partner, and in isolated movement groups.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Write out the movement groups of every kata you currently know, giving each group a short name. Perform each kata group by group, pausing between groups.
2. Sketch the floor pattern of each kata you know. Perform each kata with a marker at the starting point and record how far from it you finish.
3. Practise the formal opening and the closing mawashi uke on their own, ten times each, with full attention and breathing, at the beginning of every session for two weeks.
4. Choose your most recently learned kata and apply the four-stage check after each session for a month, recording your answers in a training notebook.
5. Watch three competent performers of the same kata. List what they all do in the same way and what they do differently. Discuss the differences with your teacher.
6. With a partner, choose one movement from any kata you know and work out at least two possible applications. Test each one slowly and cooperatively, and note which feels more natural and why.
:::

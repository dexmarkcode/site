# Seisan: Thirteen Hands at Close Quarters

::: chapter-intro
Seisan is a fast, compact kata built for fighting at the closest of ranges. Its techniques are short, its stances are tight, and its rhythm is relentless. It combines the structure of Sanchin with grips, knees, joint kicks and dropping strikes, and it asks for a level of coordination and conditioning that only experienced students possess. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the short power on which it depends, deals with its commonest faults and explains its principal applications.
:::

Seisan is a kata with a long history and a wide family. Versions of it, under the names Seisan, Seishan, Hangetsu and others, are practised in many Okinawan and Japanese styles, from Uechi-Ryu and Shorin-Ryu to Shotokan. They differ considerably in detail. But they share a family resemblance: they are all close-range kata, built on a stance related to sanchin dachi, combining strikes with grips and short, powerful movements. It is likely that they all descend from a common Chinese ancestor, though the details of that ancestry are lost.

The Goju-Ryu Seisan is, in my view, one of the most practical of the classical kata. It has none of the slow, meditative passages of Seiyunchin or Shisochin, nor the long stillness of Kururunfa. It is fast from beginning to end, and almost everything in it can be applied at the range at which a real assault is most likely to take place: the range at which you can smell the other person's breath.

## The character of Seisan

Seisan means thirteen. As with the other numerical names, its significance is uncertain. In some styles, the kata is said to contain thirteen key techniques, or to have been designed to counter thirteen kinds of attack. In Goju-Ryu, it is simply the name of a kata.

The Goju Seisan is usually taught at around third dan, though some associations introduce it earlier. It is a medium-length kata with a relatively simple floor pattern, mostly forward and back along the main line, with turns to the sides. Its techniques include fast chudan uke and punches in sanchin dachi, open-hand blocks and grips, front kicks and knee strikes, joint kicks, crossing steps, and dropping strikes in shiko dachi.

What distinguishes Seisan is its pace and its range. Almost everything happens close in. The stances are short. The punches are short. Even the kicks are short. And the techniques follow one another with very little pause, so that the kata feels like a sustained burst of pressure rather than a series of separate exchanges.

## Short power

Seisan depends on the ability to produce power over very short distances. At the range at which the kata works, there is no room for a long punch or a big swing. Techniques must be delivered from a few inches away, and their power must come from the body, not from the distance the hand travels.

This is chinkuchi, which we met in Chapter 2: the coordination of alignment, compression and drop to produce explosive force in an instant. Seisan is perhaps the kata in which chinkuchi matters most, because without it the short techniques have no effect at all.

::: drill
**Drill: The short combination**

Stand in sanchin dachi facing a heavy bag, close enough that your elbow is bent when your fist touches the bag. From this position, deliver a short chudan uke with the left arm, then a short punch with the right hand, then a short punch with the left, all without stepping back and without drawing the hands back more than a few inches. Use the waist, the drop and the breath for every technique.

Do this ten times, rest, and repeat three times. Then try the same combination with a step forward between each repetition, maintaining the short range. The bag should move on every punch.
:::

## Group one: the opening steps

From the ready position, step forward into sanchin dachi or seisan dachi. The hands perform a fast chudan uke and a punch, or a combination of uke and two punches, with sharp exhalations. Then step forward and repeat, for three steps in all. In some versions, the steps are followed by a series of open-hand techniques, such as a kake uke and a spear-hand thrust, before the kata steps back.

The opening is similar in shape to Sanseru, but faster and tighter. The block and the punches should feel almost like a single action: block-punch-punch, with no break between them.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Separate techniques**

*The fault.* Your block and punches are clean, but there is a clear stop between each one, so that the group looks like a series of separate techniques.

*The cause.* You are completing each technique fully before beginning the next.

*The correction.* Let each technique begin before the previous one has fully finished, so that they overlap slightly. The block should still be arriving as the first punch begins; the first punch should still be returning as the second begins. Practise the combination against a bag until the rhythm is block-punch-punch rather than block, punch, punch.
:::

## Group two: the kick and the cross

After the opening, the kata performs a front kick, followed by a crossing step into kosa dachi with a downward strike. In a typical version, the knee lifts and the front kick snaps out at low or middle level; the kicking foot lands forward and the rear foot crosses behind it into kosa dachi, as the body drops and the hands strike down with a back-fist, a hammer-fist or a double strike.

This group combines the low kick of Saifa with the crossing step of Sepai, delivered at Seisan's faster pace. The kick disrupts the opponent; the crossing step closes in; the dropping strike finishes.

## Group three: the grab and the knee

The central group of Seisan contains a movement in which both hands reach forward, grip and pull down sharply as the knee drives upward. This is similar to the grab and knee of Sanseru, but often performed more quickly and with a stronger pulling action. In some versions the grab is made at head height and pulled down to the waist; in others the hands grip at chest height and pull to the hips.

In some lineages, this group also includes a jump or a quick change of stance after the knee strike, so that the kata moves suddenly in a new direction.

## Group four: the turns and the low attacks

The later part of Seisan turns to the sides with a series of low attacks. In a typical version, you turn into shiko dachi with a low sweeping block, or a downward knife-hand strike, and follow with a joint kick or a low punch. The group is repeated to the other side.

The low attacks of this group are a reminder that, at close range, the legs are vulnerable. Having controlled the opponent's arms with the earlier groups, Seisan attacks their base: knees, shins and groin.

## Group five: the close

The kata returns to its starting point and finishes with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

## The pace of Seisan

Seisan is the fastest of the classical kata. This creates particular problems. At speed, stances tend to shrink, structure tends to break, and techniques tend to lose their focus. The faster you go, the more these faults are magnified.

The answer, as with Sanseru, is to build speed gradually on a foundation of correct technique. Learn each group slowly, with full attention to stance, structure and timing. Then increase the speed of each group in stages, going back to a slower speed whenever the technique breaks down. Only when each group can be performed correctly at full speed should you join them together at full speed.

It also helps to practise Seisan slowly and with tension, as described for Saifa in Chapter 7. The slow, tense performance shows up every weakness in the structure and builds the strength needed to maintain it at speed.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Speed without structure**

*The fault.* Your Seisan is fast, but your stances are high and short, your shoulders rise and your techniques lack power.

*The cause.* Trying to perform the kata at full speed before the structure is strong enough to support it.

*The correction.* Perform Seisan at half speed for a month, concentrating on structure. Once a week, perform it slowly with Sanchin breathing and tension. Gradually increase the speed, one group at a time, checking your structure at each stage.
:::

## Seisan and Sanchin

It is often said that Seisan is Sanchin applied. There is some truth in this. Seisan's stances are related to sanchin dachi; its posture is the posture of Sanchin; its opening steps echo Sanchin's opening; and its short, powerful techniques depend on the structure that Sanchin builds. A student with a weak Sanchin will always have a weak Seisan.

The difference is that Seisan applies the Sanchin structure at speed and under pressure, with an imagined opponent who is close, active and dangerous. In this sense, Seisan is a test of Sanchin. If your Sanchin structure holds up in Seisan, it is genuinely strong. If it collapses, you have work to do in Sanchin.

## Applications

### The close-range combination

The opening group's application is a close-range exchange. The partner punches. The defender's chudan uke deflects the punch and stays in contact with the partner's arm, pressing it aside. The defender's first punch strikes the body as the partner's arm is held; the second punch strikes the face or body as the partner tries to recover. Because the range is so close, the punches are short, and the deflecting arm does most of the work of keeping the defender safe.

### The kick, the cross and the drop

The application of group two is a disruption, a close and a finish. The low front kick strikes the partner's groin or knee, bending them forward or stopping their advance. The crossing step brings the defender in close, often to the side of the partner. The drop into kosa dachi adds the defender's weight to a downward strike to the partner's head, neck or back.

(4) The second punch. (5) The low front kick. (6) The partner bends forward. (7) The crossing step into kosa dachi. (8) The downward strike to the partner's back.](figures/fig13_6.png)

### The grab and the knee

The application of group three is the same as in Sanseru: the head, neck or clothing is gripped and pulled down onto the rising knee. In Seisan, it is usually preceded by a technique that bends the partner forward, such as a body punch or a low kick, so that the grab and knee follow naturally.

### The low attack after control

The application of group four is a low attack against an opponent whose upper body is controlled. The defender's low sweeping block or downward knife hand may represent a strike to the partner's arm or a grip on their wrist, pulling it down. The joint kick then attacks the partner's knee while their arm is held.

::: note
**Note: Intensity in partner work**

The close-range techniques of Seisan, particularly the knees, joint kicks and downward strikes, can cause serious injury. Practise them with control, against pads where possible, and with partners whose experience matches your own.
:::

## Breathing in Seisan

Because Seisan is fast, its breathing is easily neglected. Many students hold their breath through the opening combination and gasp at the end of it, or breathe in short, shallow snatches that leave them exhausted by the middle of the kata. Neither will do.

The breath in Seisan is short and sharp on the main techniques, but it is never held. In the opening group, a useful pattern is a short inhalation as you step, and a single exhalation that carries the block and both punches, with a sharper emphasis at the moment each punch lands. The breath is pushed out in three small pulses rather than one long stream, rather like the sound of a steam engine pulling away. In the kick and the cross, the exhalation is timed to the drop into kosa dachi. In the grab and the knee, it comes as the knee meets the pull.

It is worth practising the breathing on its own. Stand in seisan dachi and perform the opening combination slowly, with exaggerated breathing, so that you can feel and hear the pulses. Then speed it up, keeping the pulses but making them smaller and quieter. The aim is breathing that supports each technique without ever leaving you short of air, which is exactly what you would need in a real close-range struggle that lasted more than a few seconds.

## The Seisan family

I mentioned at the beginning of this chapter that versions of Seisan are found in many styles. It is worth saying a little more about this, because comparing the versions can teach you a good deal about your own.

In Uechi-Ryu, Seisan is one of the core kata, performed in a stance very close to sanchin dachi with an emphasis on open-hand techniques and toe kicks. In the Shorin-Ryu lineages, Seisan is performed with a more upright posture and longer, more linear movements. In Shotokan it appears, much altered, as Hangetsu, with slow, circular stepping and deep breathing. Each version has kept something and changed something. All of them are concerned with fighting at close range from a stable, inward-pressing stance.

If you have the opportunity to watch a Seisan from another style, take it. Do not watch it to decide which version is better. Watch it to see what it emphasises that yours does not. You may find that a movement which seems puzzling in your own kata is clearer in another version, or that a technique you have always performed one way has another possible meaning. The family resemblance is a reminder that kata are not fixed objects but living traditions, shaped by the people who practise them.

## Common questions about Seisan

*Why is Seisan taught so late in some associations and so early in others?* In styles where Seisan is a foundational kata, it is taught early because it embodies the style's basic method. In Goju-Ryu, it is usually taught later because its speed and density demand a level of structure and coordination that comes only after years of Sanchin and the earlier kaishu kata. Neither approach is wrong; they reflect different teaching logics.

*Should Seisan ever be performed slowly?* Yes. Although its proper rhythm is fast, slow practice with tension is one of the best ways to build the structure the kata needs. Just do not mistake the slow version for the finished one.

*Is Seisan dachi the same as sanchin dachi?* It is closely related but slightly longer, with the front foot turned in a little less. Some lineages make the distinction clearly; others perform Seisan largely in sanchin dachi. Follow your teacher.

*What is the most important single skill for Seisan?* Short power. Without the ability to produce force from a few inches, the kata's techniques are empty at the range for which they were designed. Everything else can be built on that.

## Training Seisan

**Build the short power.** Use the short combination drill at every Seisan session.

**Build speed gradually.** Learn each group slowly, then speed it up in stages. Join the groups at full speed only when each is correct at full speed.

**Test the structure.** Perform Seisan slowly with tension once a week. If your structure fails, return to Sanchin.

**Work close.** Practise the applications at genuinely close range, closer than feels comfortable.

**Combine with Sanseru.** Seisan and Sanseru share many techniques. Practising them together will help you see the connections and the differences.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Seisan is a fast, compact kata for fighting at the closest range. Versions of it exist in many styles.
- It depends on short power: chinkuchi delivered over a few inches.
- The opening combination should flow as block-punch-punch, with overlapping techniques.
- The kick and cross combine a low kick with a crossing step and a dropping strike.
- The grab and knee pull the opponent down onto the rising knee.
- The turns attack the opponent's base with low blocks, joint kicks and low punches.
- Seisan applies the structure of Sanchin at speed and under pressure. A weak Sanchin means a weak Seisan.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise the short combination drill against a heavy bag, three sets of ten, at every Seisan session for a month.
2. Perform Seisan at half speed for a month, then increase the speed one group at a time.
3. Perform Seisan slowly with Sanchin breathing and tension once a week.
4. With a partner, practise the close-range combination of group one, keeping the deflecting arm in contact with the partner's arm.
5. Practise the kick, cross and drop against a heavy bag, ten times on each side.
6. Perform Sanchin immediately before and after Seisan, and note any differences in your structure.
:::

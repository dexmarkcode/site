# Sanchin Revisited: Shime Testing and the Repair of Common Faults

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is the root of Goju-Ryu, and shime, the practice of testing it by hand, is how its quality is checked. This chapter revisits the kata in detail, explains what a teacher is actually looking for when they test you, sets out a graded and safe method of testing, and works through the faults that most commonly appear in intermediate and senior students, with a correction for each.
:::

There is an old saying in Goju-Ryu that Sanchin is the first kata you learn and the last kata you understand. I used to think this was the sort of thing teachers said to make beginners feel humble. I now think it is simply true. I have been practising Sanchin for a long time, and I am still finding things in it. A few years ago a visiting teacher placed one hand flat on my upper back during Sanchin and said, very quietly, "There is nobody home here." He was right. The muscles under his hand were doing nothing. I had been performing Sanchin for decades with a part of my back that had simply gone to sleep, and none of my training partners, nor I, had noticed.

That is the problem Sanchin testing is designed to solve. Sanchin is performed largely with the body still and the movements slow. From the outside, a good Sanchin and an empty one can look almost identical. The difference lies inside, in whether the muscles are engaged, whether the structure is connected, whether the breath is doing its work. You cannot see these things in a mirror. Someone has to put their hands on you and find out.

## What Sanchin is for

You will remember from the first volume that Sanchin is traditionally said to unify three things: mind, body and breath. In more practical terms, it does several jobs at once.

It builds a **stable, connected structure**, so that force can travel through your body without being lost at the joints, and so that force arriving from outside, such as a strike or a push, is absorbed and grounded rather than knocking you over.

It **strengthens the muscles** that hold the structure together, especially those of the back, the shoulder girdle, the abdomen and the inner thighs, through sustained isometric tension.

It **conditions the body** to receive impact, through the testing that accompanies it.

It **trains the breath**, teaching you to breathe deeply and to coordinate breath with tension and movement.

And it **trains the mind**, through the demand for complete concentration on a slow, repetitive and physically demanding task.

All of these jobs depend on doing the kata correctly. A Sanchin performed with the wrong tension, or with the shoulders raised, or with the breath held, does not do them. It may even do harm, by training faulty structure or by raising blood pressure dangerously. This is why the quality of your Sanchin matters so much more than the quantity.

## The two forms of Sanchin

Kanryo Higaonna is generally said to have taught Sanchin with open hands, performed with a forward-and-back pattern and without turns. Chojun Miyagi revised the kata, performing it with closed fists and introducing turns, so that the student had to maintain the structure while changing direction. Most Goju-Ryu dojo today practise Miyagi's version as the standard Sanchin, and some also practise a version closer to Higaonna's, often called *Higaonna Sanchin* or *Sanchin without turns*.

The two forms share the same structure and the same principles. The descriptions in this chapter refer to Miyagi's form unless otherwise stated. Your teacher will tell you which form, or forms, your dojo practises.

## The sequence in movement groups

I described the basic sequence of Sanchin in the first volume. Here, it is set out in movement groups, with an emphasis on what each group trains.

### Group one: the opening

From musubi dachi, you bow and move to the ready position. You then step forward with the right foot into sanchin dachi and raise both arms into the double chudan uke position: forearms vertical, fists at shoulder height, elbows a fist's width from the body and pointing down. The breath is drawn in and released slowly. This position, which I shall call *sanchin kamae*, is the home position of the kata and the position in which most testing takes place.

The opening group establishes the structure. Everything that follows depends on it.

### Group two: the forward steps

From sanchin kamae, you perform a slow punch with the left hand, drawing the right hand back to the side, then return the left hand to the chudan uke position. This is followed by a step forward with the left foot, using the crescent step, into a new sanchin dachi, and the same sequence of punch and block with the other hand. The pattern is repeated for three steps in all.

Each punch and block is performed with full ibuki breathing and continuous tension. The movement of the arm is slow and smooth, and the body stays absolutely firm as it moves.

(3) Sanchin kamae, the double chudan uke. (4) The left hand draws back to the side. (5) The left punch extends slowly. (6) The punch at full extension. (7) The left hand returns to chudan uke. ### Group three: the turns

After the three forward steps, you turn one hundred and eighty degrees, maintaining sanchin dachi, and repeat the punch and block. Depending on the lineage, there may be one or two turns and further steps before the next group. The turns test your ability to keep the structure intact while the body rotates. They are also where most students' Sanchin falls apart.

### Group four: the closing techniques

After the turns, the kata moves into its closing section. In Miyagi's form, this usually contains a series of slow double open-hand thrusts, *morote nukite*, followed by a pulling action in which the hands are drawn back to the sides, and a series of slow, powerful double palm pushes. The details of this section vary between lineages more than any other part of the kata. It ends, like the classical kata, with mawashi uke, followed by the return to the starting position and the closing bow.

(5) The hands open outward. (6) The pull back to the sides. (7) The double palm push. (8) Mawashi uke at the close of the kata.](figures/fig4_2.png)

## Shime: what the teacher is looking for

*Shime* is the practice of testing Sanchin by pressing, pushing, slapping or striking the performer's body while they perform the kata. The word comes from a verb meaning to tighten or to close, and the practice tightens the performer in two senses: it checks that they are tight in the right places, and it trains them to tighten in response to pressure.

When a teacher performs shime, they are not trying to hurt you or to see how much you can take. They are gathering information. Each touch is a question. Is the shoulder down and connected? Is the back engaged? Are the thighs active? Is the stance rooted? Is the breath moving properly? The teacher's hands are listening, and what they hear tells them where your Sanchin is strong and where it is weak.

A good teacher performs shime with great sensitivity. The pressure is matched to the student's level of training and to the purpose of the test. A beginner is tested with light touches and gentle pressure. A senior student may be tested with firm slaps and strikes. In all cases, the intent is to find information, not to inflict damage.

### The checking points

There is no single universal sequence of shime, and every teacher does it slightly differently. But most testing covers the same areas, and it helps to know what each one is checking.

**The shoulders.** The teacher presses down on the tops of the shoulders, sometimes firmly. They are checking that your shoulders are down, that your trapezius muscles are not bunched up towards your ears, and that the pressure travels down through your body into the stance rather than collapsing your posture. If your shoulders are raised, the pressure will push them down and you will feel them sink. If they are correctly placed and connected, the pressure will go through them into your back and legs, and you will barely move.

**The upper back.** The teacher slaps or presses the area across the shoulder blades and along the sides of the back, below the armpits. They are checking that the large muscles of the back, particularly the latissimus dorsi, are engaged. These muscles connect the arms to the trunk. If they are not working, the arms are disconnected and the shoulders will tend to rise. This was the area my visiting teacher found asleep.

**The arms.** The teacher strikes the forearms from the outside or pushes them inward or outward. They are checking that the arms hold their position under pressure, which depends on the back and shoulder girdle being engaged, and that the elbows stay in and down.

**The abdomen.** The teacher presses or strikes the abdomen. They are checking that the abdominal muscles are firm and that the breath is controlled. A strike to the abdomen at the moment of exhalation tests whether the abdomen can absorb impact.

**The thighs.** The teacher strikes the fronts and sides of the thighs, sometimes with the hand and sometimes with a light kick. They are checking that the thigh muscles are active and that the stance is rooted.

**The stance.** The teacher pushes and pulls at the hips from different directions, or pushes on the shoulders. They are checking that the stance resists the force and that the body responds as a single unit rather than bending at the waist or the knees.

**The breath.** Throughout the test, the teacher is listening to the breath. They are checking that it is steady, controlled and continuous, and that it does not stop or become ragged under pressure.

## A graded method of testing

In many dojo, shime is performed only by the senior instructor, and students simply receive it. I think there is great value in senior students learning to test each other, under supervision, because learning to test teaches you a great deal about what you are being tested for. But testing must be done carefully, and the person testing must understand what they are doing and why.

I teach testing in four levels. Students learn to give each level only when they can receive it comfortably, and the person being tested should always be tested at a level they are ready for.

### Level one: placing

The tester simply places their hands on each checking point in turn and feels what is happening under them. There is no pressure. The tester is learning to feel the difference between an engaged muscle and a relaxed one, and the performer is learning to direct their attention to each part of their body as it is touched.

This level is suitable for all students and can be used from the earliest stages of Sanchin training.

### Level two: pressing

The tester applies steady, increasing pressure to each checking point: pressing down on the shoulders, pushing the arms, pushing the hips. The pressure is applied slowly and released slowly. The performer responds by maintaining their position and structure, using the pressure as information about where they need to engage more fully.

This level is suitable once the performer can hold a correct sanchin kamae for the length of the kata without obvious collapse.

### Level three: slapping

The tester applies open-handed slaps to the large muscle groups: the upper back, the sides, the thighs, the buttocks. The slaps should be firm enough to make a sound but not hard enough to cause pain beyond a sting. They are timed, at least at first, to the performer's exhalation, when the muscles are most fully engaged.

This level is suitable for experienced students who have been training Sanchin regularly for at least a year or two and whose structure holds well under pressing.

### Level four: striking

The tester delivers closed-hand strikes, forearm strikes or light kicks to the large muscle groups, the forearms and the abdomen. These must be controlled and must be applied only to appropriate targets. This level is suitable only for senior students with years of conditioning, and should only be given by experienced testers under supervision.

::: note
**Note: Where never to strike**

Sanchin testing should never involve strikes to the head, the neck, the throat, the spine, the kidneys, the groin or the joints. The tester should never strike with full power, and should never strike a performer who is not breathing out or whose structure is visibly collapsing. Some dojo traditionally test the groin by a light upward tap of the foot, to check that the knees are protecting it. I do not recommend this practice; the point it makes can be made more safely by pressing inward on the knees from the outside.
:::

## The faults that testing reveals

Over many years of testing, I have found that the same faults appear again and again. Here they are, roughly in order of how often I meet them, with their causes and corrections.

### Rising shoulders

This is by far the commonest fault in Sanchin, and indeed in Goju-Ryu as a whole. As the performer tenses, the shoulders creep upward towards the ears. The neck shortens, the trapezius muscles bunch, and the arms become disconnected from the back.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Rising shoulders**

*The fault.* Your shoulders rise as you tense. Your teacher presses them down repeatedly, but they creep back up within a few breaths.

*The cause.* You are tensing from the top of the body downward, using the muscles nearest your neck because they are the easiest to feel and control. The large back muscles that should hold your shoulders down are not engaged.

*The correction.* Stop trying to press the shoulders down directly. Instead, focus on the muscles along the sides of your back, just below your armpits. Imagine you are trying to squeeze a book between each upper arm and your side, and at the same time draw your shoulder blades down towards your back pockets. When these muscles engage, your shoulders will settle automatically. Practise this in sanchin kamae, standing still, for ten breaths at a time, with a partner placing their hands lightly on your shoulders to tell you when they rise.
:::

### The puffed chest

Many students, trying to look strong, push their chests out and arch their upper backs. This tilts the ribcage upward, disconnects the abdomen from the chest and makes deep breathing much harder.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The puffed chest**

*The fault.* Your chest is pushed forward and your upper back is arched. You feel strong and upright, but your teacher tells you that your back is "empty" or that your breath is "high".

*The cause.* A mistaken idea of good posture, often carried over from military-style "chest out, shoulders back" instructions.

*The correction.* Let your chest relax and sink slightly, so that your lower ribs draw down towards your hips. Feel your upper back widen, as though your shoulder blades were spreading apart. This is sometimes described as "hollowing the chest and rounding the back", though the rounding should be very slight. Your posture will feel less impressive and much more solid. Check it with level-two pressure on the shoulders; it should now resist much better.
:::

### Holding the breath

Under pressure, especially during testing, many students stop breathing. They hold their breath at the end of an inhalation or partway through an exhalation, their faces redden, and their tension becomes rigid and brittle.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Holding the breath**

*The fault.* Your face goes red during Sanchin. You feel lightheaded afterwards. Your teacher tells you that you are holding your breath, or you notice that your breath stops when you are struck.

*The cause.* Fear of the impact, or the mistaken belief that holding the breath makes the body harder.

*The correction.* Practise Sanchin with a slightly lower level of tension, so that you can concentrate on keeping the breath moving. Count your exhalations: each should last a steady four to six seconds, without stopping. Ask your tester to strike only during your exhalation, and to stop at once if your breath stops. Over time, build the tension back up while keeping the breath continuous. Never sacrifice the breath for the tension.
:::

### Locked knees

Some students straighten their knees during Sanchin, particularly as they tense. A locked knee cannot absorb force and transmits shocks directly to the hip and lower back.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Locked knees**

*The fault.* Your knees are straight, or nearly so, when you are in sanchin dachi, especially during the slow punches.

*The cause.* Tensing the thigh muscles so strongly that they straighten the knees.

*The correction.* Concentrate on tensing the inner thighs, pressing the knees slightly inward, rather than tensing the fronts of the thighs. Keep the feeling of sitting slightly into the stance. A partner can check by pressing lightly on the back of your knee; it should feel bent and springy, not locked.
:::

### Flaring elbows

In sanchin kamae and during the punches and blocks, the elbows drift outward away from the body. This weakens the arm structure and disconnects it from the back.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Flaring elbows**

*The fault.* In sanchin kamae your elbows are more than a fist's width from your body, and they point outward rather than down.

*The cause.* Inactive back muscles, or an attempt to make the chudan uke look larger.

*The correction.* Engage the latissimus dorsi muscles, drawing the elbows in and down. Place a folded towel between each elbow and your side and hold it there through ten breaths of sanchin kamae. Then remove the towel and try to keep the same feeling.
:::

### Bent wrists

At the end of the punch, or in the chudan uke position, the wrist bends slightly, usually outward or downward. This breaks the line of force and, in a real strike, would injure the wrist.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Bent wrists**

*The fault.* Your wrist is not straight at the end of a punch or in the blocking position.

*The cause.* Weakness in the forearm muscles, or inattention to the alignment of the fist.

*The correction.* Check the wrist in a mirror at every punch. Make sure the line from your forearm runs straight through the first two knuckles. Strengthen the forearms with the grip exercises described in Chapter 6, particularly the nigiri game and the chi ishi.
:::

### Tension without connection

The last fault is the hardest to see and the hardest to correct. The student is tense throughout, their shoulders are down, their breath is controlled and their structure looks correct, but the tension is isolated. Each part of the body is tense on its own, without connection to the others. When tested, each checking point feels hard, but a push on the shoulder still moves the whole body, because the tension does not link the shoulder to the stance.

This fault can only be corrected through extensive testing and through a gradual shift in attention from individual muscles to the body as a whole. Instead of thinking "tense the shoulders, tense the back, tense the thighs", the performer must learn to think of the whole body as a single structure, from the soles of the feet to the fists, and to tense it as a whole. When this happens, a push on the shoulder is felt in the feet, and the body responds as one.

## Turning in Sanchin

I mentioned that the turns in Sanchin are where most students' structure collapses. This is worth looking at more closely.

The turn in Sanchin is performed by moving the rear foot across behind the front foot, pivoting on both feet and settling into sanchin dachi facing the opposite direction. During the turn, the arms remain in sanchin kamae, the breath continues, and the tension is maintained throughout the body. The aim is to arrive in the new direction with the structure completely intact, as though nothing had happened.

What actually happens, in most students, is that the tension drops during the turn. The shoulders rise, the arms flare, the stance rises, and the student arrives in the new direction having to rebuild their structure from scratch. A test immediately after the turn, a push on the shoulders or a slap on the back, reveals this at once.

![**Figure 4.6** The turn in Sanchin. (1) Sanchin kamae before the turn. (2) The head turns to look behind. (3) The rear foot moves across. (4) The pivot on the balls of both feet. (5) The new sanchin dachi, arms unchanged. (6) Fault: the shoulders rise and the arms flare during the turn. (7) Fault: the stance rises during the pivot. (8) A test immediately after the turn, a downward press on the shoulders.](figures/fig4_6.png)

::: drill
**Drill: The tested turn**

Stand in sanchin kamae. Your partner stands behind you and places their hands lightly on your shoulders. Perform the turn slowly. Your partner keeps their hands on your shoulders throughout, moving with you, and tells you whether your shoulders rise at any point. After the turn, they press down firmly on your shoulders for a slow count of three.

Repeat ten times, turning alternately to the left and the right. Then change roles. When the turn is consistently clean, increase the speed of the turn while keeping the partner's hands in place.
:::

## Testing yourself

Not everyone has access to a skilled tester every time they train. There are several ways to test yourself, none of which replaces a partner but all of which are better than nothing.

**The mirror.** Perform Sanchin in front of a mirror, side-on, and watch your shoulders, chest and stance. A mirror will not show you whether your muscles are engaged, but it will show you whether your shoulders are rising and your stance is shrinking.

**The wall.** Stand in sanchin dachi with your back to a wall, heels a few inches from it, and your upper back just touching it. Perform the slow punches and blocks. If your shoulders rise or your back arches, you will feel it against the wall.

**The doorframe.** Stand in a doorframe in sanchin dachi and press the outsides of your forearms against the frame in sanchin kamae. Push outward steadily, breathing out, for a slow count of five. Feel which muscles are working. If it is mainly your shoulders, you need to engage your back more.

**Self-testing by touch.** In sanchin kamae, take one hand out of position and place it on your own side, below the armpit, or on your abdomen or thigh. Feel whether the muscle under your hand is engaged. Return the hand and repeat on the other side.

**Recording.** Record yourself performing Sanchin, side-on, and watch it back. Pay attention to your shoulders, your stance height and your breathing. Recording is particularly good at showing you the moments, such as the turns, where your structure collapses.

## How often to practise Sanchin

Sanchin is demanding, and the quality of each performance matters much more than the number of performances. For most intermediate students, I suggest practising Sanchin three to five times at each training session, with at least one of those performances tested, at whatever level is appropriate. More than this is rarely useful and may lead to fatigue, which leads to poor structure, which defeats the purpose of the exercise.

On days when you train alone, practise Sanchin two or three times with full concentration, using one of the self-testing methods above. Then practise sanchin kamae on its own, standing still, for a few minutes, focusing on one aspect of your structure at a time.

It is also worth practising Sanchin at reduced intensity from time to time, perhaps once a week. This means performing the kata with correct structure and breathing but with only moderate tension. Low-intensity Sanchin allows you to focus on the breath and the connection of the body without the distraction of maximal effort, and it helps to prevent the gradual creep of excessive tension that affects so many Goju-Ryu students.

## Sanchin and fighting

It is sometimes said that Sanchin has nothing to do with fighting, that it is purely a training exercise. This is not quite right. The structure that Sanchin develops is exactly the structure you need at close range, where most real fights take place. The ability to absorb a strike without being knocked off balance, to deliver force from a short distance, to remain rooted while being pushed and pulled, and to keep breathing under pressure: these are all fighting skills, and Sanchin develops them more directly than anything else in the system.

What Sanchin does not do is teach you how to apply those skills. That is the job of the kaishu kata and of partner training. But without Sanchin, the kaishu kata are built on sand. The structure they need is not there. This is why every serious Goju-Ryu teacher insists that Sanchin is practised at every session, by every student, at every level, for as long as they train.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sanchin builds structure, strength, conditioning, breath control and concentration. Its value depends entirely on the quality of its performance.
- Shime, the testing of Sanchin by hand, is a way of gathering information about the performer's structure. Each touch is a question.
- The main checking points are the shoulders, upper back, arms, abdomen, thighs, stance and breath.
- Testing should be graded: placing, pressing, slapping and striking. The level must match the performer's experience.
- Never strike the head, neck, throat, spine, kidneys, groin or joints.
- The commonest faults are rising shoulders, a puffed chest, holding the breath, locked knees, flaring elbows, bent wrists and tension without connection. Each has a specific correction.
- The turns in Sanchin are where structure most often collapses. Practise them with a partner's hands on your shoulders.
- Practise Sanchin a few times with full quality rather than many times with poor quality.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. With a partner, practise level-one testing on each other, placing your hands on each checking point and describing what you feel. Swap roles after each performance.
2. Identify your own two commonest faults from this chapter, with your teacher's help if possible. Practise the correction for each for ten minutes at every session for a month.
3. Perform the tested turn drill ten times in each direction at each session for two weeks.
4. Practise sanchin kamae standing still for five minutes, focusing on the muscles below the armpits, three times a week.
5. Record yourself performing Sanchin side-on. Watch the recording and note the moments at which your shoulders rise or your stance shrinks.
6. Perform Sanchin once a week at reduced intensity, concentrating on continuous breath and whole-body connection rather than maximal tension.
:::

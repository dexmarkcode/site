# The Body Mechanics of Goju-Ryu

::: chapter-intro
Goju-Ryu does not generate power the way most modern karate does. This chapter sets out the mechanics that make the style work: the hard and the soft, the grounded structure, the use of the waist, the short explosive power the Okinawans call chinkuchi, the heavy, adhesive quality called muchimi, and the breathing that ties them together.
:::

Some years ago a visitor came to train with us for a week. He was a strong, talented karateka from a style that emphasises long, fast techniques and dynamic footwork, and he was very good at what he did. On his second evening I paired him with one of our quieter senior students for a simple pushing drill. Each of them stood in sanchin dachi and tried to move the other off balance using only the arms and body, without stepping.

He lost every round. Not because he was weaker. He was taller, heavier and, by any measure you could take in a gym, considerably stronger. He lost because every time he pushed, his force went somewhere other than where he intended. Some of it went up into his own shoulders. Some of it went back into his own heels. Some of it simply disappeared into a body that was not connected to itself. Our senior student, meanwhile, was pushing with her whole frame, from the floor to her hands, and almost nothing was being wasted.

At the end of the evening he asked, very reasonably, what she was doing that he was not. This chapter is my attempt at a proper answer.

## Go and ju: the hard and the soft

The name of the style comes from a line in an old Chinese text on martial arts, the *Bubishi*, which says that the method of breathing involves both hardness and softness. *Go* means hard and *ju* means soft, and Goju-Ryu, the "hard-soft school", was the name Chojun Miyagi gave his art when he was pressed to register it formally.

It is easy to read this as a simple division: some techniques are hard and some are soft, some kata are tense and some are relaxed. That reading is not wrong, but it is shallow. The deeper meaning is that hardness and softness are present in every movement, in proportions that change from moment to moment, and that the skill of the art lies in managing that change.

Consider a straight punch. It begins soft. The arm is relaxed, the shoulder is loose, and the fist is closed but not clenched. This softness allows the punch to start quickly and travel smoothly. As the punch approaches its target, the body begins to firm up, from the ground through the legs, hips, back and shoulder, and at the moment of impact the whole structure becomes hard for an instant. Then it softens again, to allow the arm to return or to flow into the next technique. A punch that is hard all the way through is slow and telegraphed. A punch that is soft all the way through has no impact. A good punch is both, at different moments.

![**Figure 2.1** Hard and soft in a single punch, seen from the side. (1–3) The punch begins relaxed, with the shoulder loose and the fist lightly closed. (4–5) As the fist approaches the target, the stance, hips and back begin to firm. (6) At the moment of focus, the whole body is hard. (7–8) The body softens immediately and the arm returns, ready for the next technique.](figures/fig2_1.png)

The same pattern applies to blocks, stances and even to the way you move between techniques. And it applies, in a different way, to the relationship between you and an opponent. When an opponent pushes hard, you can often yield softly and redirect their force. When they are soft and uncommitted, you can press hard and drive through them. The interplay between go and ju is not only inside your own body. It is in the exchange.

### The tension problem

Goju-Ryu has a reputation for tension. Sanchin is performed with strong, deliberate tension throughout the body, and many students come to believe that more tension always means better Goju. This belief causes more problems at the intermediate level than any other single misunderstanding.

Tension is a tool. It is used in Sanchin and in the slow parts of the classical kata to build strength, to develop awareness of the body's structure and to condition the body to withstand impact. It is also used, very briefly, at the moment of focus in a technique, to make the body a single solid mass. But tension held for too long, or applied in the wrong places, slows you down, tires you out, raises your blood pressure and, perhaps worst of all, blinds you. A tense body cannot feel what an opponent is doing. It cannot respond to a change of pressure, a shift of weight or an opening that appears for half a second and is gone.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Tense all the time**

*The fault.* Your kata look strong but feel exhausting. You tire quickly in kumite and kakie. Partners tell you that you are "stiff" or that they can see your techniques coming.

*The cause.* You have learned to equate tension with power and are holding tension throughout your movements rather than applying it at the moment of focus.

*The correction.* Practise every technique in your basic repertoire in three ways: completely relaxed, completely tense, and relaxed until the last moment then tense for an instant. Notice how much faster and more powerful the third version is. Then perform a kata you know well with the instruction to yourself that only the finishing moment of each technique is hard. Ask a partner to watch and tell you where you are holding tension between techniques.
:::

## The grounded structure

Power in Goju-Ryu begins at the floor. This is true of most martial arts, but Goju places unusual emphasis on it, and the stances of the style are designed above all to connect the body to the ground.

Think of the body as a chain of links running from the soles of the feet to the striking surface of the hand. A force generated by the legs pushing against the floor must travel up through the ankles, knees, hips, spine, shoulder girdle, arm and wrist before it reaches the target. At each link, some of it can be lost. A knee that collapses inwards absorbs force. A pelvis that tips backwards breaks the connection between the legs and the spine. A shoulder that rises disconnects the arm from the back. A wrist that bends on impact wastes everything that came before it.

The aim of Goju-Ryu structure is to make each link as firm and as well aligned as possible, so that the force travels through the chain with the least loss. This is what you were really doing when you stood in sanchin dachi in your first year of training and your teacher pressed down on your shoulders, pushed at your hips and tapped your thighs. They were checking the links.

### The three links that fail most often

In my experience, the links that most often fail in intermediate students are the knees, the pelvis and the shoulders.

The **knees** fail when they are allowed to collapse inwards or splay outwards beyond the line of the feet. In sanchin dachi the knees are pressed slightly inwards and the feet grip the floor, so that the legs form a stable arch. In shiko dachi the knees are pressed outwards over the toes. In both cases the knee must stay in line with the foot. When it does not, force leaks sideways and the joint itself is placed under strain.

The **pelvis** fails when it is tipped either too far forward, so that the lower back arches and the buttocks stick out, or too far back, so that the lower back rounds. The first is common in tall students and in those who have trained in styles with long, deep forward stances. The second is common in students who have been told repeatedly to "tuck the hips under" and have overdone it. The correct position lies between the two and is discussed below under the heading of gamaku.

The **shoulders** fail when they rise. This is the most common fault in all of Goju-Ryu and is discussed at length in Chapter 4. A raised shoulder disconnects the arm from the large muscles of the back, so that the arm is driven only by the muscles of the shoulder and chest. These are not weak muscles, but they are small compared to the back, and they cannot use the force generated by the legs.

::: drill
**Drill: The wall push**

Stand facing a solid wall, about an arm's length away, in sanchin dachi. Place both palms flat on the wall at shoulder height. Push steadily into the wall for a slow count of five, breathing out, then release.

As you push, check each link in turn. Are your feet gripping the floor? Are your knees pressing slightly inward, and are they in line with your feet? Is your pelvis level, neither arched nor tucked? Are your shoulders down? Is your back wide? Are your elbows pointing down rather than out? Are your wrists straight?

Now deliberately break one link, for example by raising your shoulders, and push again. Notice how the force changes. Restore the link and push again. Work through each link this way. Repeat the drill daily for two weeks. It takes five minutes and teaches more about structure than an hour of kata.
:::

## Gamaku: the engine in the waist

*Gamaku* is an Okinawan word that refers to the area of the body around the waist and the sides of the lower torso, roughly the region between the bottom of the ribs and the top of the hip bones. In Okinawan karate it also refers to the skilled use of that area: the engagement and control of the muscles that connect the legs to the upper body and that allow the hips and torso to rotate, drop and lock with precision.

Gamaku is not the same as "hip rotation" as it is usually taught in modern karate. In many styles, students are taught to generate power by turning the hips sharply in the direction of the technique, so that the hip leads and the arm follows like the end of a whip. This produces a long, fast technique with considerable power, and it is a perfectly good method. But it is not the main method of Goju-Ryu.

In Goju, the waist does rotate, but the rotation is shorter and is accompanied by a sinking of the body and a firming of the muscles around the waist and lower back. Instead of the hip swinging through a wide arc, it turns a short distance and then locks, so that the energy of the legs is transmitted through a solid trunk rather than flung outwards. The effect is like the difference between a whip and a hammer. Both deliver force, but a hammer is heavier, shorter and harder to stop.

(2) Fault: the pelvis tucked under and the lower back rounded. (3) Correct: the pelvis neutral, the lower back long and the waist engaged. (4–6) The same three positions with a straight punch, showing how the arched and tucked positions break the line from the back foot to the fist.](figures/fig2_3.png)

### Finding gamaku

Gamaku is difficult to describe and easier to find by feel. Here is one way.

Stand in sanchin dachi with your hands on your hips, thumbs to the rear, fingers resting on the sides of your waist just above the hip bones. Breathe out slowly and fully, as you would in Sanchin, and as you do so, try to feel the muscles under your fingers firm up and draw slightly downwards and inwards, as though your waist were narrowing and your ribs were drawing closer to your hips. Do not tip your pelvis or round your back. The movement is small and almost entirely internal.

Now, keeping that feeling, turn your hips a few degrees to the left and then a few degrees to the right. Notice that the turn comes from the waist rather than the legs, and that your upper body turns with your hips as a single unit. This is the beginning of gamaku.

The practical test of gamaku is whether it lets you transmit force without losing it. Stand in sanchin dachi with your fist touching a partner's palm or a heavy bag. Without drawing your arm back, try to deliver a push into the target using only the turning and locking of your waist. If you are using gamaku well, the push will be surprisingly strong, and your partner will feel it come from your whole body rather than your arm. If not, the push will feel weak and disconnected, and you will notice yourself trying to use your shoulder.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Turning with the shoulders**

*The fault.* When you rotate to deliver a technique, your shoulders turn first and your hips follow, or your hips do not turn at all.

*The cause.* Habit, and the belief that power comes from the arms and upper body.

*The correction.* Practise the gamaku turn with a short stick or belt held across the front of your hips. Watch the stick in a mirror as you turn from side to side. The stick should move first and most. Then hold the stick across your shoulders and repeat. The shoulders should follow the hips, not lead them. Return to the punch and repeat the hip-first feeling slowly, twenty times on each side.
:::

## Chinkuchi: short, explosive power

*Chinkuchi* is one of the most frequently discussed and least clearly defined terms in Okinawan karate. It is an Okinawan word without a precise Japanese or English equivalent, and different teachers explain it in different ways. Broadly, it refers to the ability to concentrate the whole body's force into a single instant, through the precise coordination of muscle tension, skeletal alignment, breathing and the dropping of the body's weight. A technique delivered with chinkuchi seems to arrive with much more force than its size and speed would suggest.

The old Okinawan masters were famous for this kind of power. Stories are told of teachers who could knock a man down with a punch that travelled only a few inches, or who could break a board held against their own chest. Some of these stories have grown in the telling. But the underlying skill is real, and it is the heart of Goju-Ryu striking.

I find it useful to think of chinkuchi as having three components that must arrive together.

The first is **alignment**. At the moment of impact, the skeleton must be arranged so that the force of the impact passes straight back through the bones into the ground. If any joint is out of line, the force will be absorbed there instead of going into the target. This is why Goju pays such attention to the angle of the wrist, the position of the elbow and the line of the back.

The second is **compression**. At the moment of impact, the muscles throughout the body contract together, briefly and sharply, so that the body becomes a single rigid mass. This contraction must be very short. If it lasts too long, it becomes ordinary tension and the advantages of softness are lost. The best description I have heard is that the body should "snap shut", like a door slammed by the wind, and then open again.

The third is **dropping**. At the moment of impact, the body's centre of gravity sinks slightly, as though you were dropping your weight through your stance into the floor. This drop adds the mass of the whole body to the technique and also grounds it, so that the reaction from the target goes into the floor rather than pushing you backwards.

![**Figure 2.4** The drop in chinkuchi. (1–4) A downward hammer strike in shiko dachi, showing the body settling a short distance as the fist arrives; the broken line marks the height of the hips before the drop. (5–8) The same principle in a short punch from sanchin dachi: the knees press, the hips settle and the breath is expelled at the instant of focus.](figures/fig2_4.png)

When these three arrive at the same moment, together with a short, sharp expulsion of breath, the result is chinkuchi. When they arrive at different moments, the effect is greatly reduced. A common fault is for the drop to come slightly after the impact, so that the body sinks into a technique that has already landed. Another is for the compression to begin too early, so that the technique slows down before it arrives.

::: drill
**Drill: One-inch power**

Stand in sanchin dachi facing a heavy bag, with your fist resting against it and your elbow bent slightly. Without drawing your arm back at all, deliver a punch into the bag using only alignment, compression and drop. Breathe out sharply at the moment of the strike. Relax immediately afterwards.

At first the bag will barely move. Persist. Try five strikes with the right hand, then five with the left, then rest. Do three rounds. Over several weeks, as you learn to bring the three components together, you will find the bag moving more and more.

When you can move the bag noticeably from contact, increase the distance to two inches, then four, then six. Do not go beyond six inches. The point of the drill is to learn to produce power without relying on a long run-up.
:::

### Chinkuchi in kata

In the classical kata, chinkuchi appears most clearly in the short, explosive techniques that punctuate the slower movements. The downward hammer strikes in Saifa and Sepai, the short punches in Seisan, the elbow strikes in Shisochin and the palm strikes in Kururunfa are all opportunities to practise it. When you perform these techniques, think less about how fast your hand is moving and more about how completely your body arrives with it.

## Muchimi: the heavy, sticky hand

If chinkuchi is the hard side of Goju-Ryu power, *muchimi* is the soft side. The word refers to a quality of the hands and arms that is heavy, adhesive and flexible, like a rice cake or a lump of dough, which is what the Okinawan word originally described. A hand with muchimi does not bounce off an opponent's arm when it blocks. It sticks to it, presses into it and follows it, controlling its movement and feeling its intention.

Muchimi is essential to the close-range fighting that the classical kata teach. Many of the movements in the kaishu kata begin with contact: a block that becomes a grip, a parry that becomes a pull, an arm that presses against an opponent's arm to trap or redirect it. If your contact is light, hard or brief, you cannot control what happens next. If it is heavy, soft and continuous, you can.

![**Figure 2.5** Muchimi in contact with a partner. (1–2) The partner's punch is met with a heavy, adhesive kake uke. (3–4) The blocking hand stays in contact, following and pressing the partner's arm downward. (5–6) The contact is maintained as the defender turns the partner's arm and opens a line for a strike. (7–8) The same exchange with a light, bouncing block for comparison: contact is lost and the partner's second hand arrives unchecked.](figures/fig2_5.png)

Muchimi cannot be developed by solo training alone. It requires a partner, and the main exercise for developing it in Goju-Ryu is *kakie*, the pushing-hands drill discussed in Chapters 5 and 17. But you can lay the groundwork in solo practice by paying attention to the quality of your blocks in kata. A block with muchimi does not stop dead at its finishing position. It arrives with weight and stays there, as though it were pressing against something. Tensho, the second of the heishu kata, is largely devoted to developing this quality.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Bouncing blocks**

*The fault.* Your blocks are fast and sharp, but they make only brief contact. In partner drills, your opponent's arm slides off your block and continues towards you, or their other hand comes through the gap.

*The cause.* You have trained your blocks as strikes against the opponent's arm, aiming to knock it away, rather than as tools for controlling it.

*The correction.* Practise blocking drills with a partner at slow speed, with the instruction that once your arm has made contact, it must stay in contact for a full second, pressing gently into your partner's arm. Gradually increase the speed while keeping the contact. Then practise Tensho with particular attention to the feeling of weight and pressure in the hands.
:::

## The breath

Breathing is the thread that ties all of these qualities together. Goju-Ryu places more emphasis on breathing than almost any other karate style, and the two heishu kata are largely exercises in breath control.

There are two main methods of breathing in Goju-Ryu. They go by various names in different dojo, but the most common terms are *ibuki* and *nogare*.

**Ibuki** is the strong, audible breathing of Sanchin. The breath is drawn in through the nose, deep into the lower abdomen, and then expelled through the mouth with a strong contraction of the abdominal and torso muscles, producing a characteristic hissing or rasping sound. Ibuki is used to build strength, to condition the body and to coordinate tension with breath. It is not normally used in fighting, because it is slow, it is loud and it tells the opponent exactly what you are doing.

**Nogare** is softer and quieter. The breath is still drawn deeply into the lower abdomen and the exhalation is still controlled, but there is little or no sound and less overt tension. Nogare is the breath of Tensho and of much of the classical kata. It is the breath you use when you need to stay relaxed and responsive. In fighting, a version of it allows you to keep breathing steadily without broadcasting your intentions.

![**Figure 2.6** The two breaths. (1–2) Ibuki: the inhalation through the nose with the abdomen expanding, and the forceful exhalation with the abdomen contracting and the whole body firm. (3–4) Nogare: the same cycle with less tension, the shoulders loose and the breath almost silent. The arrows show the movement of the abdominal wall.](figures/fig2_6.png)

### Breath and technique

In the classical kata, the breath is coordinated with the techniques according to a few broad principles. As a rule, you breathe out as you deliver a technique and breathe in as you prepare for the next one. In slow, tense movements, the exhalation lasts as long as the movement. In fast techniques, the exhalation is short and sharp, timed to the moment of focus. In flowing sequences of several techniques, you may perform two or three techniques on a single breath, with a short, sharp emphasis at each point of focus.

The exact breathing pattern of each kata is usually taught by the teacher and varies somewhat between lineages. What matters more than the precise pattern is that the breath is always under control. You should never find yourself holding your breath unintentionally, gasping between movements or running out of breath halfway through a technique. If you do, it is a sign that your breathing has not yet become part of the kata and is still something you are adding on top.

::: note
**Note: Breath-holding and blood pressure**

The strong abdominal contraction used in Sanchin and in the slow parts of the kata raises blood pressure significantly, particularly if the breath is held rather than released steadily. This is one reason why Sanchin should be approached gradually, and why anyone with high blood pressure, a heart condition or a history of stroke, hernia or eye problems such as glaucoma should consult a doctor before training it with full intensity. Never hold your breath during tense movements. The exhalation should be continuous, even when it is slow.
:::

## The three battles

The name *Sanchin* is usually translated as "three battles" or "three conflicts", and the three are often said to be the battles to unify mind, body and breath. Whether or not this was the original meaning of the name, it is a useful way of thinking about what all the qualities in this chapter are for.

The **body** must be aligned, grounded and connected, able to move from softness to hardness and back again in an instant. The **breath** must be deep, controlled and coordinated with movement. The **mind** must be calm, alert and directed, neither so tense that it freezes nor so relaxed that it drifts. When the three come together, the result is a quality of movement that is unmistakable once you have seen it. The performer seems heavier, calmer and more dangerous than their size would suggest.

None of these qualities can be developed quickly. They are built gradually, through years of Sanchin and Tensho, through the slow practice of the classical kata, through kakie and partner work, and through countless small corrections from a teacher who knows what to look for. But they can all be understood now, and understanding them will help you to recognise them when they begin to appear in your own training.

## Putting it together: the anatomy of a single technique

To finish this chapter, let us take a single technique and trace all of these principles through it. The technique is the simplest one in karate: a straight punch delivered from sanchin dachi.

You begin in a correct sanchin dachi. Your feet grip the floor. Your knees press slightly inwards. Your pelvis is neutral and your waist is engaged. Your back is wide and your shoulders are down. Your punching hand is drawn back at the side, relaxed but ready. You breathe in, quietly and deeply.

The punch begins. Your arm extends smoothly from the shoulder, which stays down and connected to the back. Your elbow travels close to the body, brushing the side. The arm is soft; this is the ju phase. As the fist travels, your waist begins to turn a short distance in the direction of the punch, carrying the shoulder forward with it.

At the moment of impact, everything arrives together. The fist turns over and the wrist locks straight. The waist stops turning and locks. The muscles throughout the body contract sharply. The body drops a fraction, as though settling into the floor. The breath is expelled in a short, sharp burst. This is the go phase, the moment of chinkuchi. The force of the punch travels from the floor through the feet, knees, hips, back, shoulder, arm and fist and into the target, and the reaction from the target travels back down the same path into the floor.

Immediately afterwards, the body softens. The arm relaxes. The breath begins to return. You are ready for whatever comes next.

That is one punch. Every technique in every kata in this book can be analysed in the same way, and every one of them, performed well, contains the same elements. The difference between an ordinary punch and a Goju-Ryu punch is not the shape. It is everything happening inside the shape.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Go and ju, hardness and softness, are present in every movement in changing proportions. A good technique is soft until the moment of focus, hard for an instant, then soft again.
- Excessive tension is the most common misunderstanding of Goju-Ryu at the intermediate level. Tension is a tool, not a goal.
- Power begins at the floor and travels through a chain of links. The links that most often fail are the knees, the pelvis and the shoulders.
- Gamaku is the skilled use of the muscles around the waist. In Goju-Ryu the waist turns a short distance and locks rather than swinging through a wide arc.
- Chinkuchi is short, explosive power produced when alignment, compression and dropping arrive together with the breath.
- Muchimi is the heavy, adhesive quality of the hands that allows you to control an opponent through contact. It is developed through Tensho and kakie.
- Ibuki is the strong, audible breath of Sanchin. Nogare is the softer, quieter breath of Tensho and much of the classical kata. Never hold your breath under tension.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Perform the wall push drill daily for two weeks, checking each link of the chain in turn.
2. Practise every basic technique in three versions: completely relaxed, completely tense, and relaxed with a moment of tension at focus. Record which feels fastest and most powerful.
3. Spend five minutes each day finding gamaku with your hands on your waist, then practise the short gamaku turn with a stick across your hips in front of a mirror.
4. Work on the one-inch power drill on a heavy bag three times a week for a month. Note any change in how far the bag moves.
5. With a partner, practise slow blocking drills in which your blocking arm must stay in contact with theirs for one full second after each block.
6. Perform one kata you know well in slow motion with nogare breathing, then again at speed. Note where your breath becomes uncontrolled and work on those movements in isolation.
:::

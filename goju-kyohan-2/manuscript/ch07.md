# Saifa: Tearing Free and Striking Back

::: chapter-intro
Saifa is usually the first classical kata a Goju-Ryu student learns, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. It is short, compact and explosive, and almost every movement in it answers the same question: what do you do when someone has hold of you? This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the mechanics that make its techniques work, deals with the faults that most often weaken it, and shows its principal applications.
:::

A student of mine once told me that she had been grabbed by the wrist on a crowded train platform, late at night, by a man who had been drinking. She was a green belt at the time and had been learning Saifa for about three months. She said she did not think about it at all. Her free hand covered the hand that was being held, her body turned, her elbow came up and round, and she was free and two steps away before she quite understood what had happened. The man, more surprised than hurt, swore at her and went the other way.

I tell this story not because it is dramatic, which it is not, but because it shows what Saifa is for. It is not a kata about winning a duel. It is a kata about not being held. Its first movement, repeated three times so that you cannot miss it, is a release from a grip, and the rest of the kata follows from there: once you are free, strike; once you have struck, control; once you have controlled, finish. It is a complete answer to one of the commonest forms of real-world aggression, and it is given to students early precisely because of that.

## The character of Saifa

The name Saifa is usually written with characters meaning "smash" or "break" and "tear", and interpreted as "smash and tear" or "tear and destroy". Whatever its original meaning, the name fits the kata. Its movements are short and circular. Its power comes from the turning and dropping of the whole body rather than from long, extended techniques. And many of its techniques work by tearing: pulling a gripped limb one way while the body moves another, so that the grip is broken or the opponent's structure is ripped apart.

Saifa is a short kata, and it takes well under a minute to perform. Its floor pattern begins with three movements to the diagonals, then works forward and back along the main line, with turns to the sides. It finishes, like all the classical kata, with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi, on or close to the starting point.

It contains most of the stances you will need for the classical kata: musubi dachi, shiko dachi, zenkutsu dachi or seisan dachi, neko ashi dachi and, in some lineages, kosa dachi. It contains open-hand and closed-hand techniques, a front kick, back-fist and hammer-fist strikes, elbow strikes and the characteristic circular releases. For many students, it is the kata in which they first begin to feel the difference between Goju-Ryu and the more linear karate of their early training.

## Group one: the three tearing releases

The kata opens with its signature movement, performed three times: to the right diagonal, to the left diagonal and to the right diagonal again.

From the ready position, step forward with the right foot to the front-right diagonal and draw the left foot up beside it, so that you arrive with the feet close together. As you step, your right hand closes into a fist and your left hand covers it, and both hands are drawn in a tight curve to the left side of the body, at about the level of the hip. This is the "pull" of the release.

Then, without pausing, step back with the left foot and settle into a stance facing the same diagonal, usually shiko dachi in the Okinawan lineages, as the hands rotate together in a short circle and drive forward and outward, the right elbow and forearm leading. The left palm stays on the right fist, adding the weight of the left side to the movement. The breath is expelled at the moment the movement finishes. This is the "tear".

The movement is then repeated to the left diagonal, with the hands reversed, and once more to the right.

(3) The feet come together; the left palm covers the right fist. (4) Both hands drawn to the left hip. (5) The left foot steps back; the hands begin to rotate. (6) The elbow and forearm drive forward in the circular tear. (7) The finishing position in shiko dachi. (8) The transition towards the left diagonal.](figures/fig7_2.png)

### What makes the release work

It is tempting to think of this movement as a pair of arm actions: pull to the hip, then strike with the elbow. If you perform it that way, it will look roughly correct, and it will not work.

The release works because the body moves the arm, not the other way round. When someone grabs your wrist, their grip is strongest against a direct pull and weakest at the gap between their thumb and fingers. The covering hand locks your own fist in place so that it cannot be twisted. The step and the turn of the body then carry the whole arm in a circle that rotates the wrist against the weak point of the grip, while the drop into the stance adds the weight of the body. No amount of arm strength can do what this combination does.

To feel this, ask a partner to grip your right wrist firmly with their right hand. First try to pull free using only the arm. Then perform the movement as the kata teaches it: cover the fist, step, turn the whole body and drop. The difference is usually startling.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The arm-only release**

*The fault.* Your hands make the right shapes, but your body barely turns, and the movement looks like an arm swing.

*The cause.* You are thinking of the technique as an arm action and the stance as something that happens underneath it.

*The correction.* Practise the movement with your hands held still against your body, in the covered-fist position, and make the whole movement with the feet, the hips and the drop. Then add the hands back in, keeping the feeling that the body is carrying them. Test it against a partner's grip at least once in every session in which you practise Saifa.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Losing the diagonal**

*The fault.* Your three releases drift towards the front rather than going cleanly to the diagonals, and you finish the opening group in the wrong place.

*The cause.* Under-stepping, or turning the body less than forty-five degrees.

*The correction.* Mark the two diagonals on the floor with tape. Practise the three releases slowly, checking your feet against the tape at the end of each. Remember that the diagonal is the point of the movement: you are stepping off the line of the person who is holding you.
:::

## Group two: the kick and the strike

After the third release, the kata turns to face the front. The second group is a short, explosive combination: a front kick, followed by a sequence of hand techniques as you land.

From the finishing position of the third release, turn to face the front and raise the right knee, then deliver a front kick, *mae geri*, at low or middle level. As the foot lands forward, the left hand sweeps down and forward in an open-hand pressing block, and the right hand fires a back-fist strike, *uraken*, to head height, often followed immediately by a hammer-fist or a reverse punch with the other hand. The details of the hand techniques vary between lineages, but the pattern of kick, press and strike is common to most.

The group is then repeated facing the rear, after a turn of one hundred and eighty degrees, so that the combination is practised in both directions.

(4) The right back-fist strike to head height. (5) The follow-up strike with the left hand. (6) The turn to the rear begins. (7) The knee lifts again. (8) The kick to the rear.](figures/fig7_3.png)

### The timing of the group

The key to this group is timing. The kick, the landing, the pressing hand and the back-fist should flow into each other almost as a single movement. A common fault is to perform them as four separate techniques, each with its own little stop. Another is to fire the back-fist before the foot has landed, so that it has no stance beneath it.

The best way to think of it is that the landing of the kicking foot is the moment of power. The pressing hand arrives a fraction before the landing, clearing the way. The back-fist arrives exactly as the foot lands and the body drops into the stance. The follow-up technique arrives on the rebound, as the body turns back.

::: drill
**Drill: Kick, land, strike**

Practise the kick and the landing alone, twenty times with each leg, with the instruction that the foot must land silently and the body must be settled in the stance immediately. Then add the back-fist, timed to the landing, twenty times on each side. Finally add the pressing hand and the follow-up strike. Do the whole combination ten times on each side, slowly, then ten times at speed.
:::

## Group three: the shiko dachi section

The middle of Saifa turns to the sides and drops into shiko dachi. The details of this section differ more between lineages than any other part of the kata, but in most versions it contains a sequence in which one hand grips or presses an imaginary opponent's arm while the other delivers a short, heavy strike, usually a hammer fist or a back fist, as the body drops into shiko dachi.

The movement is typically performed first to one side and then, after a turn, to the other. In some versions there is a step forward with the knee raised, followed by a drop into shiko dachi; in others, a shift sideways; in others, a crossing step into kosa dachi before the drop.

What all versions share is the use of the drop. The hammer-fist or back-fist in this group is not a strike thrown with the arm. It is the weight of the body arriving through the fist as the stance sinks. This is chinkuchi in its most visible form.

![**Figure 7.4** Group three, the shiko dachi section, seen from the front. (1) Facing the side, the left hand reaches forward. (2) The left hand grips and draws back as the right fist rises. (3–4) The body drops into shiko dachi as the right hammer fist descends. (5) The right fist withdraws. (6) The turn to the opposite side. (7) The grip and rise on the other side. (8) The drop and strike.](figures/fig7_4.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The floating shiko dachi**

*The fault.* You arrive in shiko dachi and then strike, so that the strike happens on top of a stance that has already settled.

*The cause.* Treating the stance and the strike as separate events.

*The correction.* Begin the strike from a higher position, with the knees only slightly bent, and let the strike and the drop arrive together. Practise in front of a mirror, watching the top of your head. It should descend at the same moment as the fist and stop dead at the same moment.
:::

## Group four: the double-hand techniques

In the later part of the kata there is a short section in which both hands work together, often from neko ashi dachi. In many versions this includes a double block, one hand high and one hand low, or a double-hand press, followed by a step forward and a strike. Some lineages include a knee strike or a second front kick here.

This group is an introduction to the two-handed work that becomes so important in the later kata. One hand controls while the other strikes, or both hands control together while the legs attack.

## Group five: the close

The final section returns to the starting line. In most versions it contains a drop into shiko dachi with a double technique, typically one fist striking high in a back-fist while the other is held low or strikes downward, followed by a step back into neko ashi dachi and the closing mawashi uke. The kata ends with the return to the ready position and the bow.

(4) The drop into shiko dachi with the double technique, back-fist high and fist low. (5) The step back into neko ashi dachi. (6–7) Mawashi uke. (8) The return to the ready position.](figures/fig7_5.png)

## The rhythm of Saifa

Saifa is a fast kata, but it is not uniformly fast. The three tearing releases are each performed as a single flowing movement, with a short pause at the end of each. The kicking combinations are explosive, with almost no pause between kick and strikes. The shiko dachi section has a heavier rhythm, with a definite drop and a short settle. The closing sequence slows down, with full attention to mawashi uke.

A useful way to learn the rhythm is to count it. Not every movement needs a count, but each group can be given a shape. The releases are *one, and settle*. The kicks are *knee, kick-land-strike-strike*. The shiko dachi section is *grip, and drop*. Practise counting aloud, softly, as you perform the kata. It may feel silly, but it will stop the kata from becoming flat.

## Applications

Saifa's applications are among the most direct in the system. I give here the principal applications of the first three groups, which are taught in some form in almost every Goju-Ryu dojo. Others will be discussed in Part Six.

### The wrist release

The application of the opening group is a release from a same-side wrist grab: the partner's right hand grips your right wrist. Your left hand covers your right fist, locking it. You step to the diagonal, drawing the hands to your left hip, which pulls the partner slightly off balance and turns their wrist. Then you step and drop, driving the elbow and forearm round in a circle against the gap between the partner's thumb and fingers. The grip breaks, and your elbow and forearm continue forward into the partner's face, neck or upper arm.

The same movement works against a cross-hand grab and, with small adjustments, against a two-handed grab on one wrist. Practise all three.

![**Figure 7.6** The wrist release from group one, with a partner. (1) The partner grips the defender's right wrist. (2) The defender covers the fist with the left palm. (3) The step to the diagonal and the pull to the hip. (4) The partner is drawn off balance. (5) The defender steps and drops; the hands begin to rotate. (6) The grip breaks at the thumb. (7) The elbow continues forward into the partner. (8) The defender's final position, the partner's arm cleared.](figures/fig7_6.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Pulling against the grip**

*The fault.* When your partner grips firmly, you end up in a tug of war, pulling directly away from them.

*The cause.* You are trying to pull your hand out rather than turning it against the thumb.

*The correction.* Look at the partner's hand and find the gap between their thumb and fingers. That is the door. Every part of the movement, the cover, the step, the turn and the drop, should rotate your wrist towards that door. Practise slowly at first, with your partner gripping firmly but not resisting the turn, until you can feel the moment the grip opens.
:::

### The kick, press and back-fist

The application of the second group is a response to an opponent who is close and about to strike, or who has just thrown a strike. The front kick goes low, to the groin or the front of the knee, to stop their advance and bend them forward. As you land, your pressing hand smothers their lead arm, pinning it down and preventing it from coming back at you. The back-fist strikes over the top of the pinned arm to the face or temple. The follow-up strike finishes the exchange or creates space to escape.

The important point about this application is the order. Most students, when they first try it, want to strike first. But the kata kicks first, then presses, then strikes. The kick disrupts the opponent's structure; the press controls their arm; only then is the strike safe to throw. This order, disrupt, control, strike, runs through the whole of Goju-Ryu.

### The grip and drop

The application of the third group is a response to an opponent who has grabbed your clothing or your arm with one hand. Your gripping hand takes hold of their wrist or forearm and draws it towards you, straightening their arm. Your body drops into shiko dachi, and your hammer fist strikes down onto their straightened arm, usually just above the elbow, or onto the side of the neck if they have been pulled down far enough. The drop adds the weight of your body to the strike and, at the same time, pulls the opponent down and forward.

![**Figure 7.7** Applications of groups two and three, with a partner. (1) The partner prepares to strike. (2) The defender's low front kick. (3) The landing; the left hand presses the partner's lead arm down. (4) The back-fist over the pinned arm. (5) The partner grips the defender's lapel. (6) The defender grips the partner's wrist and draws the arm straight. (7) The drop into shiko dachi. (8) The hammer fist onto the straightened arm above the elbow.](figures/fig7_7.png)

::: note
**Note: Safety with arm strikes**

Strikes to a straightened elbow can cause serious injury. In partner practice, strike the muscle of the upper arm, above the joint, with a light, controlled touch, or strike a pad held against the arm. Never strike a partner's elbow joint, and never strike with full power in a cooperative drill.
:::

## Training Saifa

Saifa repays steady, detailed work. Here are some suggestions for how to structure your practice.

**Group by group.** Practise each movement group on its own, ten times, before performing the whole kata. Pay particular attention to the group you find weakest.

**Both sides.** Many of Saifa's techniques are performed on one side only in the kata. Practise them on the other side as well, as separate drills. The wrist release, in particular, should be equally good on both sides.

**Slow with tension.** Once a week, perform Saifa slowly, with Sanchin breathing and moderate tension. This will show you where your structure weakens, especially in the drops and the turns.

**Against a partner.** At least once a week, practise the applications of the first three groups with a partner, first cooperatively and then with gradually increasing resistance. The wrist release, in particular, should be tested against a partner who grips hard.

**With hojo undo.** The circular releases are strongly supported by the chi ishi exercises of Chapter 6, particularly the side rotation, and by the nigiri game, which strengthens the grip used in the third group.

## Common questions about Saifa

*Why are there three releases at the beginning?* Repetition in kata serves several purposes. It allows the movement to be practised to both sides. It emphasises the movement's importance. And it gives the performer a chance to perfect the movement within the kata itself. The three releases of Saifa are often said to be the core of the kata; everything else follows from them.

*Why does the kick come before the hand techniques?* Because a low kick is the fastest way to disrupt an opponent's structure at close range, and because it is very difficult to defend against while also defending against hand techniques. In many of the classical kata, the kicks are low and are used to set up hand techniques, rather than as finishing techniques in themselves.

*Why does my version of Saifa differ from the one described here?* Because Saifa, like all the classical kata, has been passed down through several lineages, each of which has preserved and developed it in slightly different ways. The principles are the same. Practise your teacher's version.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Saifa is usually the first classical kata, and its central theme is escaping from being held.
- Its opening movement, the tearing release, is performed three times to the diagonals. It works by turning the whole body against the weak point of the grip, not by pulling with the arm.
- The kick and strike combination teaches the order: disrupt, control, strike.
- The shiko dachi section teaches the use of the drop to add the weight of the body to a short strike.
- The rhythm of Saifa varies from group to group. Count it to stop it becoming flat.
- Test the applications against a partner's grip regularly, and practise the techniques on both sides.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Write out the movement groups of your own version of Saifa and give each group a name.
2. Practise the tearing release on both sides, twenty times each, with your hands held still against your body, so that the body does all the work. Then add the hands.
3. Test the wrist release against a partner's same-side grip, cross-hand grip and two-handed grip, ten times each, at every session for a month.
4. Practise the kick, land, strike drill twenty times on each side.
5. Perform Saifa once a week slowly with Sanchin breathing and once at full speed. Record yourself and compare the two.
6. With a partner, work through the kick, press and back-fist application slowly and cooperatively, making sure the order is kick, then press, then strike.
:::

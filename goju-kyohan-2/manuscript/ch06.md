# Hojo Undo and Tension Drills: Building the Goju Body Safely

::: chapter-intro
The old Okinawan teachers did not have gyms. They had stones, clay jars, iron rings and wooden posts, and with them they built bodies of remarkable strength and resilience. This chapter introduces the traditional supplementary training of Goju-Ryu, hojo undo, explains how each implement is used and what it develops, sets out tension drills that need no equipment at all, and gives a safe, progressive way to build all of it into your training.
:::

In the corner of most traditional Goju-Ryu dojo there is a collection of objects that would puzzle anyone who walked in off the street. There are stones with wooden handles set into them, like oversized lollipops. There are clay or ceramic jars, sometimes half-filled with sand. There are padlock-shaped blocks of stone, an oval iron ring, a heavy wooden post wrapped at the top with straw rope or padding. To the uninitiated they look like museum pieces, and in a sense they are; most of them have been used in much the same way for over a century.

But they are not ornaments. In the hands of someone who knows how to use them, these simple tools build a particular kind of strength that is hard to develop any other way: strength in the grip, the wrists and the forearms, in the shoulders and the back, in the connection between the hands and the centre of the body. This is the strength that the classical kata assume you possess. Without it, many of their techniques, the grabs, the pulls, the twisting releases and the short, heavy strikes, cannot be performed with real effect.

## The purpose of hojo undo

*Hojo undo* means "supplementary exercise". It refers to the conditioning exercises, mostly using traditional implements, that support karate training. Hojo undo is not karate. You can be strong from hojo undo and still have poor karate. But it is very difficult to have strong Goju-Ryu without some form of it, because the style places such heavy demands on the grip, the forearms, the shoulders and the back.

There are three main things hojo undo develops.

**Grip and forearm strength.** Goju-Ryu applications rely heavily on grabbing, holding, twisting and pulling. Many of the classical kata contain movements that only make sense if you have a strong, reliable grip. Several of the traditional implements, especially the nigiri game and the ishi sashi, are designed specifically to build it.

**Joint strength and stability.** The chi ishi, used through a full range of motion, strengthens the wrists, elbows and shoulders, especially in the rotational and twisting movements that are characteristic of Goju-Ryu. Strong, stable joints are less prone to injury, both in training and in applications.

**Connection and structure.** Perhaps most importantly, the traditional implements are always used with correct stance and posture, and often with Sanchin breathing. This means that they train the hands and arms to work in connection with the stance and the back, rather than in isolation. A student who lifts a chi ishi with a raised shoulder and a sagging stance is not doing hojo undo; they are lifting a stone. A student who lifts the same stone in a solid sanchin dachi, with the shoulder down, the back engaged and the breath controlled, is developing exactly the structure that Sanchin requires.

## Principles of safe training

Hojo undo has a reputation in some quarters for being brutal, and it is true that some of the old training methods were very hard. But the best teachers were always careful, and the best students were always patient. The following principles apply to all of the training in this chapter.

**Technique before load.** Learn each exercise with a light weight, or no weight at all, until the movement is correct and you can perform it with good structure. Only then add load. A heavy implement used with poor technique will injure you.

**Gradual progression.** Increase the weight, the number of repetitions or the duration of an exercise slowly, over weeks and months. Your muscles adapt more quickly than your tendons, ligaments and bones, and it is the slower tissues that are injured by too-rapid progress.

**Structure throughout.** Every exercise should be performed in a correct stance, with the shoulders down, the back engaged and the breath controlled. If your structure fails, the set is over, however many repetitions you had planned.

**Balance.** Train both sides equally. Train pulling as well as pushing. Train the back as well as the front. Imbalanced training leads to imbalanced bodies and, eventually, to injury.

**Rest.** Hojo undo should not be done every day at full intensity. Two or three sessions a week are enough for most students, with lighter work on other days if desired.

::: note
**Note: Medical caution**

Some hojo undo exercises place considerable strain on the wrists, elbows and shoulders, and conditioning exercises involve impact. If you have any history of joint problems, high blood pressure, osteoporosis or other conditions that might be affected by heavy loading or impact, consult your doctor before beginning. Children and adolescents, whose bones are still growing, should not perform heavy conditioning or impact work; light implements and bodyweight exercises are appropriate for them.
:::

## Chi ishi: the weighted lever

The *chi ishi*, or "strength stone", is a wooden handle about forty-five to fifty centimetres long with a weight fixed at one end. Traditionally the weight was a stone; today it is often concrete cast in a mould, or a disc of iron. The weight can be as little as one kilogram for beginners and as much as five or six for experienced students, but the leverage of the long handle makes even a modest weight feel heavy.

The chi ishi is used through a wide range of movements that mimic and strengthen the actions of Goju-Ryu techniques: rotations of the wrist and forearm, circular movements of the arm, punching and blocking actions, and lifts and swings that work the shoulders and back. Because the weight is at the end of a lever, the exercises place heavy demands on the wrist and forearm, which must control the weight throughout the movement.

### Basic chi ishi exercises

The following exercises form a basic chi ishi routine. Begin with a light implement and five repetitions of each on each side, and build up gradually to ten or fifteen.

**The forward rotation.** Stand in shiko dachi. Hold the chi ishi by the end of the handle, arm extended in front at shoulder height, the weight pointing up. Lower the weight forward and down, rotating the wrist, until it points at the floor, then raise it back up. Keep the arm still; only the wrist moves.

**The backward rotation.** As above, but lower the weight backward over the shoulder, then raise it again. This is harder, and the weight should be lighter.

**The side rotation.** Stand in shiko dachi with the arm extended to the side. Rotate the forearm so that the weight swings from pointing up, to pointing forward, to pointing down, and back again. This builds the rotational strength used in kake uke, tensho uke and the twisting releases of Saifa.

**The chudan uke.** Stand in sanchin dachi, holding the chi ishi near the weight, so that the handle extends below the hand. Perform a slow chudan uke, then return. The weight resists the outward turn of the forearm.

**The punch.** Stand in sanchin dachi, holding the handle near the weight, and perform a slow punch with full turn of the fist. Then return. Keep the elbow close to the body.

**The circular swing.** Stand in shiko dachi, holding the end of the handle, and swing the chi ishi in a large circle in front of the body, passing over the shoulder and down to the opposite hip. This works the shoulders and back and resembles the large circular movements of mawashi uke.

![**Figure 6.2** Basic chi ishi exercises. (1–2) The forward rotation in shiko dachi. (3–4) The side rotation, weight up and weight down. (5–6) The chudan uke in sanchin dachi, start and finish. (7) The punch. (8) The circular swing over the shoulder.](figures/fig6_2.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Lifting with the shoulder**

*The fault.* As you lift or rotate the chi ishi, your shoulder rises and your upper body leans.

*The cause.* The implement is too heavy, or you are using the shoulder to compensate for weak wrists and forearms.

*The correction.* Use a lighter implement, or hold the handle nearer to the weight to reduce the leverage. Keep the other hand on your shoulder, if necessary, to feel whether it rises. The movement should come from the wrist and forearm, supported by a still, connected shoulder.
:::

## Nigiri game: the gripping jars

The *nigiri game*, or "gripping jars", are a pair of jars, traditionally made of clay or ceramic, with a lip or rim around the opening. They are held by the rim with the fingertips, and the student performs stance training, walking and kata movements while holding them. The jars can be filled with sand, gravel or water to increase their weight, a little at a time.

The nigiri game builds the strength of the fingers and grip, and especially of the fingertips, in a way that few other exercises can match. Because the jars are held at the sides while the student moves through stances, they also train the shoulders and back to stay down and engaged, since a raised shoulder makes the jars swing.

### Using the nigiri game

Begin with empty jars. Grip each jar by its rim, with the fingers spread around the lip, fingertips gripping the outside and the thumb pressing from the inside or opposite side. Do not let the jar rest in the palm.

Stand in sanchin dachi with the arms hanging at the sides, holding the jars. Perform slow Sanchin steps forward and back, maintaining the grip and keeping the shoulders down. Then try shiko dachi, stepping forward and back, and neko ashi dachi. Then try raising the jars forward to shoulder height and lowering them slowly, or raising them to the sides.

Start with just a minute or two of walking. Your fingers will tire very quickly. Over several weeks, extend the time and begin to add weight to the jars, a handful of sand at a time.

(5) Walking in shiko dachi. (6) Raising the jars forward. (7) Raising the jars to the sides. (8) Fault: the shoulders raised and the jars swinging.](figures/fig6_3.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Palming the jar**

*The fault.* As your fingers tire, the jar slips down into your palm and you end up carrying it rather than gripping it.

*The cause.* Fatigue, or a jar that is too heavy for your current grip strength.

*The correction.* The moment the jar slips into the palm, put it down and rest. Use lighter jars or shorter sets. The aim is to train the fingertip grip, not to carry weight for as long as possible.
:::

## Ishi sashi: the stone padlock

The *ishi sashi* is a block of stone or iron shaped like an old-fashioned padlock, with a handle. It is used in pairs, one in each hand, for exercises that combine grip, wrist strength and the whole-body coordination of Goju-Ryu techniques. Because the handle runs across the top of the weight, the implement tends to twist in the hand, and controlling it builds strong wrists and forearms.

Typical ishi sashi exercises include slow punches in sanchin dachi, upward and downward blocks, punches in shiko dachi, and sequences from the kata. In more advanced training, the ishi sashi may be released and caught again, or swung through circular movements. These advanced exercises require great care and should be learned only from an experienced teacher.

## Kongoken: the iron oval

The *kongoken* is a large, heavy oval of iron, typically weighing between twenty and forty kilograms. It is said to have been introduced to Goju-Ryu by Chojun Miyagi after he saw something similar used by wrestlers in Hawaii. It is held at its ends and used for a variety of lifting, swinging and pushing exercises, often with a partner.

The kongoken develops strength in the whole body, especially in the back, the shoulders and the legs, and trains the body to work as a single unit when moving a heavy, awkward load. It is excellent preparation for the pulling, pushing and throwing actions of the classical kata.

Because of its weight, the kongoken is not suitable for beginners or for training alone. It should be used only under supervision, with careful attention to lifting technique.

## Makiwara: the striking post

The *makiwara* is a flexible wooden post, set firmly into the ground or into a heavy base, with a striking pad at the top. Traditionally the pad was made of rice straw rope wrapped around the post; today it is often made of leather, canvas or rubber over foam. The post is usually tapered, so that it flexes when struck and pushes back against the striker.

The makiwara is perhaps the best-known karate training device, and also one of the most misunderstood. It is not primarily for toughening the knuckles. It is for training correct alignment and the delivery of force through a striking surface into a resisting target. The flexibility of the post means that a correctly delivered strike will bend it and feel solid, while a poorly aligned strike will feel weak or will hurt the wrist.

### Using the makiwara

Stand in front of the makiwara in sanchin dachi or seisan dachi, at such a distance that your fist reaches the pad with the elbow slightly bent. Strike the pad with a straight punch, aiming to drive it back several inches, and allow it to push your fist back. Concentrate on the alignment of the fist, wrist, elbow and shoulder, and on the connection of the punch to your stance. Breathe out at the moment of impact.

Begin with light strikes, perhaps twenty with each hand, and increase gradually. You should never strike so hard that you damage your knuckles; the occasional graze is acceptable, but bleeding, swelling or persistent pain means you are overdoing it.

The makiwara can also be used for other techniques: the palm heel, the back fist, the hammer fist, the elbow, the knife hand and the forearm. Each needs its own careful introduction.

(4) The post pushing the fist back. (5) The palm heel. (6) The hammer fist. (7) The elbow strike. (8) Fault: the wrist bending on impact.](figures/fig6_5.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Hitting the makiwara with the arm**

*The fault.* Your punches make a loud slap on the pad but barely move the post.

*The cause.* You are striking with the arm alone, and the punch is not connected to your stance and back.

*The correction.* Reduce the speed and power of the punch and concentrate on the connection. Feel the punch begin from the floor. Turn the waist a short distance and lock it. Drop slightly at the moment of impact. Breathe out sharply. The post should move further even though the punch is slower.
:::

## Conditioning with a partner

In addition to the traditional implements, Goju-Ryu uses partner exercises to condition the body against impact. The most common of these is *kote kitae*, forearm conditioning, sometimes called *ude tanren* or simply "arm pounding". The partners stand facing each other and strike forearm against forearm in a set pattern, gradually toughening the arms and accustoming them to impact.

A typical kote kitae sequence involves three strikes on each side: the outside of the forearm against the partner's outside forearm in a downward swing, the inside of the forearm against the partner's inside forearm in an upward swing, and the outside against the outside again in a middle-level swing. The partners then step and repeat on the other side.

Partner conditioning is also used for the legs and the body, with the partners striking each other's thighs, shins or abdomens in controlled patterns. Body conditioning of this kind is closely related to Sanchin testing and should be approached with the same graded caution.

::: note
**Note: Conditioning without damage**

The aim of conditioning is to make the body more resilient, not to damage it. Strike with control, increasing the force gradually over weeks and months. Never strike bone against bone at full power. Bruising is common at first and will diminish as the body adapts; if bruising becomes severe or persistent, reduce the intensity. Do not condition an area that is already injured.
:::

## Tension drills without equipment

Not everyone has access to traditional implements, and even those who do will benefit from tension drills that need no equipment. These drills use isometric tension, partner resistance and slow movement to develop the same qualities as hojo undo: strength in the structure, connection between the hands and the centre, and control of breath under effort.

### Drill one: the sanchin kamae hold

Stand in sanchin dachi in sanchin kamae. Hold the position for one minute, breathing slowly and steadily with nogare breathing, while maintaining moderate tension throughout the body. Rest for thirty seconds and repeat. Build up over several weeks to three holds of three minutes each.

The value of this drill lies in its simplicity. Without the distraction of movement, you can focus completely on your structure: the stance, the shoulders, the back, the elbows, the breath.

### Drill two: the partner push

Stand facing a partner in sanchin dachi, palms against palms at shoulder height. Push against each other steadily for a slow count of six, breathing out, while maintaining your structure. Release and rest. Repeat five times. Then repeat with your partner pushing on your forearms in sanchin kamae, or pushing on your shoulders, or pulling on your wrists.

### Drill three: the towel pull

Hold a rolled towel or a belt between your hands. Stand in shiko dachi and pull the towel apart, as hard as you can, for a slow count of six, breathing out. Release. Then hold the towel in front of you and twist it, as if wringing it out, in both directions. These drills build grip and the pulling strength of the back.

### Drill four: the slow kata

Perform any classical kata at one quarter of its normal speed, with moderate tension throughout and Sanchin breathing on every technique. This is exhausting and should be done only once a session, but it is one of the most effective tension drills in the system. It is discussed further in Chapter 15.

### Drill five: the self-resisted technique

Perform a technique, such as a chudan uke or a punch, while resisting your own movement with opposing muscles, so that the technique travels slowly against internal resistance. This is sometimes called *dynamic tension*. It should be practised with care, as it raises blood pressure; keep the breath moving at all times.

## A progressive programme

Here is a simple programme for introducing hojo undo and tension drills into your training. It assumes you train karate three or four times a week and have access to at least a chi ishi and a pair of nigiri game. Adjust it to your circumstances.

| Weeks | Twice a week | Once a week |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Chi ishi, light, 5 reps per exercise; nigiri game, empty, 2 minutes walking; sanchin kamae hold, 3 × 1 minute | Partner push and towel drills |
| 5–8 | Chi ishi, light, 8–10 reps; nigiri game, a little sand, 3 minutes; makiwara, 20 strikes each hand | Partner push; kote kitae, light |
| 9–12 | Chi ishi, moderate, 10 reps; nigiri game, more sand, 4 minutes; makiwara, 30 strikes each hand | Kote kitae; slow kata once |
| 13 onwards | Progress one variable at a time: weight, repetitions or duration | Add ishi sashi and supervised kongoken work |

: **Table 6.1** A progressive introduction to hojo undo over twelve weeks and beyond.

The key word is *progressive*. Each week should be slightly harder than the last, but only slightly. If you find yourself sore for more than two days after a session, or if any joint begins to ache, reduce the load and allow more time.

## Hojo undo and the kata

Finally, it is worth remembering why you are doing all this. Hojo undo is not an end in itself. Its purpose is to build a body that can perform the classical kata, and apply their techniques, with real strength and effect.

As your grip strengthens, try the grabbing and pulling movements of Seiyunchin and Seisan and notice how much more solid they feel. As your forearms strengthen, try the twisting releases of Saifa and the hooking blocks of Tensho. As your structure strengthens, try the drops and pulls of Seiyunchin against a resisting partner. The connection between the training tools and the kata should become more and more obvious, until the two feel like parts of the same practice. Which, of course, they are.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Hojo undo is the supplementary training of Goju-Ryu. It develops grip and forearm strength, joint strength and stability, and the connection between the hands and the centre.
- Always put technique before load, progress gradually, maintain structure throughout, train both sides equally and rest adequately.
- The chi ishi builds wrist, forearm and shoulder strength through rotations and technique-specific movements.
- The nigiri game builds fingertip grip and teaches the shoulders to stay down.
- The ishi sashi and kongoken develop whole-body strength and should be learned under supervision.
- The makiwara trains alignment and the delivery of force, not merely tough knuckles.
- Partner conditioning such as kote kitae should be approached gradually and with control.
- Tension drills without equipment, including the sanchin kamae hold, partner pushes, towel pulls and slow kata, develop the same qualities.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Learn the six basic chi ishi exercises with a light implement and perform them twice a week for a month, recording the weight and repetitions.
2. Walk with empty nigiri game for two minutes in sanchin dachi, twice a week, adding a little weight every fortnight.
3. Practise the sanchin kamae hold three times a week, building to three holds of three minutes.
4. With a partner, practise the partner push and towel pull drills once a week.
5. If you have access to a makiwara, begin with twenty light strikes with each hand twice a week, concentrating on alignment.
6. After eight weeks of hojo undo, perform the grabbing and twisting movements of a classical kata against a partner's resistance and note any change in how they feel.
:::

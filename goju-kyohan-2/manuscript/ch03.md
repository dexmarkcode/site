# Stances and Transitions for the Classical Kata

::: chapter-intro
The classical kata ask more of your stances than the basic kata ever did. They are lower, longer and more varied, and you must move between them quickly, often while turning. This chapter revisits each stance used in the kaishu kata, explains how to check it, describes the faults that most often creep into it, and then turns to the transitions between stances, which is where most kata actually go wrong.
:::

When I judge gradings, I find that I can usually tell within the first three or four movements whether a student's kata will be good. I am not looking at their hands. I am looking at their feet, and more particularly at what happens to their body as they move from one stance to the next. A student whose stances are solid and whose transitions are clean will almost always have acceptable technique as well, because good technique grows out of a good base. A student whose stances wobble, whose knees collapse on the turns or whose body rises and falls as they step will struggle however sharp their hands may be.

The basic stances of Goju-Ryu were covered in the first volume, and I am not going to repeat that material in full here. What I want to do instead is look at each stance as it is actually used in the classical kata, which is often rather different from the way it is taught in basic training, and to concentrate on the faults that appear at the intermediate and advanced levels.

## Stances as tools, not positions

It is worth pausing to consider what a stance is for. In basic training, stances are often taught as static positions: you step into the stance, check it, hold it, and then step into the next one. This is necessary at the beginning, but it gives a misleading impression. In the classical kata, and still more in fighting, a stance is not a position you hold. It is a tool you use for a particular job and then put down.

Each stance does certain things well and other things badly. Sanchin dachi is excellent for absorbing and delivering force along a straight line at close range, and for protecting the groin, but it is slow to move in and gives little reach. Shiko dachi is enormously stable against forces from the front and side and allows you to drop your weight sharply, but it is wide and slow to turn. Neko ashi dachi is light and mobile, frees the front leg for kicking and checking, and allows you to retreat quickly, but it is weak against a strong push from the front. Zenkutsu dachi gives you reach and forward drive, but it is easy to pull forward and exposes the front knee.

When you understand what each stance is for, you can begin to understand why the kata use the stances they do. A movement performed in shiko dachi is almost always about dropping, pulling down or resisting a strong force. A movement performed in neko ashi dachi is often about evading, kicking or preparing to move. Once you begin to see this, your stances will improve, because you will be making them for a reason rather than because the sequence says so.

## Sanchin dachi in the classical kata

You will have spent a great deal of time on sanchin dachi already, and it is examined again in the context of Sanchin testing in Chapter 4. Here I want to concentrate on the way it is used in the kaishu kata, where it is often performed more quickly and with less overt tension than in Sanchin itself.

The basic dimensions are familiar. The front foot is turned inwards at roughly thirty to forty-five degrees. The heel of the front foot is level with, or slightly ahead of, the toes of the rear foot, and the feet are about shoulder-width apart. The knees are bent and pressed slightly inwards, so that they would be over the balls of the feet if you looked straight down. The pelvis is neutral and the weight is evenly distributed between the feet, or very slightly forward.

In the classical kata, three things change. First, you often move into sanchin dachi from another stance rather than stepping in the standard crescent pattern. Second, you frequently stay in it for only a single technique before moving on. Third, the tension in the legs is lighter and more responsive, because you need to be able to move out of the stance immediately.

(5) Fault: stance too long, weight back. (6) Fault: front foot turned out, opening the groin. (7) Fault: pelvis tipped forward, buttocks out. ::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The shrinking sanchin dachi**

*The fault.* Your sanchin dachi is correct when you are standing still or performing Sanchin, but in the classical kata it becomes shorter, narrower and higher, until it is little more than an awkward walking stance.

*The cause.* You are thinking about the techniques and the sequence and have stopped paying attention to your feet. The stance shrinks because a smaller stance is easier to move in.

*The correction.* Perform the kata with your attention deliberately on your feet. Every time you arrive in sanchin dachi, pause for a moment and check its width, length and knee position. Do this for a whole session. Then perform the kata at normal speed, checking your stances only at the end of each movement group. Ask a partner to watch your feet specifically, rather than your hands.
:::

### The crescent step

The standard method of stepping forward in sanchin dachi is the crescent step, in which the moving foot travels in a curve inwards towards the supporting foot and then outwards into the new position. This is often taught as a purely aesthetic feature of the stance, but it serves several practical purposes. It keeps the knees close together as you step, protecting the groin. It allows the moving foot to sweep or hook an opponent's foot. And it keeps the body's weight centred over the supporting leg until the last moment, so that you are never caught with your weight between your feet.

At the intermediate level, the commonest fault in the crescent step is to exaggerate the curve so much that the step becomes slow and the body sways from side to side. The curve should be modest, just enough to bring the moving foot close to the supporting foot as it passes. A second fault is to rise as you step, so that your head bobs up and down. Your head should travel at the same height throughout the step.

## Seisan dachi and zenkutsu dachi

Many Goju-Ryu lineages use a stance that sits between sanchin dachi and zenkutsu dachi. It is longer than sanchin dachi, the front foot is turned in only slightly, and the rear leg is more extended. It is usually called *seisan dachi*, after the kata in which it features, or *han zenkutsu dachi*, meaning "half forward stance". In some dojo it is simply considered a longer form of sanchin dachi.

Seisan dachi gives more reach and forward drive than sanchin dachi while retaining some of its protection and stability. It appears in Seisan, in Suparinpei and in several other classical kata, usually in movements that drive forward into an opponent.

Full zenkutsu dachi is less common in Goju-Ryu than in many other styles and is used mainly for long, driving techniques and for the moments when you need to lunge or push a considerable distance. In Goju the stance is typically shorter and a little higher than the deep, long zenkutsu dachi seen in some Japanese styles, and the rear knee is usually not locked straight but kept with a slight bend, so that it can absorb force and spring.

(2) Seisan dachi: longer, front foot turned in slightly, weight a little forward. (3) Zenkutsu dachi: longest, front knee over the toes, rear leg nearly straight. (4) Fault in zenkutsu dachi: the front knee pushed beyond the toes, placing the joint under strain.](figures/fig3_4.png)

## Shiko dachi

If sanchin dachi is the root stance of Goju-Ryu, shiko dachi is its heaviest anchor. It appears in every one of the classical kata and in several of them, Seiyunchin above all, it dominates. A weak shiko dachi will undermine a great deal of your advanced training, and a strong one will give you a foundation that is very hard to shift.

The feet are placed about twice shoulder-width apart, with the toes turned outwards at roughly forty-five degrees. The knees are bent deeply and pressed outwards, so that each knee is directly over its foot. The pelvis is lowered and held neutral, with the tailbone pointing straight down. The back is upright. The weight is evenly divided between the feet.

The image I find most useful for shiko dachi is that of sitting on a horse that is too wide for you. Your thighs are pressed outwards, your hips are dropped, your back is upright and you are gripping the floor with your feet as though they were your knees gripping the horse's sides.

![**Figure 3.5** Shiko dachi and its common faults, seen from the front. (1) Correct. (2) Fault: knees collapsing inward, inside the line of the feet. (3) Fault: too high, with the thighs well above horizontal. (4) Fault: leaning forward, with the hips pushed back. (5) Fault: feet turned out too far, straining the knees. (6) Fault: weight shifted onto one leg. (7) Fault: stance too narrow. (8) The drop into shiko dachi from a standing position, with the head travelling straight down.](figures/fig3_5.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Collapsing knees in shiko dachi**

*The fault.* Your knees drift inwards, inside the line of your feet, especially after you have been in the stance for a few seconds or when you move into it quickly.

*The cause.* Weak or inactive muscles on the outside of the hips and thighs, tight muscles on the inside of the thighs, or simply lack of attention.

*The correction.* Stand in shiko dachi with a light resistance band looped around both thighs just above the knees. Press outward against the band for thirty seconds, then rest. Repeat five times. Then practise shiko dachi with your back to a wall, your heels a few inches from it, and press your knees back towards the wall. Over the following weeks, practise dropping into shiko dachi from a standing position, checking that your knees travel outward over your feet as you descend, not inward.
:::

### Shiko dachi depth and flexibility

Many adult students cannot achieve a deep shiko dachi with correct knee alignment because their hips are not flexible enough. Forcing the stance lower than the hips allow is a mistake; it will push the knees inward, tip the pelvis forward and put strain on the knees and lower back. It is far better to stand in a slightly higher shiko dachi with correct alignment than in a deep one with the knees collapsing.

Work on hip flexibility steadily over months, using the stretches from the junbi undo routine, particularly the wide-legged squat and the side lunge. As your flexibility improves, your shiko dachi will deepen naturally. There is no shortcut, and attempts to find one usually end in injury.

## Neko ashi dachi

Neko ashi dachi, the cat stance, is the stance in which every classical kata ends, and it appears frequently in the middle of several of them, especially Kururunfa, where it is used more than any other stance.

In Goju-Ryu neko ashi dachi, the rear foot is turned out at about thirty to forty-five degrees and bears most of the body's weight, usually about seventy to eighty per cent. The front foot rests lightly on the floor in front, on the ball of the foot, with the heel raised. The front knee is bent and turned slightly inwards to protect the groin. The rear knee is deeply bent, and the hips are low. The upper body is upright.

The Goju-Ryu version of neko ashi dachi is generally longer and lower than that seen in some other styles, with the front foot further forward. This gives it more stability and allows the front leg to be used for kicking, checking and sweeping. But the length must not be overdone, or the stance loses its mobility.

![**Figure 3.6** Neko ashi dachi from the side and its faults. (1) Correct. (2) Correct, with the front foot lifting for a kick. (3) Fault: weight evenly divided, making the front leg heavy. (4) Fault: too high, rear knee nearly straight. (5) Fault: leaning back. (6) Fault: front knee turned out, exposing the groin. (7–8) The step back into neko ashi dachi used at the end of every kata: the rear foot slides back and the weight settles over it as the front foot draws in.](figures/fig3_6.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The heavy front foot**

*The fault.* In neko ashi dachi, you cannot lift your front foot without first shifting your weight backward, so your kicks from the stance are slow and obvious.

*The cause.* Too much weight on the front foot, usually because the stance is too long or the hips are not settled far enough back.

*The correction.* Stand in neko ashi dachi and, without any other movement, lift your front foot an inch off the floor and put it down again. If you cannot do this without your body moving, your weight is too far forward. Adjust until you can tap the front foot freely. Then practise stepping into neko ashi dachi from sanchin dachi and tapping the front foot immediately on arrival. Do twenty repetitions on each side.
:::

## Kosa dachi

Kosa dachi, the crossed-leg stance, appears in several of the classical kata, notably Sepai and Seisan, and in some lineages in Saifa and Kururunfa. It is a transitional stance, used for moving sideways, for turning and for closing distance with a short, deceptive step.

In kosa dachi, one foot crosses in front of or behind the other, so that the legs are crossed at the knees or shins. The front foot is flat on the floor and bears most of the weight; the rear foot rests on the ball of the foot, with the heel raised, tucked close behind the front heel. The knees are bent and pressed together, and the body is low.

Kosa dachi is rarely held for long. It is usually a step into position for the next movement, or a means of lowering the body sharply while maintaining forward pressure. Because the legs are crossed, it is inherently unstable against a push from the side, and you should not linger in it.

## Sagi ashi dachi

Sagi ashi dachi, the heron or crane stance, is a one-legged stance in which the supporting leg is bent and the other is drawn up, with the foot tucked against the inside of the supporting knee or held in front of it. It appears in a few classical kata, often as a preparation for a kick or as a way of lifting the leg clear of a low attack.

Balance on one leg is the obvious challenge. Less obvious is the need to keep the supporting knee bent and the hips low, so that you can move from the stance quickly. A sagi ashi dachi performed on a straight supporting leg looks graceful but is almost useless.

## Transitions: where kata go wrong

I said at the start of this chapter that I look first at a student's transitions. Here is why. A stance held still can be checked and corrected. A transition happens in a fraction of a second, and during that fraction of a second, all the faults you have suppressed in your static stances come back. The knee that you keep pressed outward in shiko dachi drifts inward as you turn. The head that stays level in sanchin dachi bobs up as you step. The weight that is well centred in neko ashi dachi lurches forward as you change direction.

The classical kata are full of transitions: steps, turns, drops, shifts and crossings. Mastering them is at least as important as mastering the stances themselves.

### Principle one: the head stays level

As a general rule, your head should travel at the same height through any transition, unless the kata specifically requires you to rise or drop. This means that your hips must stay at the same height, which in turn means that your supporting knee must stay bent as you step or turn. Rising and falling as you move wastes energy, telegraphs your intentions and makes you easier to unbalance.

There are exceptions. Some transitions in the classical kata involve a deliberate drop, for example from sanchin dachi into shiko dachi, and some involve a deliberate rise, for example when lifting into sagi ashi dachi or preparing for a kick. But these are specific movements with specific purposes. The default is level.

### Principle two: the centre moves first

When you step or turn, your centre of gravity should begin to move before your feet do, or at the same time. A common fault is for the feet to move first and the body to catch up afterwards, which leaves you momentarily with your weight behind your stance. In the classical kata this is particularly noticeable on turns, where the student swivels their feet and then swings their body around to face the new direction.

Instead, think of your hips as initiating the movement and your feet as following. When you turn, the hips begin to rotate, the weight shifts onto the pivoting foot, and the moving foot travels to its new position as a consequence of the hips' movement, arriving at the same moment as the body.

### Principle three: arrive together

At the end of a transition, the stance, the technique and the breath should all arrive at the same moment. If your foot lands and then your hand completes its technique, or your hand completes its technique and then your foot lands, the power of the technique is reduced and the timing is visibly off. This is one of the most obvious differences between a student and an expert performing the same kata. The expert's foot, hand and breath all arrive together. The student's arrive one after the other, like a series of separate events.

### Turning

The classical kata contain turns of ninety degrees, one hundred and eighty degrees and occasionally more. The most important principle in turning is to turn on the balls of the feet, never on the heels. Turning on the heels lifts the front of the foot, breaks the grip on the floor and unbalances you. Turning on the balls of the feet keeps the foot connected and allows you to control the rotation precisely.

The second important principle is to keep the knees bent throughout the turn. Many students straighten their legs as they turn, which raises the body and breaks the connection to the floor. Others allow their knees to collapse inward, which unbalances them and puts the knees at risk.

The third principle is to look before you turn. In almost every turn in the classical kata, the head turns first, so that you see the new direction before your body arrives there. This is not only a matter of good form. It is what you would actually do if an opponent appeared behind you.

![**Figure 3.9** The one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turn in sanchin dachi, seen from above. (1) Starting position. (2) The head turns and the weight shifts onto the front foot. (3) The rear foot travels across behind the front foot in a curve. (4) Both feet pivot on the balls. (5) The new stance, facing the opposite direction. (6) Fault: turning on the heels. The arrow in each panel shows the direction the body faces.](figures/fig3_9.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Losing balance on turns**

*The fault.* You wobble or stagger when you turn, especially on one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turns, and sometimes finish the turn in the wrong position.

*The cause.* Usually a combination of turning on the heels, straightening the knees and moving the feet before the body.

*The correction.* Practise the turn on its own, slowly, twenty times in each direction. Before each turn, pause and check that your weight is on the balls of your feet and your knees are bent. Turn your head first. Then initiate the turn from the hips, keeping the knees bent, and let the feet follow. Arrive in the new stance and pause to check it. When you can do this smoothly and slowly, gradually increase the speed.
:::

### Dropping

Several classical kata, Seiyunchin above all, contain transitions in which you drop suddenly from a higher stance into shiko dachi. These drops are powerful techniques in their own right; the weight of the whole body falling a short distance can break an opponent's grip, pull them off balance or add enormous force to a downward strike.

The key to a good drop is that it should be straight down. The head should descend vertically, without tipping forward or back. The knees should travel outward over the feet as you descend. The pelvis should stay neutral. And the drop should stop sharply at the bottom, in a solid, grounded shiko dachi, rather than bouncing or sagging.

A useful drill for the drop is to stand in heiko dachi and, on a count, drop suddenly into shiko dachi by stepping one foot out to the side and sinking at the same time. Check the stance immediately on arrival, then return to heiko dachi and repeat on the other side. Do ten on each side, three times a week. Over time, the drop will become faster, deeper and more stable.

::: drill
**Drill: The stance ladder**

This drill trains the transitions between the main stances of the classical kata. Perform it slowly at first, checking each stance on arrival, and then gradually increase the speed.

1. Begin in heiko dachi.
2. Step forward into sanchin dachi. Check.
3. Drop sideways into shiko dachi, facing ninety degrees to the side. Check.
4. Shift back into neko ashi dachi, facing the original direction. Check.
5. Step forward into seisan dachi. Check.
6. Cross step into kosa dachi. Check.
7. Lift into sagi ashi dachi. Check.
8. Step down and back into sanchin dachi. Check.

Repeat the whole ladder five times, then repeat it starting with the other foot. As you become more confident, add a simple hand technique on arrival in each stance, making sure that foot, hand and breath arrive together.
:::

## The stance under pressure

There is one final point to make about stances, and it is perhaps the most important. A stance that looks correct may still be weak. The only way to know whether a stance is strong is to test it.

In Sanchin, this testing is formal and is discussed in Chapter 4. For the other stances, you can test them simply by having a partner push or pull you from different directions while you hold the stance. Begin gently and increase the pressure gradually. Notice which directions you are weakest in, and how your body responds to pressure. Do your knees collapse? Does your back round? Do your shoulders rise? Each of these responses tells you something about where your stance needs work.

You will find that each stance is strong in some directions and weak in others. Shiko dachi resists pushes from the front and side well but is weak against a pull directly forward or a push directly from behind along the line of the feet. Neko ashi dachi is strong against a pull from the front but weak against a push. Understanding these strengths and weaknesses is part of understanding why the kata use the stances they do, and it will become important when we turn to applications in Part Six.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Stances are tools for particular jobs, not positions to be held. Understanding what each stance is for explains why the kata use it.
- Sanchin dachi tends to shrink in the classical kata. Check your stances deliberately, especially at the end of each movement group.
- Seisan dachi, or han zenkutsu dachi, sits between sanchin dachi and zenkutsu dachi and is used for driving forward.
- In shiko dachi, the knees must stay over the feet. Do not force depth that your hips cannot support.
- In neko ashi dachi, the front foot must be light enough to lift without any other movement.
- Kosa dachi and sagi ashi dachi are transitional stances and should not be held for long.
- In transitions, keep the head level, move the centre first and make foot, hand and breath arrive together.
- Turn on the balls of the feet with the knees bent, and turn the head first.
- Test your stances with a partner to find their strengths and weaknesses.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Stand in each stance in front of a mirror for thirty seconds, checking it against Figure 3.1 and the fault drawings. Note your two worst faults.
2. Perform every kata you know with your attention entirely on your feet. Ask a partner to watch only your feet and stances and to report what they see.
3. Practise the one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turn in sanchin dachi twenty times in each direction, slowly, checking each principle.
4. Practise the drop from heiko dachi into shiko dachi ten times on each side, three times a week for a month.
5. Practise the stance ladder five times in each direction at every session for two weeks.
6. With a partner, test each stance against pushes and pulls from the front, back and both sides. Record the directions in which each stance is weakest.
:::

# From Bunkai to Oyo: Partner Drills and Pressure Testing

::: chapter-intro
An application that has never been tested is only an idea. This final chapter sets out a structured method for turning the interpretations of the kata into reliable skill: how to be a good training partner, how to build prearranged sequences, how to introduce resistance safely and progressively, how to pressure-test without injury, and how to keep a personal library of applications that genuinely work. It closes with some thoughts on where the work goes from here.
:::

Years ago, I trained for a while with a small group who were very proud of their applications. They had worked out detailed interpretations of every movement in every kata, and they could demonstrate them with great precision. Their partner attacked; the defender blocked, gripped, struck, locked and threw, all in a beautiful flowing sequence, and the partner flew through the air and landed neatly on the mat.

One evening, a visitor joined us, a former rugby player who had taken up karate late. He was asked to act as partner for one of the demonstrations. He attacked as asked, but then, when the defender gripped his arm, he simply did not let go of his balance. He stood there, solid, with his arm in the defender's grip and a puzzled expression on his face, waiting for something to happen. Nothing did. The application, which had worked a thousand times, did not work on him.

It was a useful lesson for everyone in the room. The applications were not wrong. But they had only ever been tested on partners who knew what was supposed to happen and, without meaning to, helped it happen. Against someone who did not, they failed. The group spent the next year rebuilding their applications from the ground up, testing each one against honest resistance. Many did not survive. Those that did became genuinely useful.

## The role of the partner

Before discussing drills and resistance, it is worth considering the person on the other side of the drill: the partner, the one who attacks and receives the technique. The quality of your partner largely determines the quality of your training.

A good partner does four things.

**They attack honestly.** Their punches are aimed at a real target, at a real distance, with real intent. A punch aimed to miss, or thrown from too far away, teaches the defender nothing. This does not mean full power; it means correct aim, distance and commitment.

**They react honestly.** When struck, locked or unbalanced, they respond as a real person would. They do not fly through the air when lightly touched, nor do they stand rigid when genuinely unbalanced. Their reactions give the defender accurate information about whether the technique is working.

**They provide the right level of resistance.** In the early stages, they cooperate. As the defender's skill grows, they resist more, according to a level agreed in advance. They never resist more than agreed, and they never resist less.

**They keep the defender safe.** They take responsibility for their own safety, tapping when a lock begins to hurt and falling correctly when thrown, and they never use the drill to score points or settle scores.

Being a good partner is a skill, and it should be trained as deliberately as any other. Many teachers believe that you learn as much about an application from receiving it as from applying it, because you feel exactly where it works and where it does not.

## The resistance ladder

Resistance must be introduced gradually. If you go straight from cooperation to full resistance, the applications will fail, the partners will become frustrated and someone will probably get hurt. If you never introduce resistance at all, you will never know whether your applications work.

I use a ladder of six levels.

**Level 0: shape.** The partner stands still or moves very slowly. The defender works out the shape of the application, step by step. No resistance at all.

**Level 1: cooperation.** The partner attacks at slow to moderate speed and reacts naturally, but makes no attempt to resist. The defender practises the application until it is smooth.

**Level 2: light resistance.** The partner attacks at moderate speed and offers light resistance: they tense their arm a little when gripped, they shift their weight slightly when pulled. The defender must adapt.

**Level 3: moderate resistance.** The partner attacks at realistic speed and resists genuinely but not fully: they try to pull their arm free, they try to keep their balance, they may try one counter. The defender must work to make the application succeed.

**Level 4: strong resistance within a scenario.** The partner attacks in a specified way and then resists fully, trying to escape or counter, within the limits of safety. The defender must make the application work against a determined partner.

**Level 5: free pressure.** The partner attacks unpredictably within agreed limits, and the defender must recognise the situation and respond with an appropriate application. This is the closest training comes to reality.

Each application should be practised up the ladder, level by level. Do not move up a level until the application works consistently at the current level. Many applications will stall at level 2 or 3; this tells you something about them. The ones that reach level 4 and 5 are the ones worth relying on.

::: note
**Note: Safety at higher levels**

At levels 3 to 5, use protective equipment where appropriate: gum shields, groin guards, light gloves and, for striking, pads or shields. Agree clear limits before each drill: which techniques are allowed, which targets, what intensity. Have a supervisor present. Stop immediately if anyone is hurt or if the drill becomes uncontrolled. Joint locks must still be applied slowly enough for the partner to tap.
:::

## Bunkai kumite: prearranged sequences

The first step from individual applications to fighting skill is the prearranged sequence, or *bunkai kumite*. In bunkai kumite, the partners work through a sequence of attacks and responses drawn from a kata, in order, as a continuous exchange. Many Goju-Ryu associations have standard bunkai kumite for each classical kata; others leave it to individual teachers.

Bunkai kumite has several benefits. It connects the applications of a kata into a coherent whole. It develops timing and distance in a controlled setting. It trains the partner as well as the defender. And it provides a framework that can later be loosened, as resistance and variation are introduced.

Its danger is that it can become choreography: a rehearsed performance in which both partners know exactly what will happen and neither tests anything. To avoid this, bunkai kumite should be practised at increasing levels of resistance, and should eventually be broken up into its component exchanges, which are then practised with variation.

## Flow drills

Between prearranged sequences and free pressure lie flow drills. In a flow drill, the partners exchange a small set of techniques in a continuous loop: one attacks, the other responds; then the roles reverse. The drill can be varied by changing the speed, the order or the techniques.

Flow drills develop the ability to move smoothly from one technique to the next under pressure. They also allow a great many repetitions in a short time.

A simple Goju-Ryu flow drill uses the opening of Shisochin. The partner punches; the defender deflects with kake uke, grips, pulls and strikes with nukite; the partner deflects the nukite with their own kake uke and the roles reverse. The drill continues back and forth, each partner alternately attacking and defending.

![**Figure 18.3** A flow drill from Shisochin. (1) The partner punches. (2) The defender's kake uke. (3) The grip and pull. (4) The defender's nukite. (5) The partner's kake uke deflects the nukite. (6) The partner grips and pulls. (7) The partner's nukite. (8) The defender's kake uke, and the cycle continues.](figures/fig18_3.png)

::: drill
**Drill: Building a flow drill**

Choose two or three techniques from a kata that naturally lead into each other, such as a deflection, a grip and a strike. Practise them as a one-way sequence until both partners can perform them smoothly. Then find a way for the partner to respond to the final technique with the same deflection, so that the roles reverse. Practise the loop slowly for two minutes, then at moderate speed for two minutes, then with light resistance for two minutes.
:::

## Pad work

Many of the most important techniques in the classical kata, the strikes, the elbows, the knees, the joint kicks, cannot be practised at full power against a partner without injury. Pad work fills this gap.

Use focus mitts for short strikes to the head and body, such as back-fists and palm strikes. Use a large kick shield for knees, joint kicks and front kicks. Use a heavy bag for elbows, hammer-fists and the short combinations of Seisan. Use a body shield held against the partner's chest for the double palm pushes of Shisochin and Kururunfa.

The value of pad work is that it allows full power and full commitment, which are impossible in partner drills. A technique that works on a partner at half power may fail at full power, because the timing changes, or it may work much better. Only pad work will tell you.

Pad work can also be combined with partner drills: the partner grips the defender, the defender applies the controlling part of the application, and then strikes a pad held by a third person, or held by the partner in their free hand.

## Pressure testing

Pressure testing is the final stage: applying your techniques against an unpredictable partner who is genuinely trying to make them fail. It is not sparring in the competitive sense. Its aim is not to win but to find out what works.

A good pressure test has a clear scenario, clear limits and a clear question. For example:

*Scenario:* The partner grabs the defender's lapel with one hand and threatens to strike with the other.

*Limits:* Light gloves; the partner may push, pull and strike lightly to the body; the defender may use any technique from Saifa or Seiyunchin; locks applied slowly; stop on tap or call.

*Question:* Which of the defender's applications succeed in breaking the grip and taking control within five seconds?

The test is run for a short period, perhaps thirty seconds, and then stopped. The partners discuss what happened. What worked? What failed? Why? The test is then run again, perhaps with a change to the scenario or the limits.

### Learning from failure

Pressure testing will show you that many of your applications fail. This is its purpose. A failed application is not a disaster; it is information. When an application fails, ask:

*Was the technique correct?* Perhaps the grip was in the wrong place, or the turn was too small, or the drop came too late.

*Was the timing right?* Perhaps the technique was applied too early, before the partner had committed, or too late, after they had recovered.

*Was it the right technique for the situation?* Perhaps a different application from the same kata, or from a different kata, would have worked better.

*Does the application work at all against resistance?* Some applications, however attractive, simply do not. Be honest about this.

Record your findings in your training notebook. Over time, you will build a clear picture of which applications you can rely on and which you cannot.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Fighting the test**

*The fault.* In pressure tests, you abandon the kata entirely and resort to whatever techniques you can, often from kickboxing or wrestling, or simply brute strength.

*The cause.* Under pressure, untrained instinct takes over.

*The correction.* This is normal at first and is itself useful information: it tells you that your kata applications are not yet available to you under pressure. Return to levels 2 and 3 of the resistance ladder and drill the applications more until they are automatic. Then return to pressure testing. Gradually, the kata techniques will begin to appear.
:::

## Building a personal library

Over years of study and testing, you will accumulate a library of applications: interpretations of kata movements that you understand, that you can perform, and that you have tested. This library is one of the most valuable products of your training. Keep it organised.

I suggest a simple system. For each application, record the kata and movement it comes from, the situation it addresses, a brief description, the highest level of resistance at which it has worked for you, and any notes on what makes it work or fail. Group the applications by situation: escapes from grips, responses to punches, responses to pushes, close-range strikes, takedowns, and so on.

Review the library regularly. Test the applications again. Remove those that no longer work for you and add new ones as you discover them. Over time, the library will become a reliable guide to what you can actually do, which is a very different thing from what you know.

## Teaching applications

If you teach, you will need to teach applications to others. A few suggestions, drawn from my own mistakes as much as my successes.

**Teach the principle first.** Before showing a specific application, explain the principle it relies on: the weak point of the grip, the drop, the turn, the returning hand. Students who understand the principle can adapt the application; students who only know the application cannot.

**Start slowly and simply.** Begin at level 0 or 1 of the resistance ladder, with a simple version of the application. Add detail and resistance gradually.

**Teach partnering.** Spend time teaching students how to be good partners: how to attack honestly, react honestly, resist at the right level and keep each other safe.

**Encourage exploration.** Once students understand the principles, ask them to find their own applications. Some of their discoveries will be poor; some will surprise you.

**Test everything.** Do not teach applications you have not tested yourself. Do not let students believe that an application works until they have tested it under resistance.

## Where the work goes from here

We have come a long way since the first chapter of this book. We began with a stalled student, a capable brown belt or new black belt who had learned the basic kata and Sanchin and was not sure how to go further. We have looked at the body mechanics that make Goju-Ryu work, the stances and transitions of the classical kata, the deep work of Sanchin and Tensho, the old training tools, each of the eight classical kata in turn, the lifetime curriculum, and finally the methods of reading the kata and testing their applications.

If you have worked through this material, you will have noticed that it has no end. Every chapter points to further work. Every kata contains more than any book can describe. Every application opens onto others. That is not a failing of the book or of the art. It is the nature of a living tradition.

The old Okinawan teachers did not regard karate as something to be completed. They regarded it as something to be practised, every day, for as long as they lived. Chojun Miyagi is reported to have trained until shortly before his death. Many of his students did the same. They did not do this because they had not yet learned enough. They did it because the practice itself was the point.

I hope that this book has helped you to see a little further into Goju-Ryu, and to find the way past whatever obstacle had stalled you. But the book can only point. The walking is yours. Go back to the floor. Stand in Sanchin. Breathe. Begin again.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- An application that has never been tested is only an idea.
- A good partner attacks honestly, reacts honestly, resists at the agreed level and keeps the defender safe. Partnering is a skill to be trained.
- Introduce resistance gradually, using a ladder of six levels from shape to free pressure. Do not move up until the application works consistently.
- Bunkai kumite connects applications into sequences but must not become choreography.
- Flow drills develop smooth transitions and allow many repetitions.
- Pad work allows full power for techniques that cannot be practised fully on a partner.
- Pressure tests should have a clear scenario, clear limits and a clear question. Failure is information.
- Keep a personal library of tested applications, and teach principles before techniques.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. With a regular partner, discuss and agree what honest attacking, reacting and resisting mean for each of you. Practise being a good partner deliberately for a month.
2. Choose three applications and take each up the resistance ladder, recording the highest level at which it works.
3. Learn or devise a bunkai kumite for one classical kata and practise it at levels 1 to 3.
4. Build and practise a flow drill from a kata of your choice.
5. Practise the striking techniques of the classical kata on pads, at full power, once a week.
6. Design a pressure test with a clear scenario, limits and question. Run it with your partner and record what you learn.
7. Begin your personal application library, using Table 18.1 as a model.
:::

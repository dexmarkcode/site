# Sepai: Circles, Deception and the Hidden Throw

::: chapter-intro
Sepai is a kata of circles. Its blocks redirect rather than stop, its steps cross and turn, and its most interesting techniques are hidden inside movements that look, at first sight, like something else. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the circular and deceptive principles on which it is built, deals with its characteristic faults and explains the throws and close-range applications it contains.
:::

There is a moment in Sepai that most students perform for years without understanding. Both arms sweep round in a large circle, one after the other, like the spokes of a wheel, and the body drops into a low stance. It looks graceful and slightly theatrical, and many students treat it as a kind of flourish, a moment of decoration between the more obviously practical techniques on either side of it.

It was only when a teacher asked me to perform that movement with my hands on a partner's shoulders and arm that I understood it. The circle of the arms, combined with the turn and the drop of the body, tipped my partner forward and over in a smooth, almost effortless arc. He landed on the mat looking very surprised. The "flourish" was a throw. It had been a throw all along, and I had been practising it in the air for years without knowing.

Sepai is full of moments like this. More than any of the kata we have studied so far, it hides its applications inside movements that look like something else. That is part of what makes it difficult, and a large part of what makes it rewarding.

## The character of Sepai

Sepai, also written Seipai, means eighteen, and like the other numerical names its meaning is uncertain. It is a medium-length kata with a varied floor pattern, moving to the diagonals, crossing and turning, and returning repeatedly to the centre.

Its defining characteristic is circularity. Almost every technique in Sepai either moves in a circle or depends on a circular movement of the body. Blocks sweep round rather than strike out. Steps cross over and turn. Strikes follow curved paths. The effect is a kata that flows, that seems to wind in and out of an imagined opponent rather than attacking them head-on.

Sepai also introduces, more strongly than the earlier kata, the idea of deception. Several of its movements contain a technique that appears to go one way while the real work is done in another direction. A low block conceals a grab. A turning step conceals a sweep. A high strike conceals a pull that brings the opponent onto a low one. Learning to see these hidden techniques is one of the main tasks of studying Sepai.

## The principle of the circle

Why circles? Because a circular movement can redirect a force without having to stop it. If someone punches you and you block with a straight, hard technique, you must meet the full force of their punch with your arm. If they are stronger than you, you lose. If you block with a circular technique, sweeping their arm aside as it passes, you need only enough force to change its direction slightly. A small, well-timed circle can redirect a very large force.

The same principle applies to the body. A body that turns in a circle as an opponent pushes it cannot be pushed over; the force simply slides off. A body that turns while holding an opponent can whip them around with surprising power, because the force at the edge of a turning circle is much greater than the force at its centre.

Sepai trains both of these uses of the circle: the circular deflection of incoming force, and the circular projection of force outward through the turning body.

::: drill
**Drill: The circular deflection**

Stand facing a partner in sanchin dachi. Your partner throws slow straight punches at your chest, alternately with the left and right hands. Instead of blocking each punch with a hard, straight block, sweep it aside with an open hand moving in a small circle, from the inside outward or from the outside inward. Use the least force you can.

Begin slowly. When you can redirect each punch easily, ask your partner to speed up and to vary their targets. Notice how little force is needed when the timing is right, and how much is needed when it is wrong.
:::

## Group one: the opening deflections

From the ready position, the kata turns to the right diagonal and performs a sequence that combines an open-hand deflection with a counterattack. In a common version, you step into neko ashi dachi or sanchin dachi facing the diagonal; the hands sweep in a circle to deflect an imagined attack; one hand grips and pulls; and you strike with the other hand, often with a back-fist or a palm strike, sometimes dropping into shiko dachi as you do so.

The sequence is then repeated to the left diagonal.

The opening establishes the character of the kata. Nothing in it is a straight line. The deflection curves, the grip turns, the strike follows the curve back towards the opponent.

## Group two: the crossing steps

The second group moves forward along the main line using the crossing step into kosa dachi. In a typical sequence, you step across with one foot so that the legs are crossed, the body drops, and the hands strike with a back-fist, a hammer-fist or an inverted punch, *ura tsuki*. The other foot then steps out of the cross into a new stance, often shiko dachi or sanchin dachi, with another technique.

The crossing step serves several purposes. It allows you to close distance quickly and deceptively, because the step across is shorter and less obvious than a full step forward. It drops the body sharply, adding weight to the strike. And it turns the body slightly, which can be used to pull an opponent off balance or to set up a sweep.

![**Figure 11.3** Group two, the crossing step, seen from the side. (1) The starting stance. (2) The rear foot crosses in front. (3) The drop into kosa dachi with the back-fist. (4) The inverted punch, ura tsuki, from kosa dachi. (5) The rear foot steps out. (6) The new stance with the low block. (7) The second crossing step. (8) The hammer-fist downward from kosa dachi.](figures/fig11_3.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The unstable cross**

*The fault.* When you step into kosa dachi, you wobble or tip sideways.

*The cause.* The feet are too far apart along the line of the cross, or the weight is evenly divided rather than mostly on the front foot.

*The correction.* Keep the crossed feet close together, the rear foot tucked behind the front heel and resting only on the ball of the foot. Put almost all your weight on the front foot. Keep the knees pressed together and bent. Practise the step into kosa dachi and out again, twenty times on each side, pausing in the cross to check your balance.
:::

## Group three: the wheel

The central group of Sepai contains the circular arm movement I described at the start of this chapter. The arms sweep round in large circles, one after the other, as the body turns and then drops into a low stance, typically shiko dachi. The exact form differs between lineages: in some versions the arms sweep forward and down, in others backward and up; in some the body turns a full one hundred and eighty degrees, in others only ninety.

In the kata, the wheel is usually followed by a low strike, often a hammer-fist or back-fist downward, from the low stance. This represents a strike to an opponent who has just been thrown to the floor.

![**Figure 11.4** Group three, the wheel, seen from the front. (1) The arms extended, one high and one low. (2) The high arm begins to sweep down and across. (3) The low arm rises. (4) The arms cross. (5) The body turns as the arms complete the circle. (6) The drop into shiko dachi. (7) The low hammer-fist. (8) The return to guard.](figures/fig11_4.png)

### Why the wheel is a throw

The wheel works as a throw because it combines three forces. The hand that sweeps down pulls the opponent's arm or shoulder forward and down. The hand that sweeps up lifts the opponent's other arm, or their head, or pushes under their armpit. And the turn and drop of the body add rotation and weight. The result is that the opponent is tipped forward and rotated around the defender's body, landing on the floor beside or in front of them.

When you perform the wheel in the kata, imagine your hands on an opponent's arm and shoulder. Feel the pull, the lift and the turn as a single movement. The arms are not waving; they are turning a body over.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The decorative wheel**

*The fault.* Your arms sweep gracefully round, but your body stays upright and does not drop until after the arms have finished.

*The cause.* Treating the arm movement as the technique and the drop as a separate event.

*The correction.* The drop is the power of the throw. Practise the wheel with your hands on a partner's arm and shoulder, slowly, on a mat, and feel how the drop does most of the work. Then perform the kata with the drop beginning as the arms begin their circle, so that the arms and the body arrive together.
:::

## Group four: the rear elbow

Towards the end of the kata, there is a group in which the body turns and the elbow strikes backward. In many versions, the hands are first raised or crossed in front of the body, then the body turns or steps and the elbow drives back, sometimes followed by a back-fist or hammer-fist as the body turns to face the rear.

This is one of the few techniques in the classical kata that deals directly with an opponent behind you. Its application is an escape from a grip from behind, such as a bear hug or a collar grab.

## Group five: the close

The kata returns to its starting point and finishes with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

## Deception in Sepai

I said that Sepai hides its applications inside movements that look like something else. This is not unique to Sepai; all the classical kata do it to some extent. But Sepai does it more than most, and it is worth thinking about why.

One explanation is that the old masters deliberately disguised their techniques, so that onlookers could not learn the real applications simply by watching. There is probably some truth in this, though it is easy to exaggerate.

A more useful explanation is that a single movement often contains several possible applications, and that the kata shows the movement in a general form that can be adapted to many situations. The wheel in Sepai can be a throw, a release from a grip, a deflection of two attacks at once or a way of turning an opponent around to attack their back. The kata does not tell you which; it gives you the movement and leaves you to discover its uses.

A third explanation is that some movements in the kata show the technique as it would be performed in the air, without an opponent, and that this naturally makes it look different from the technique as it would be performed with one. A throw performed without anyone to throw looks like a dance. A grip performed without anyone to grip looks like a vague hand gesture.

Whatever the explanation, the practical lesson is the same. When a movement in Sepai seems purely decorative or puzzling, ask what it would do if there were a person in your hands. Very often the answer will surprise you.

## Applications

### The deflection and counter

The opening group's application is a deflection of a straight punch followed by a grip and a counterstrike. The partner punches; the defender sweeps the punch aside with a circular open hand, which then turns over and grips the partner's wrist or sleeve. The defender pulls the partner forward and off balance, and strikes with the other hand as the partner is drawn in.

### The crossing step and the sweep

The crossing step of group two can be used to close distance and set up a sweep or a takedown. As the partner attacks, the defender steps across into kosa dachi, moving inside or to the side of the attack. The crossed rear foot is placed behind the partner's foot. As the defender steps out of the cross, the stepping leg sweeps or blocks the partner's leg, and the hands push or pull the partner over it.

(4) The pull and the counterstrike. (5) The partner attacks; the defender steps across into kosa dachi. (6) The defender's rear foot is placed behind the partner's leg. (7) The defender steps out, blocking the partner's leg. (8) The partner is pushed back over the leg.](figures/fig11_6.png)

### The wheel throw

The application of the wheel has already been described. The partner grips or pushes the defender. The defender takes the partner's arm with one hand and their shoulder, collar or upper arm with the other. The defender turns and drops, the low hand pulling down and the high hand lifting and turning, and the partner is tipped over and rotated to the floor. The defender follows with a low strike.

### The rear elbow escape

The application of group four is an escape from a grip from behind. The partner seizes the defender from behind in a bear hug, over or under the arms. The defender drops their weight to prevent being lifted, raises the hands or turns the hips to create a little space, and drives the elbow back into the partner's ribs or solar plexus. As the grip loosens, the defender turns to face the partner and strikes with a back-fist or hammer-fist.

(3) The turn and the drop begin. (4) The partner is tipped forward. (5) The partner seizes the defender from behind. (6) The defender drops the weight. (7) The rear elbow strike. (8) The defender turns and strikes.](figures/fig11_7.png)

::: note
**Note: Practising the wheel throw**

The wheel throw can drop a partner heavily onto the shoulder or back. Practise it only on mats, with a partner who knows how to fall, and under supervision. Begin with the entry and off-balancing only, then complete the throw slowly. Never perform it at speed in a cooperative drill.
:::

## Training Sepai

**Learn the circles.** Practise the circular deflection drill with a partner until circular blocking feels natural.

**Steady the cross.** Practise kosa dachi on its own until you can step into it and out of it smoothly and without wobbling.

**Find the throw.** Practise the wheel with a partner on a mat, slowly, until you can feel how the drop and the turn create the throw. Then return to the kata and try to preserve the feeling.

**Ask what it does.** For every movement in Sepai that seems puzzling, try it with a partner and ask what it would do with a person in your hands. Record your discoveries in your training notebook.

**Keep it flowing.** Sepai should flow. Avoid stopping dead between movements. Even the pauses should feel like part of a continuous circle.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sepai is a kata of circles: circular deflections, crossing and turning steps, and circular projections of force.
- A circular block redirects an attack with little force. A turning body can project an opponent with great force.
- The crossing step into kosa dachi closes distance deceptively, drops the body and can set up a sweep.
- The wheel is a throw, combining a pull, a lift, a turn and a drop. The drop provides most of the power.
- The rear elbow is an escape from a grip from behind.
- Sepai hides many of its applications inside movements that look decorative. Ask what each movement would do with a person in your hands.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise the circular deflection drill with a partner for five minutes at every Sepai session.
2. Practise stepping into and out of kosa dachi twenty times on each side, pausing in the cross to check your balance.
3. On a mat, with a partner, practise the entry and off-balancing of the wheel throw, ten times on each side.
4. Practise the rear elbow escape with a partner gripping you lightly from behind, ten times on each side.
5. Choose the three movements in Sepai you understand least. Try each with a partner and record at least one possible application for each.
6. Perform Sepai with the instruction to yourself that there must be no dead stops. Record yourself and look for places where the flow breaks.
:::

# Kakie, Tuite and Close-Range Control

::: chapter-intro
Almost every classical application depends on skills that are not obvious in the solo kata: the ability to feel an opponent through contact, to seize and control a limb, to bend a joint against its natural movement, and to take an opponent's balance. This chapter develops kakie beyond its basic form, introduces the principles of tuite, the art of joint manipulation, and sets out the close-range controls and takedowns on which the applications of Goju-Ryu rest.
:::

When I first began to study applications seriously, I made a discovery that rather annoyed me. I could interpret a movement correctly, explain it clearly and demonstrate it beautifully with a cooperative partner. But when the partner moved even slightly differently from what I expected, I lost them. Their arm slipped out of my grip. Their elbow bent when I wanted it straight. Their weight shifted and my throw went nowhere. The application was right; I simply did not have the skills to make it work on a real body.

Those skills are the subject of this chapter. They are not taught in the solo kata, though the kata assume them. They are taught through partner exercises, above all kakie, and through the patient study of how the human body can be controlled at close range: how joints move and resist, how balance is held and lost, how a grip can be made secure and how it can be broken. Without them, applications remain theoretical. With them, the kata come alive.

## Kakie revisited

In Chapter 5 we met kakie in its basic form: two partners with the backs of their wrists in contact, pushing, absorbing and redirecting in a continuous cycle. That basic cycle develops structure, sensitivity and muchimi. But it is only the beginning.

Kakie in its fuller form becomes a free, close-range exercise in which either partner may, at any moment, change from pushing to grabbing, striking, locking, throwing or escaping. It is not sparring in the usual sense; it is slower and closer, and the emphasis is on control rather than scoring. But it is unscripted, and it tests whether the skills of the kata can be applied against a partner who is trying to apply them to you.

### Stage one: the basic cycle

Begin, as in Chapter 5, with the basic cycle of push, absorb and redirect. Practise it with both arms, with steps forward and back, and with changes of rhythm, until it is smooth and continuous.

### Stage two: the opening

In the second stage, each partner looks for openings during the cycle. When one partner's guard opens, because their elbow has drifted outward or their hand has been redirected too far, the other partner touches the target, usually the chest or face, with the palm. The touched partner must then close the opening on the next cycle.

This stage develops awareness of openings and the habit of protecting your own centre line.

### Stage three: the grab

In the third stage, either partner may grab the other's wrist or forearm at any point in the cycle. The grabbed partner must immediately respond, either by releasing the grip, using the techniques of Saifa, or by counter-grabbing and taking control.

### Stage four: the technique

In the fourth stage, either partner may apply a technique from the kata at any moment: a strike, a lock, a takedown. The other partner must respond: defend, escape or counter. The exercise becomes a continuous exchange of attempts and responses, always maintaining contact.

### Stage five: free kakie

In the fifth stage, all restrictions are removed except safety. The partners engage in continuous close-range exchange, using whatever techniques they can apply, at a moderate pace and with control. This is the closest Goju-Ryu comes to free fighting at grappling range, and it is one of the most valuable exercises in the system.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Strength over sensitivity**

*The fault.* In free kakie, you rely on strength to force your techniques, and against a stronger partner you cannot do anything.

*The cause.* You have not yet developed the sensitivity to feel your partner's weak moments and use them.

*The correction.* Go back to stage two and work at a very low level of force. Concentrate on feeling your partner's pressure and finding the moments when they are off balance or overcommitted. When you do move to stages three to five, set a rule that neither partner may use more than half their strength. You will be surprised how much more you can do.
:::

## The principles of tuite

*Tuite*, sometimes written *tuidi*, is an Okinawan term for the art of seizing and manipulating the joints. In Goju-Ryu, it is not a separate system but a dimension of the kata: many movements, especially the grips, twists and turns, contain joint manipulations that become visible once you know what to look for.

Tuite depends on a few simple principles.

### Joints have a range

Every joint moves easily in some directions and not at all in others. The elbow bends but does not bend backward. The wrist bends forward and back and rotates, but only so far. The shoulder rotates, but has limits. Tuite works by moving a joint beyond its comfortable range, or in a direction it does not move, so that the opponent must either follow the movement with their whole body or be injured.

### Control the end, move the middle

To lock a joint, you usually need to control the limb on both sides of it. To lock the elbow, for example, you control the wrist and press against the upper arm just above the elbow. To lock the wrist, you control the hand and the forearm. The joint in the middle is then trapped and can be bent or twisted.

### The body follows the joint

When a joint is locked, the opponent's body will try to move to relieve the pressure. This is not a problem; it is an opportunity. By moving the joint in a particular direction, you can guide the opponent's whole body: down to the floor, round in a circle, onto your next technique.

### Use the body, not the arms

As with everything in Goju-Ryu, joint manipulations are powered by the body, not the arms. The turn of the waist, the drop into a stance and the step of the feet supply the force. The hands simply hold and guide.

::: note
**Note: Safety with joint locks**

Joint locks can injure very quickly, especially at the wrist and elbow. Always apply them slowly in training. Your partner must tap, on you or on the floor, as soon as they feel pain, and you must release immediately. Never apply a lock with a jerk or a sudden twist. Never practise locks on someone who has not been taught to tap. Children and adolescents should practise only the gentlest versions.
:::

## Wrist controls

The wrist is the joint most often seized in Goju-Ryu applications, because it is the joint closest to an attacking hand. Two wrist controls appear again and again.

### The outward turn

The defender grips the partner's hand, with the thumb on the back of the partner's hand and the fingers wrapped around the base of the thumb. The defender then turns the partner's hand outward, away from the partner's body, bending the wrist and rotating the forearm. The partner's body follows the turn, and if the turn continues, they will fall to the floor. This control appears in the turning movements of Kururunfa and Sepai.

### The inward bend

The defender grips the partner's hand and bends the wrist forward and inward, towards the inside of the forearm, while pressing the partner's elbow down. This creates a painful lock that can be used to lead the partner forward and down. It appears in the hooking and pressing movements of Tensho and Shisochin.

## Elbow controls

The elbow is the target of some of the most powerful applications in the classical kata: the arm bars of Seiyunchin, Shisochin and Suparinpei. All of these work by controlling the partner's wrist and pressing or striking against the back of the arm, just above the elbow, while the arm is straight.

### The straight arm bar

The defender grips the partner's wrist and pulls it, so that the arm straightens. The defender's other forearm or palm presses against the back of the partner's upper arm, just above the elbow. The defender turns and drops, driving the partner's shoulder down towards the floor. This is the most common elbow control in Goju-Ryu.

### The bent arm lock

The defender takes the partner's arm, bent at the elbow, and rotates it so that the forearm moves behind the partner's back, as in a hammerlock. This control is less common in the kata, but it appears in some interpretations of the turning movements of Sepai and Kururunfa.

### The figure-four lock

The defender traps the partner's bent arm with both arms, one hand on the partner's wrist and the other arm threaded under the partner's upper arm to grip the defender's own wrist. The lever created rotates the partner's shoulder. Some teachers find this lock in the double-hand movements of Seisan and Suparinpei.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Locking with the arms**

*The fault.* Your arm bars work against a cooperative partner but fail when your partner tenses their arm.

*The cause.* You are trying to bend the joint with arm strength alone.

*The correction.* Secure the grip, then use your body. Turn your waist to pull the wrist. Drop into your stance to press down on the arm. Step to change the angle. The arms only hold; the body does the work. Practise slowly with a partner who tenses moderately, and feel how the turn and drop overcome their resistance where the arms alone cannot.
:::

## Breaking balance

Every throw and takedown depends on breaking the opponent's balance first. In Japanese martial arts this is called *kuzushi*. In Goju-Ryu the term is used less, but the principle is everywhere: in Saifa's diagonal pull, in Seiyunchin's drop, in Sepai's wheel, in Kururunfa's turning retreat.

Balance can be broken in eight basic directions: forward, backward, to either side and to the four diagonals. In each case, the aim is to move the opponent's centre of gravity outside their base, so that they must step to recover. If they cannot step, because their foot is blocked, swept or held, they fall.

The most useful directions in Goju-Ryu are forward and down, which is achieved by pulling the opponent's arm or head towards and below you, often with a drop; and backward on the diagonal, which is achieved by pushing the opponent's upper body back over one of their heels.

::: drill
**Drill: Eight directions**

Stand facing a partner, each holding the other's sleeves at the elbow. Without stepping, try to break your partner's balance in each of the eight directions in turn, using small pulls, pushes and turns of your body. Your partner stands naturally and allows themselves to be moved, stepping to recover when their balance goes. Then change roles.

Notice which directions are easy and which are hard. Notice how much less force you need when you drop slightly as you pull, or turn your waist as you push.
:::

## Takedowns

The classical kata contain several takedowns. They are rarely the spectacular throws of judo, in which the opponent is lifted high into the air. They are more often short, close-range techniques that drop the opponent to the floor with a twist, a push or a sweep, leaving the defender standing.

### The forward drop

The defender breaks the partner's balance forward and down, usually by pulling on the arm and dropping into shiko dachi, and drives the partner to the floor face down. This appears in Seiyunchin, Seisan and Suparinpei.

### The rear trip

The defender steps behind the partner's leg, often with a crossing step into kosa dachi, and pushes the partner's upper body back over the blocked leg. This appears in Sepai and in some interpretations of Saifa.

### The turning throw

The defender turns the partner around their own body, using a grip on the arm and a lift or push on the shoulder or head, as in Sepai's wheel and Kururunfa's turning takedown.

### The hip throw

The defender turns in front of the partner, lowers their hips below the partner's centre of gravity and lifts the partner over the hip. This appears in some interpretations of Seiyunchin's lifting movement.

## Escapes and counters

Close-range control works both ways. If you can grab, lock and throw your partner, they can do the same to you. Learning to escape is as important as learning to control.

The escapes of Goju-Ryu follow a few simple principles. Attack the weak point of the grip, which is usually the gap between the thumb and fingers. Move the whole body, not just the arm. Turn and drop to take the partner's balance as you escape. And follow the escape immediately with a counter, so that the partner has no chance to grab again.

These principles are taught most clearly in Saifa, whose tearing releases are escapes from wrist grips, and in Seiyunchin, whose opening is an escape from a two-handed grip. Sepai's rear elbow is an escape from a grip from behind. Practise these escapes regularly, not only as kata applications but as responses to grips applied during kakie.

(5) The partner's grip from behind. (6) The Sepai escape. (7) The partner's arm bar. (8) The defender's escape by stepping into the partner and bending the elbow.](figures/fig17_5.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The escape without a counter**

*The fault.* You escape successfully from your partner's grip, but then stand back and wait for them to try again.

*The cause.* Treating the escape as the end of the exchange.

*The correction.* Every escape in the kata is followed by a counter. Practise every escape with a counter attached: Saifa's release with the elbow, Seiyunchin's release with the low strike, Sepai's rear elbow with the turning strike. The escape and the counter are one movement.
:::

## Integrating close-range control into kata practice

Once you have begun to develop these skills, take them back to the kata. Perform each kata with the specific intention of controlling an opponent at close range. Feel the grips as grips, the turns as joint locks or throws, the drops as balance-breakers. The kata will look much the same from the outside. But from the inside, it will feel completely different, and when you return to partner work you will find that your applications work much better.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Applications depend on close-range skills that the solo kata assume but do not teach: sensitivity, grip, joint control and balance-breaking.
- Kakie progresses through five stages, from the basic cycle to free kakie. Sensitivity matters more than strength.
- Tuite, the art of joint manipulation, works by controlling the limb on both sides of a joint and moving the joint beyond its range. The body follows the joint.
- The most common wrist controls are the outward turn and the inward bend. The most common elbow control is the straight arm bar.
- Every takedown depends on breaking balance first. Forward and down, and backward on the diagonal, are the most useful directions.
- Escapes attack the weak point of the grip, move the whole body and are followed immediately by a counter.
- Joint locks and throws must always be practised slowly, with tapping and immediate release.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. With a partner, work through the five stages of kakie over three months, spending at least ten minutes on kakie at every session.
2. Practise the outward wrist turn and the inward wrist bend slowly with a partner, ten times on each side, with tapping and immediate release.
3. Practise the straight arm bar with a partner who tenses moderately, using the turn and drop rather than arm strength.
4. Practise the eight directions drill for five minutes at every partner session for a month.
5. On mats, practise the entry and off-balancing of each of the four takedowns, ten times on each side.
6. During kakie, have your partner apply random grips. Respond to each with an escape and a counter from the kata.
:::

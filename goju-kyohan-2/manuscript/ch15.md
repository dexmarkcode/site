# Kata as a Lifetime Curriculum

::: chapter-intro
Eight classical kata, two breathing kata, a handful of old training tools and a partner drill: on paper it is not a large syllabus. In practice it is enough for a lifetime. This chapter steps back from the individual forms to ask how the whole system fits together, how to keep many kata alive at once, how to structure training sessions and training years, how to adapt practice as the body ages or is injured, and how to keep improving long after the last new kata has been learned.
:::

One of the teachers I most admired in my early years was a woman in her seventies who still trained four times a week. She no longer performed the deep shiko dachi of her youth, and her kicks rarely rose above knee height. But when she performed Sanchin, the room went quiet, and when you stood in front of her for kakie, you found yourself being moved about with an ease that was faintly humiliating. I once asked her what she was working on. I expected her to say something about a particular kata or technique. Instead she said, "The same things as you. Just more slowly."

I have thought about that answer many times since. In most areas of life, progress means moving on to new material. In Goju-Ryu, after a certain point, there is no new material. You have learned all the kata. What remains is to go deeper into the ones you know: to understand them more completely, to perform them with more quality, to apply them more effectively, and to keep doing so as your body changes. That is not a lesser kind of progress. It is the main kind.

## The shape of the system

It helps to have a picture of how the parts of Goju-Ryu fit together. The one I use is a wheel with five spokes.

At the hub is **Sanchin**, the source of structure, breath and concentration. Every other part of the system depends on it.

The five spokes are:

**Tensho**, which develops the soft, adhesive hand and the circular movement that balance Sanchin's hardness.

**The kaishu kata**, which apply the Sanchin body to the problems of fighting, each in its own way.

**Hojo undo**, which builds the strength and resilience the kata require.

**Kakie**, which tests and develops sensitivity, structure and close-range skill against a live partner.

**Bunkai and partner training**, which turn the kata's movements into usable fighting skills.

The rim of the wheel is your daily practice, which connects all five spokes. If any spoke is missing, the wheel is lopsided. A student who practises kata but never kakie has a beautiful form with no feeling for an opponent. A student who practises kakie but neglects Sanchin has sensitivity without structure. A student who does hojo undo without kata has strength without skill.

## The kata as a family

The eight classical kata are not a random collection. They form a family, in which each member has its own character and teaches its own lesson, while sharing features with the others. Seeing them as a family helps you to remember them, to understand them and to practise them in a balanced way.

| Kata | Central lesson | Key quality | Closest relatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saifa | Escaping from a grip | Whole-body turning | Seisan, Sepai |
| Seiyunchin | Pulling and unbalancing | Grounded drop | Saifa, Suparinpei |
| Shisochin | Pressing and turning | Shoulder power | Suparinpei, Kururunfa |
| Sanseru | Attacking the legs | Hand and foot coordination | Seisan |
| Sepai | Redirecting with circles | Deception | Saifa, Kururunfa |
| Kururunfa | Timing | Stillness and explosion | Shisochin, Sepai |
| Seisan | Fighting very close | Short power | Sanseru, Sanchin |
| Suparinpei | Bringing it together | Endurance and integration | All |

: **Table 15.1** The classical kata as a family.

One useful exercise is to take a principle and trace it through all the kata. Take the drop, for example. Where does it appear in Saifa? In Seiyunchin? In Kururunfa? How does it differ in each? Or take the grip. Which kata grip with one hand, which with two? Which grip and pull, which grip and push, which grip and turn? Tracing principles in this way reveals connections that are easy to miss when you study each kata on its own.

## Keeping many kata alive

Once you know five, six or all eight classical kata, a practical problem arises: how do you keep them all in good condition? If you practise each one thoroughly at every session, you will have time for nothing else. If you practise only the one you are currently working on, the others will decay.

The answer is rotation. Divide your kata into groups and give each group a focus day, while keeping a light touch on the others.

| Day | Daily core | Focus kata | Light review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sanchin, Tensho | Saifa, Seiyunchin | One run each of all others |
| 2 | Sanchin, Tensho | Shisochin, Sanseru | Hojo undo |
| 3 | Sanchin, Tensho | Sepai, Kururunfa | Kakie |
| 4 | Sanchin, Tensho | Seisan, Suparinpei | One run each of all others |
| 5 | Sanchin, Tensho | Current study kata | Bunkai with a partner |

: **Table 15.2** A five-day rotation for maintaining all the classical kata.

On a focus day, the focus kata receive serious attention: group-by-group work, slow and fast performances, application practice. The other kata receive a single run each, enough to keep the sequence fresh. Over the course of a week, every kata receives at least one serious session.

Adjust the rotation to your own circumstances. If you train three times a week rather than five, spread the focus kata over a fortnight. If you are preparing for a grading, give the grading kata extra focus days. The principle is the same: rotate the focus, keep everything alive.

## The structure of a session

A good training session, whether in the dojo or alone, has a shape. Here is the one I use for a ninety-minute session, which can be shortened or extended as needed.

**Preparation (10–15 minutes).** Junbi undo: the warming and loosening exercises from the first volume. Do not skip them, however short of time you are.

**The heishu kata (10 minutes).** Sanchin, three times, at least once tested. Tensho, twice. These set the structure and the breath for everything that follows.

**Basics (10 minutes).** A short selection of basic techniques, stances and transitions, chosen to support the day's focus kata. If the focus is Seiyunchin, for example, work on shiko dachi and the drop.

**Focus kata (20–25 minutes).** Group-by-group work, slow and fast performances, and corrections.

**Partner work (20 minutes).** Kakie, followed by application drills from the focus kata.

**Hojo undo (10 minutes).** A short selection of conditioning exercises, two or three times a week.

**Closing (5 minutes).** One final performance of the focus kata, or of Sanchin, followed by a few minutes of quiet breathing.

## The long view: phases of training

Training after first dan tends to fall into phases. These are not formal stages, and they differ from person to person, but recognising them can help you make sense of your progress.

### From first to third dan: acquisition

In the first years after first dan, most students are still acquiring kata. They learn Shisochin, Sanseru, Sepai and perhaps Kururunfa, each over a period of a year or more. The main work is learning sequences, cleaning up shapes and beginning to understand applications. Progress is relatively visible, because each new kata is a measurable step.

The danger of this phase is that acquisition becomes the goal. Students who see their progress only in terms of new kata may neglect the quality of the old ones, and may become frustrated when there are no new kata left to learn.

### From third to fifth dan: integration

In the middle years, the last kata are learned and the emphasis shifts to integration. The student begins to see the connections between the kata, to understand the principles that run through them, and to apply those principles freely. Kakie and application work become more important. Teaching often begins in this phase, and teaching is one of the best ways to deepen understanding.

The danger of this phase is complacency. Having learned everything, some students stop looking for anything new. Their kata become fixed, their applications become a set repertoire, and their progress stops.

### Beyond fifth dan: refinement

In the later years, progress is a matter of refinement. The student keeps working on the same kata, the same principles, the same drills, but with increasing subtlety. Small details that were invisible before become clear. Techniques become simpler and more efficient. Power comes more from structure and timing and less from strength.

This is the phase my teacher in her seventies was in. It is, in many ways, the most interesting phase of all, and it can last for decades.

## Training through age and injury

Every karateka who trains long enough will face the effects of age, and most will face injury at some point. How you respond to these will largely determine whether you continue to train and improve, or give up.

### Adapting the stances

The most common adaptation is to the depth of stances. A deep shiko dachi or a low neko ashi dachi places considerable strain on the knees and hips, and as the body ages, or after a knee or hip injury, it may become painful or impossible. The answer is not to abandon these stances but to raise them, keeping the correct alignment and the correct structure at a higher level.

A higher shiko dachi with correct knee alignment is far better than a lower one with collapsing knees. The principles of the stance, the outward pressure of the knees, the neutral pelvis, the upright back, the evenly divided weight, can all be maintained at any height. The power of the stance comes from its structure, not its depth.

### Adapting the tension

As the body ages, high levels of tension and breath pressure become riskier, particularly for anyone with high blood pressure or heart problems. Sanchin can and should continue to be practised, but with lower levels of tension and with particular attention to continuous breathing. Many older teachers perform a Sanchin that looks almost relaxed from the outside, but whose structure, when tested, is astonishingly solid. This is not a lesser Sanchin. In many ways it is a better one.

### Adapting the kicks

High kicks are rarely necessary in Goju-Ryu, and most of the kicks in the classical kata are low. As flexibility decreases, kicks can be lowered further without changing their application. A front kick to the groin or knee is as effective at fifty as it was at twenty.

### Training around injury

When you are injured, train what you can. A knee injury does not prevent you from practising Tensho, the hand techniques of the kata or kakie from a high stance. A shoulder injury does not prevent you from practising stances, transitions and kicks. A wrist injury does not prevent you from practising Sanchin with open hands or Seiyunchin's stance work.

Always follow medical advice, and never train through pain that feels wrong. But do not stop training altogether unless you must. Injury is often a chance to work on aspects of your karate that you have neglected.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Training against the clock**

*The fault.* You are frustrated that you can no longer perform your kata as you did ten or twenty years ago. You push yourself to match your younger self and keep getting injured.

*The cause.* Measuring your karate by its external form rather than its quality.

*The correction.* Stop comparing your current kata with your past kata. Compare your current understanding with your past understanding. Raise your stances, lower your kicks, reduce your tension, and concentrate on structure, timing and sensitivity. These can continue to improve long after speed and flexibility have begun to decline.
:::

## Gradings beyond first dan

Gradings beyond first dan differ from earlier gradings. The examiners are less interested in whether you can perform the sequence correctly, which they take for granted, and more interested in the quality of your performance and the depth of your understanding.

Specifically, they are usually looking for the following.

**Structure.** Does your Sanchin hold up under testing? Do your stances keep their shape throughout the kata, including on the turns?

**Rhythm.** Does each kata have its proper rhythm, with slow and fast passages, pauses and explosions, in the right places?

**Breath.** Is your breathing controlled and appropriate to each section?

**Intent.** Do your techniques look as though they are directed at a real opponent, or are they shapes in the air?

**Application.** Can you demonstrate convincing applications of the kata's movements, against a partner who offers some resistance?

**Understanding.** Can you explain what you are doing and why? Many associations ask higher-grade candidates questions about the history, principles and applications of the kata.

If you prepare for gradings with these criteria in mind, rather than simply polishing your sequences, you will be preparing in the right way.

## The training notebook

I have mentioned the training notebook several times in this book. I want to make a final case for it here, because I believe it is one of the most valuable tools a serious karateka can have.

A training notebook is simply a record of what you have practised, what you have noticed, what corrections you have received and what questions you have. It need not be elaborate. A few lines after each session are enough.

Over months and years, a notebook becomes a map of your training. It shows you which faults keep returning, which corrections have worked and which have not, which kata you have neglected and which you have overworked. It records the insights that come to you in the middle of training and would otherwise be forgotten. And it shows you how far you have come, which is encouraging on the days when it seems you have not come far at all.

::: drill
**Drill: The notebook habit**

After every training session for the next three months, write down three things: one thing you practised, one thing you noticed, and one question. At the end of each month, read back through the entries and look for patterns. Which faults appear most often? Which questions have you answered? Which remain?
:::

## Teaching

Finally, a word about teaching. Most students who train seriously beyond first dan will, at some point, begin to help teach others. Teaching is a responsibility, but it is also one of the most powerful ways of improving your own karate.

When you teach, you have to explain what you do. In explaining it, you discover what you do not understand. When you correct a student's fault, you often recognise the same fault in yourself. When a student asks a question you cannot answer, you have to go away and find the answer. Every teacher I know says that they learned more in their first few years of teaching than in any comparable period of their own training.

If you have the opportunity to teach, take it. Begin with beginners, who will ask the most basic and therefore the most searching questions. Teach the things you know well, and be honest about the things you do not. And keep training yourself. A teacher who stops training stops improving, and a teacher who stops improving has less and less to offer.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- After the last kata is learned, progress means going deeper into the kata you know.
- Goju-Ryu can be pictured as a wheel: Sanchin at the hub, with Tensho, the kaishu kata, hojo undo, kakie and bunkai as the spokes.
- The classical kata form a family. Tracing principles through all of them reveals their connections.
- Use a rotation to keep many kata alive, with a focus group each day and a light review of the others.
- Structure each session: preparation, heishu kata, basics, focus kata, partner work, hojo undo, closing.
- Training after first dan moves through phases of acquisition, integration and refinement.
- Adapt stances, tension and kicks to age and injury while keeping the principles intact.
- Higher gradings look for structure, rhythm, breath, intent, application and understanding.
- Keep a training notebook, and teach when you have the opportunity.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Draw your own version of the training wheel and mark which spokes you currently neglect.
2. Choose one principle, such as the drop, the grip or the turn, and trace it through every kata you know. Record your findings.
3. Design a rotation for the kata you currently know and follow it for a month.
4. Structure each of your sessions for a month according to the template in this chapter, and note any differences in your progress.
5. Perform one kata with raised stances and reduced tension, concentrating only on structure and timing. Note what you discover.
6. Keep a training notebook for three months, using the notebook habit drill.
:::

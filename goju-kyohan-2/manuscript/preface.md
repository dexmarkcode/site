# Preface

There is a particular kind of student I have met in almost every dojo I have visited. They have trained for four or five years. They hold a brown belt, or a new black one. They can perform Gekisai Dai Ichi and Dai Ni cleanly, their Sanchin is respectable, and they have learned Saifa well enough to pass a grading. They are fit, committed and honest. And somewhere around this point, quite quietly, they begin to stall.

They do not stop coming to class. They do not lose interest. What happens is subtler than that. The new kata start to blur together. Seiyunchin feels like a long series of shapes rather than a fighting method. Shisochin's open hands seem soft and vaguely pointless. Their Sanchin testing produces the same corrections, week after week, and they no longer know what to do with them. When the instructor asks for an application, they offer the one they were shown at their last grading, and then they run out.

This book was written for that student. If you recognise yourself in the description, you are in good company. I was that student for longer than I care to admit.

The first volume of this textbook dealt with the ground floor of Goju-Ryu: the etiquette of the dojo, the basic stances, the fundamental blocks and strikes, the preparatory exercises, the two Gekisai kata, and the first serious encounter with Sanchin. It assumed you knew nothing. This volume assumes you know all of that and are ready to be asked harder questions. It deals with the eight classical *kaishu* kata, the open-hand forms that carry the fighting knowledge of the system; with the tension and breathing work of Sanchin and Tensho in much greater depth; with the traditional supplementary training of *hojo undo*; and with the classical applications that give the kata their purpose.

## Why a problem-solving textbook

Most karate books are written as descriptions. They tell you what the stance is, what the kata looks like and what the movement is called. That is useful, and there is some of it here, but it is not what stalls an intermediate student. You already know what the stance is supposed to look like. Your problem is that yours doesn't, and you are not sure why.

So I have organised this book around problems. Every chapter identifies the faults that most commonly appear at its stage of training, explains what causes them and sets out practical corrections. Many of these appear in boxed panels marked *Fault and Correction*, so that you can find them again quickly when you need them. There are drills that isolate a single quality, practice tasks at the end of each chapter, and a troubleshooting index at the back of the book that lets you start from the symptom you have noticed and work back to its cause.

This approach reflects how I teach. After a good many years on the dojo floor, I have come to believe that the most useful thing a teacher can do is not to demonstrate perfection but to diagnose imperfection accurately. A student who understands why their shoulders rise in Sanchin can fix it. A student who is simply told "shoulders down" for the hundredth time will press them down for three breaths and then let them creep up again.

## On the drawings

The subtitle promises more than eight hundred line drawings, and the book contains them. They are arranged in numbered sequences, so that a single figure may hold eight or twelve separate drawings showing the stages of a movement, or the same movement seen from two angles, or the right and wrong versions of a position side by side.

They are deliberately plain. A photograph shows you one person on one day, with every crease of their uniform and every quirk of their body. A clean line drawing shows you the shape you are aiming for and nothing else. Where an arrow appears, it shows the direction of travel. Where a broken line appears, it shows a previous position or a path. Floor diagrams are seen from above, with the starting point marked by a small circle.

Drawings have limits, too. They are flat, and karate is not. They cannot show breath, timing or the quality of tension, which are the very things that matter most in Goju-Ryu. Use them as maps, not as the territory. The territory is your own body, on the floor, under the eye of a good teacher.

## On lineage and respect

Goju-Ryu was organised by Chojun Miyagi in the 1920s and 1930s from the art he learned from Kanryo Higaonna in Naha, together with what he studied in southern China and what he developed himself. After his death in 1953 his students carried the art in several directions. The Okinawan lines associated with Eiichi Miyazato, Seiko Higa, Meitoku Yagi, Seikichi Toguchi and others, the international organisation built by Morio Higaonna, and the Japanese branch developed by Gogen Yamaguchi all preserve the same core kata, but they differ, sometimes noticeably, in the details.

I have no wish to declare one branch correct and the others mistaken. Where this book describes a kata sequence, it follows a widely practised Okinawan form, and I have tried to note the places where you are most likely to see differences. If your teacher does something differently, do it your teacher's way. The principles in this book apply to all of them. That is rather the point of principles.

## A word of caution

Some of the training described here is demanding. Sanchin testing, conditioning with weighted implements, sustained breath control and partner work involving locks and throws can all injure you if they are approached carelessly or too quickly. Read the notice at the front of the book. Build up gradually. Train with people you trust. And if something hurts in a way that feels wrong rather than merely hard, stop and find out why before you continue.

The old teachers in Okinawa trained for decades and many of them lived into their eighties and nineties. They did not do it by being reckless. They did it by being patient.

I owe thanks to my own teachers, who corrected me with more patience than I deserved; to the students who have trained with me over the years, whose questions shaped almost every chapter; and to the training partners who allowed themselves to be locked, thrown and pushed around so that the applications in Part Six could be tested rather than imagined. Any errors that remain are mine.

Now tighten your belt, find a clear patch of floor and let us begin.

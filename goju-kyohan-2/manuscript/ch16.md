# The Rules of Analysis: Reading Kata as a Fighting Manual

::: chapter-intro
A kata is a record of fighting knowledge written in movement. To read it, you need to know the conventions in which it was written. This chapter sets out the principles that Goju-Ryu teachers have used to analyse the classical kata, explains each one with examples, shows how to move from analysis to working application, and warns against the most common errors of interpretation.
:::

Imagine you have found a book written in a language you half know. Most of the words are familiar, but some of the grammar is strange, and certain sentences seem to make no sense. You could guess at their meaning, and some of your guesses might be right. But you would make much faster progress if someone gave you the grammar: the rules by which the language was put together.

The classical kata are like that book. You know most of the techniques in them. But the way they are put together, the order, the directions, the odd shapes, the repetitions, follows conventions that are not obvious from the outside. Without those conventions, interpreting the kata is guesswork. With them, it becomes a disciplined study.

This chapter is about those conventions. In Okinawan karate they are sometimes gathered under the name *kaisai no genri*, "the theory of unlocking the secrets", a phrase that became widely used in the twentieth century. Different teachers state the principles in different ways, and there is no single authoritative list. What follows is my own synthesis of the principles most commonly taught in Goju-Ryu, with my comments on each.

## Why kata need interpreting

It is fair to ask why kata need interpreting at all. If they were made to record fighting techniques, why not record them clearly?

There are several answers. One is that kata were made for students who would learn them from a teacher, who would explain the applications in person. The kata itself was a memory aid, not a textbook. When the chain of teachers was broken, or when kata were taught to large groups without their applications, the meanings were lost and had to be reconstructed.

A second answer is that a single movement can serve many purposes. A kata that recorded every application separately would be impossibly long. By recording a general movement, the kata leaves room for many specific applications, which the student discovers through practice.

A third answer is that kata are performed without an opponent, and a technique performed in the air looks different from the same technique performed on a person. A throw without anyone to throw looks like a dance. A grip without anyone to grip looks like a strange hand gesture. Interpreting the kata means putting the opponent back.

## Principle one: do not be deceived by the floor pattern

The embusen, the floor pattern of the kata, is designed to fit the kata into a practice space and to return the performer to their starting point. It is not a map of where the opponents are.

When a kata turns to the right, this does not necessarily mean that a new opponent has appeared on the right. More often, it means that the performer has changed their angle relative to the same opponent, perhaps by stepping to the side of an attack or by turning to throw. When a kata moves forward three steps, this does not necessarily mean that the performer advances three steps against an opponent. It may mean that a single technique is being practised three times.

This is one of the most important principles, because the assumption that each direction represents a new opponent leads to some of the least convincing applications. A kata that turned to face a new opponent with every change of direction would describe a fight against a dozen people, one after another, each politely waiting their turn. That is not what the kata are about.

## Principle two: there is one opponent

As a general rule, the techniques in the classical kata are directed at a single opponent. This follows from the first principle. The changes of direction represent changes of angle, not new attackers.

This does not mean that kata have nothing to say about multiple attackers. The principles of moving off the line, of using one opponent as a shield, of finishing quickly, are all relevant. But the specific sequences of the kata are best understood as dealing with one person at a time, and usually with one person throughout a movement group.

## Principle three: every movement has a purpose

Nothing in a classical kata is decorative. Every movement, including the ones that look strange, ceremonial or merely transitional, has a purpose. If you cannot see the purpose, the fault is in your understanding, not in the kata.

This principle is a working assumption rather than a proven fact. It is possible that some movements in some kata have lost their original purpose or have been altered in transmission. But treating every movement as purposeful is the right way to study, because it forces you to look harder. Many of the most useful applications in Goju-Ryu were found by teachers who refused to accept that a movement was "just for show".

## Principle four: the returning hand has a job

In basic karate, the hand that draws back to the hip as the other hand punches, *hikite*, is often explained as a way of adding power to the punch, by the reaction of pulling one side back as the other goes forward. That explanation is not wrong, but it is incomplete.

In the classical kata, the returning hand is almost always doing something: holding, pulling, trapping or controlling part of the opponent's body. When it draws back to the hip, it is usually pulling the opponent's arm, clothing or head towards you, onto your strike or off balance. When it is held in a particular position during another technique, it is usually pinning or controlling something.

When you analyse a movement, always ask what the returning or passive hand is doing. The answer often transforms the application.

## Principle five: shapes are not always what they seem

A closed fist does not always mean a punch. It may mean a grip, since a hand that is holding something forms a fist. An open hand does not always mean a strike or a block. It may mean a grip, a push, a guide or a check. A block does not always mean a block. It may mean a strike to the attacking limb, a lock, a pull or a release.

In the same way, a movement labelled as a block in the dojo, such as chudan uke or gedan barai, may in application be something quite different. Gedan barai, the low sweeping block, can be a strike to the arm, a release from a wrist grip, a pull that drags the opponent down, or a hammer-fist strike to the groin or the thigh. The label tells you what the movement is called in basic training. It does not limit what it can do.

(4) As a release from a wrist grip. (5) As a pull, dragging the partner's arm down. (6–8) As an arm bar: the forearm across the back of the partner's elbow, driving them down.](figures/fig16_3.png)

## Principle six: the range is close

Most of the techniques in the classical kata are designed for close range: the range at which you can touch your opponent without stepping. This follows from the nature of the techniques themselves. Grips, pulls, elbows, knees, joint kicks, short punches and dropping strikes all work best, or only work, at close range.

When analysing a movement, assume close range unless there is a good reason not to. An application that requires the opponent to stand at a distance, throwing long punches and waiting to be blocked, is usually less convincing than one that places them close, grabbing or pushing or striking at short range.

## Principle seven: kata start with defence

There is a well-known saying in Okinawan karate, *karate ni sente nashi*, often translated as "there is no first attack in karate". In the kata, this is reflected in the fact that almost every classical kata begins with a movement that can be interpreted as defensive: a block, a release, an evasion.

This principle helps you to read the opening of a kata. The first movement of each group is usually a response to something the opponent has done: a grab, a push, a strike. The movements that follow are the defender's continuation, taking control of the situation.

## Principle eight: the end of a sequence is a finish

Where a movement group ends with a strong technique, especially one accompanied by a sharp exhalation or a kiai, this usually marks the finishing technique of the sequence: the strike, lock or throw that ends the exchange. The movements before it are the set-up.

This principle helps you to identify which movements are preparatory and which are decisive. It also helps you to perform the kata with the right emphasis.

## From analysis to application

Knowing the principles is one thing. Turning them into working applications is another. The process I teach has four steps.

### Step one: isolate the movement

Choose a single movement or a short group of movements. Perform it slowly, several times, paying attention to exactly what each hand, each foot and the body are doing. Note the direction of each movement, the shape of each hand, the stance and the breathing.

### Step two: put the opponent back

Ask: if there were a person in front of me, what would this movement do to them? Consider each hand separately. What could it be holding? What could it be striking? Where would the opponent need to be for this to make sense? Use the principles: assume one opponent, close range, a purpose for every movement, a job for the returning hand.

### Step three: test it with a partner

Try your interpretation with a cooperative partner, slowly. Does it work? Does it make physical sense? Are there awkward moments where you have to cheat, by changing the movement or asking your partner to do something unrealistic? If so, adjust the interpretation or try another.

### Step four: test it under pressure

When an application works cooperatively, test it with increasing resistance, as described in Chapter 18. Many applications that work beautifully in cooperation fail under pressure. Those that survive are the ones worth keeping.

## Levels of interpretation

Many teachers distinguish between levels of interpretation, often using the terms *bunkai*, *oyo* and *henka*.

**Bunkai**, meaning "analysis" or "taking apart", refers to the basic interpretation of a movement, the one that follows the kata's shape most closely. It is usually the first application a student learns.

**Oyo**, meaning "application", refers to the practical use of the movement in a realistic situation. It may depart somewhat from the exact shape of the kata, adapting it to the demands of a resisting opponent.

**Henka**, meaning "variation", refers to the different ways a movement can be adapted to different circumstances: a different attack, a different angle, a different body size.

Some teachers also speak of surface and hidden interpretations, *omote* and *ura*, or of a progression from the obvious to the secret. However the levels are named, the point is the same. The first interpretation of a movement is rarely the only one, and it is often not the most useful. Study goes deeper.

## Common errors of interpretation

### The block-punch habit

The commonest error is to interpret every movement as a block or a punch against a long-range attack. The partner steps in with a lunge punch; the defender blocks; the defender punches. This is how many students first learn applications, and it has some value for beginners. But it ignores most of what the classical kata contain: the grips, pulls, locks, throws and close-range strikes.

### The multiple-opponent fantasy

The second error, already discussed, is to treat each change of direction as a new opponent. This produces sequences in which the defender blocks one attacker, turns to block another, turns again to block a third, without ever finishing any of them.

### The impossible application

The third error is to invent applications that work only with a compliant partner, or that require the opponent to do something strange. If your application requires the opponent to leave their arm extended for five seconds while you perform four techniques on it, it is not a useful application.

### The magic technique

The fourth error is to attribute extraordinary effects to small movements: a touch to a particular point that causes collapse, a twist that disables instantly. Some techniques in the kata do target vulnerable areas, and some joint locks are very effective. But claims of near-magical effect should be treated with great caution and tested honestly.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The cooperative illusion**

*The fault.* Your applications look excellent in the dojo, but when a partner resists even slightly, they fall apart.

*The cause.* You have developed your applications entirely in cooperation, and they depend on the partner doing exactly what you expect.

*The correction.* Introduce resistance gradually, as described in Chapter 18. Ask your partner to resist at a level of ten per cent, then twenty, then thirty. Note where your application fails and adjust it. Keep only the applications that work against at least moderate resistance.
:::

## A worked example

To bring the principles together, let us analyse a single movement in detail: the elbow into palm of Seiyunchin's second group.

*Isolate.* In the kata, one hand is extended forward, open, while the other arm swings round horizontally and the elbow strikes into the open palm. The body drops into shiko dachi at the moment of impact.

*Apply the principles.* There is one opponent. The range is close. The movement has a purpose. The open hand is not passive: it has a job. The shape may not be what it seems: the palm is not a target but is holding something.

*Put the opponent back.* What could the open hand be holding? The back of the opponent's head, perhaps, or their upper arm, or their wrist. What could the elbow be striking? If the hand holds the head, the elbow strikes the jaw or temple. If the hand holds the upper arm, the elbow strikes the back of the arm, above the elbow, creating an arm bar. If the hand holds the wrist, the forearm strikes across the elbow joint.

*Test it.* With a partner, try each. The head version works as a strike but is dangerous to practise. The upper-arm and wrist versions work well as arm bars, especially with the drop into shiko dachi, which drives the partner down.

*Test under pressure.* With increasing resistance, the wrist version proves more reliable, because the wrist is easier to secure than the upper arm. The drop is essential; without it, a strong partner can resist.

*Conclusion.* The most reliable application of this movement is an arm bar, with the open hand gripping the partner's wrist and the forearm or elbow striking or pressing against the back of the arm above the elbow, as the body drops into shiko dachi. This matches the application given in Chapter 8.

(3) The hand on the partner's upper arm; the elbow to the back of the arm. (4) The hand on the partner's wrist; the forearm across the elbow. ::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Kata are records of fighting knowledge written in movement, and they need interpreting according to their own conventions.
- Do not be deceived by the floor pattern. Changes of direction usually represent changes of angle, not new opponents.
- Assume one opponent, close range and a purpose for every movement.
- The returning hand almost always has a job: holding, pulling, trapping or controlling.
- Shapes are not always what they seem. Blocks may be strikes, grips or locks; fists may be grips.
- Kata start with defence and end each sequence with a finishing technique.
- Move from analysis to application in four steps: isolate, put the opponent back, test cooperatively, test under pressure.
- Avoid the block-punch habit, the multiple-opponent fantasy, the impossible application and the magic technique.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Choose one movement from each kata you know. Apply the four-step process to each and record the results in your notebook.
2. Go through one kata and, for every movement, ask what the returning or passive hand is doing. Record at least one possibility for each.
3. Take gedan barai and find at least four different applications. Test each with a partner.
4. Choose one turn in a kata you know and work out an application in which it represents a change of angle against the same opponent.
5. Test three of your favourite applications against a partner offering moderate resistance. Keep those that survive and adjust those that do not.
6. Work through the worked example of this chapter with a partner, testing each version.
:::

# Seiyunchin: Control, Pull and the Long Stance

::: chapter-intro
Seiyunchin contains no kicks. Its power comes from the ground, through long periods in a low shiko dachi, and its techniques pull, drag, lift and unbalance rather than simply strike. It is the kata in which many students first realise that Goju-Ryu is as much a grappling art as a striking one. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, shows how its long stance is built and used, works through its faults, and explains its principal applications.
:::

When I first learned Seiyunchin, I hated the opening. It seemed to go on forever. You step into a deep shiko dachi, raise your hands slowly, lower them slowly, pull, push and pull again, all the while sinking lower into a stance that grows more painful by the second. And then you do it again on the other side. And then you do it a third time. By the end of the opening my thighs were shaking, my shoulders had crept up round my ears and I had long since stopped thinking about anything except getting to the next bit.

A few years later, I was training with a partner who was considerably bigger and stronger than me. We were working on escapes from a two-handed grip on the jacket, and he had a very good grip. Nothing I tried was working. Then, almost without meaning to, I dropped into shiko dachi, as low as I could go, and let my arms rise between his in the way the opening of Seiyunchin had taught me. His grip simply came apart. He looked at his hands as though they had betrayed him. I had the opposite thought. For the first time, I understood why I had spent all those minutes in that miserable stance.

## The character of Seiyunchin

The name Seiyunchin, also written Seienchin, is usually interpreted as meaning "control, pull and fight", or sometimes "calm before the storm" or "attack, conquer and suppress". The first interpretation is the most useful, because it describes what the kata actually does. Its techniques control an opponent, usually through grips on the arms or clothing; pull them off balance, usually by dropping the body weight into a low stance; and then fight, by striking, locking or throwing them while they are unbalanced.

Seiyunchin is unusual among the classical kata in having no kicks. Some teachers explain this by saying that the kata was designed for situations in which kicking is impossible or dangerous, such as in a crowd or on uneven ground. Others say that the absence of kicks forces the performer to rely entirely on the stance and the upper body, and so develops these qualities more strongly. Whatever the reason, the effect is that Seiyunchin is an intensely grounded kata. Almost all of its power comes from the legs pressing into the floor.

It is also, in places, a slow kata. Its opening group is performed with deliberate, controlled movements and deep breathing, and in some lineages with considerable tension. Students who are used to the explosive rhythm of Saifa often find this frustrating. But the slowness is the point. It trains the strength and endurance of the stance and the patience to apply force gradually, which is exactly what is needed when you are trying to break a strong grip or move a heavy opponent.

## The long shiko dachi

Before looking at the sequence, it is worth spending some time on the stance that dominates it. Seiyunchin's shiko dachi is often described as "long", meaning that it is held for long periods rather than that it is unusually wide. It must be strong enough to resist being pulled and pushed, low enough to drop the body weight effectively, and enduring enough to be held through slow movements without collapsing.

Everything I said about shiko dachi in Chapter 3 applies here with extra force. The knees must stay over the feet. The pelvis must be neutral. The back must be upright. The weight must be evenly divided. And, because you will be in the stance for a long time, you must be able to maintain all of this while breathing deeply and moving your arms through large, slow movements.

::: drill
**Drill: The shiko dachi ladder**

This drill builds the endurance needed for Seiyunchin. Stand in shiko dachi with your hands on your hips. Hold the stance for thirty seconds, breathing slowly, checking your knees, pelvis and back. Rest for thirty seconds. Repeat, holding for forty-five seconds. Rest. Repeat for sixty seconds. Rest. Then come back down the ladder: forty-five seconds, then thirty.

Do this three times a week. When you can complete the ladder with good form, extend the top rung to ninety seconds, then two minutes. When you can hold shiko dachi for two minutes with good form, add slow arm movements from the opening of Seiyunchin during the holds.
:::

## Group one: the three openings

The kata begins with its longest and most characteristic group, performed three times: to the right diagonal, to the left diagonal and to the right diagonal again.

From the ready position, step out with the right foot to the front-right diagonal and settle into shiko dachi, facing that diagonal. As you step, the hands, crossed low in front of the body, rise slowly together up the centre line, the wrists crossed, until they reach about chest height. They then separate outward, the forearms rotating, in a wide, wedging movement, finishing with the hands at about shoulder height and shoulder width, palms or fists facing forward. This is performed slowly, with a long inhalation and then a strong, controlled exhalation.

From this position, one hand, usually the left, sweeps downward in a low block across the front of the body, while the other draws back to the hip. Then the right hand performs a low open-hand thrust, sometimes described as a scooping spear hand, forward and down, as the left hand returns to cover it or to rest near the right elbow. Finally the hands pull back together, as if dragging something towards you, to finish the group.

The details of the hand movements in the opening group vary considerably between lineages. What is common to them all is the shape of the group: a slow rise and opening of the hands from a low centre, a low block, a low thrust, and a pull, all performed in a deep, unmoving shiko dachi.

The group is then repeated to the left diagonal and once more to the right.

### The wedge

The rising and opening movement at the start of the group is the key to its most important application, the release from a two-handed grip described at the start of this chapter. When an opponent grips your jacket with both hands, their arms form a frame in front of you. If you try to pull their hands off from the outside, you are fighting the strongest part of the frame. If you drive your own arms up through the middle of the frame and then outward, while dropping your body weight, you attack its weakest point. The combination of the rising wedge and the drop breaks the grip, often very suddenly.

The point to understand is that the drop does most of the work. The arms provide the wedge; the stance provides the force. A student who performs the opening with a high stance and strong arms will find that the release fails against a strong grip. A student who performs it with relaxed arms and a deep, sudden drop will find that it works against almost anyone.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Rising out of the stance**

*The fault.* As your hands rise in the opening, your body rises with them, so that by the time your hands reach their highest point you are standing several inches taller.

*The cause.* An unconscious linkage between the arms and the legs. When the hands go up, the body follows.

*The correction.* Practise the rising and opening movement with a partner resting their hands lightly on your shoulders. They should feel no upward movement at all. Better still, try to sink slightly as your hands rise, so that the two movements oppose each other. This is exactly what the application needs.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The collapsing hold**

*The fault.* By the third opening, your shiko dachi has become higher and narrower, and your knees are falling inward.

*The cause.* Fatigue, and a stance that is not yet strong enough to be held for the length of the group.

*The correction.* Work on the shiko dachi ladder until your stance endurance improves. In the meantime, perform the kata with a slightly higher stance, but with correct knee alignment, rather than a low stance that collapses. Depth will come with endurance; alignment must come first.
:::

## Group two: the elbow and back-fist

After the third opening, the kata turns to face the front, or in some versions the side, and moves into a group of strikes, again in shiko dachi.

The characteristic technique of this group is an elbow strike into the open palm of the other hand. From a position with one hand extended forward, open, the other arm swings round in a horizontal arc and the elbow strikes into the waiting palm. The movement is performed with a drop into shiko dachi and a sharp exhalation.

The elbow strike is usually followed by a back-fist strike with the same arm, travelling forward and upward from the elbow, and then by a low block or a hammer-fist downward.

The group is then repeated on the other side after a turn.

(8) The back-fist on the other side.](figures/fig8_3.png)

### The palm is not a target

The open palm into which the elbow strikes is not, of course, a target. It represents something you are holding, most often the opponent's head, neck or arm. When you perform the elbow strike into the palm, imagine that your hand has caught the back of an opponent's head, or the back of their upper arm, and is pulling it into your elbow. The palm should draw towards the elbow at the moment of impact, not wait passively for the elbow to arrive.

This changes the feel of the movement entirely. Instead of a single strike, it becomes a pull and a strike meeting in the middle, which is much more powerful and much more realistic.

## Group three: the forward pull

The middle of the kata moves forward along the main line. In most versions, this group contains a stepping sequence in which one hand reaches forward, hooks or grips, and pulls back to the hip while the body advances, followed by a strike or a low block with the other hand.

This is the "pull" of control, pull and fight in its plainest form. The pulling hand draws an opponent onto your strike, or off balance, while your advancing body closes the distance. The pull and the advance happen together, so that the opponent is caught between two forces moving in opposite directions.

## Group four: the lifting movement

Later in the kata comes one of its most distinctive movements, which in many lineages is interpreted as a lifting throw. From shiko dachi, one hand reaches low, often between or behind the legs of an imagined opponent, while the other arm wraps high. The body then rises and turns, the low hand lifting and the high hand pulling down, so that the opponent is tipped over the hip or shoulder.

In the kata, this movement is usually performed as a low scooping action with one hand and a high sweeping action with the other, followed by a turn and a drop. It is easy to perform it as a pair of vague arm movements. It is much harder, and much more useful, to perform it with the clear intention of lifting and turning a heavy weight.

(4) The low reach with the right hand in shiko dachi, the left arm high. (5) The lift begins, the body rising. (6) The turn, the low hand lifting and the high hand pulling down. (7) The drop back into shiko dachi. (8) The low strike to finish.](figures/fig8_4.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The weightless lift**

*The fault.* The lifting movement looks like a graceful arm gesture, with no sense of weight.

*The cause.* You are not imagining a real opponent, and so your body does not prepare to lift.

*The correction.* Practise the movement with a heavy bag or a cooperative partner, slowly and carefully. Feel how your stance must lower and your back must engage before you can lift anything. Then return to the kata and try to preserve the same preparation and the same effort, even though there is nothing in your arms.
:::

## Group five: the close

The final group of Seiyunchin, in many versions, includes a section in which the hands perform a series of low strikes or blocks from shiko dachi, followed by a step back into neko ashi dachi and the closing mawashi uke. Some versions include a brief sequence in which the arms circle slowly at shoulder height, echoing the opening, before the final drop.

As always, the closing mawashi uke should receive full attention. After the long, heavy work of the kata, it is tempting to hurry it. Do not.

## Breathing in Seiyunchin

Seiyunchin's breathing is more varied than that of Saifa. The opening group is usually performed with deep, slow breathing, close to the ibuki of Sanchin, with long inhalations as the hands rise and long exhalations as they open and as the low block and thrust are performed. The striking groups use short, sharp exhalations, as in Saifa. The lifting movement is usually performed on a single sustained exhalation.

A common fault is to carry the slow breathing of the opening into the striking groups, so that the strikes become sluggish. Another is to rush the breathing of the opening, so that the group becomes shallow and hurried. Pay attention to the change of breathing between groups; it is one of the clearest ways of marking the change of rhythm.

## Applications

### The release from a two-handed grip

This is the application of the opening group and the one with which I began this chapter. The partner grips the defender's jacket with both hands, at about chest height, and pulls or pushes. The defender drops into shiko dachi, letting the drop pull the partner's arms straight and down. At the same time, the defender's hands rise up the centre between the partner's arms and open outward, wedging the partner's arms apart. The combination of the drop and the wedge breaks the grip.

The rest of the opening group then follows. The low block clears one of the partner's arms downward. The low thrust strikes to the partner's abdomen or groin. The pull draws the partner forward and down, into the defender's next technique.

(2) The defender drops into shiko dachi; the hands begin to rise between the partner's arms. (3) The hands rise to the partner's wrists. (4) The wedge opens outward and the grip breaks. (5) The defender's left hand sweeps the partner's right arm down. (6) The low thrust to the partner's abdomen. (7) The defender grips and pulls. (8) The partner is drawn forward and down.](figures/fig8_6.png)

### The arm bar with the elbow

The elbow strike into the palm of group two has several applications. In one of the most useful, the partner throws a straight punch or reaches forward to grab. The defender deflects the arm and catches the partner's wrist with one hand, then turns and drops into shiko dachi as the other arm swings round and the elbow or forearm strikes against the back of the partner's upper arm, just above the elbow. The gripping hand pulls the wrist in the opposite direction. The result is a powerful arm bar, which can be used to strike, to lock or to drive the partner down to the floor.

### The lifting throw

The lifting movement of group four is a hip or shoulder throw. As the partner presses forward, the defender drops low, reaches through or behind the partner's legs with one arm and takes the partner's arm or collar with the other. The defender then rises and turns, lifting with the low arm and pulling down with the high arm, so that the partner is lifted and turned over the defender's hip or shoulder.

This throw is powerful and potentially dangerous. It must be practised only on suitable mats, with a partner who knows how to fall, and under supervision. In the early stages, practise only the entry and the lift, without completing the throw.

![**Figure 8.7** Applications of groups two and four, with a partner. (1) The partner reaches forward. (2) The defender catches the partner's wrist. (3) The defender turns; the forearm swings towards the partner's upper arm. (4) The arm bar, the forearm pressing above the elbow as the body drops. (5) The partner presses forward. (6) The defender drops low, one arm reaching behind the partner's legs. (7) The lift begins. (8) The turn, tipping the partner over the hip.](figures/fig8_7.png)

::: note
**Note: Practising throws safely**

All throws in this book should be practised only on suitable matting, under the supervision of a qualified instructor, with partners who have learned to fall safely. Begin with the entry and the off-balancing alone. Add the throw only when both partners are confident, and complete it slowly at first. If you do not have access to mats, practise only the entries.
:::

## Training Seiyunchin

**Build the stance.** Seiyunchin cannot be performed well without the endurance to hold shiko dachi for long periods. Work on the shiko dachi ladder alongside the kata.

**Practise the opening alone.** The opening group is the heart of the kata. Practise it alone, three times in each direction, at the start of each session in which you work on Seiyunchin.

**Test the release.** Practise the two-handed grip release with a partner regularly, gradually increasing the strength of the grip. Notice how much more effective the release is when your stance is deep and your arms are relaxed.

**Imagine the weight.** In the pulling and lifting movements, always imagine that you are pulling or lifting a real, heavy person. Practise occasionally with a heavy bag or a partner, to remind yourself what this feels like.

**Watch the breath.** Notice how your breathing changes between the slow opening and the faster groups. Make the change deliberate.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Seiyunchin contains no kicks. Its power comes from the stance, especially a long, deep shiko dachi.
- Its theme is control, pull and fight: grip an opponent, pull them off balance with the drop of the body, then strike, lock or throw.
- The opening group, performed three times, contains the release from a two-handed grip. The drop does most of the work; the arms provide the wedge.
- The elbow strike into the palm represents a pull and a strike meeting in the middle.
- The lifting movement is a throw and must be practised with the intention of moving real weight.
- The breathing changes from slow and deep in the opening to short and sharp in the striking groups.
- Build shiko dachi endurance alongside the kata.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Perform the shiko dachi ladder three times a week for a month.
2. Practise the opening group alone, three times in each direction, at the start of every Seiyunchin session.
3. With a partner's hands on your shoulders, practise the rising and opening movement until they feel no upward movement.
4. Test the two-handed grip release with a partner, ten times, at every session for a month, gradually increasing the strength of the grip.
5. Practise the elbow into palm with the feeling that the palm is pulling, twenty times on each side.
6. With a partner and on suitable mats, practise the entry and lift of the throw, without completing it, ten times on each side.
:::

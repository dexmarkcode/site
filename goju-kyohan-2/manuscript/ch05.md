# Tensho and the Soft Hand

::: chapter-intro
Tensho is the soft partner of Sanchin. Where Sanchin builds the hard frame, Tensho trains the hands to be heavy, adhesive and alive. This chapter explains the origins and purpose of the kata, sets out its sequence in movement groups, deals with the faults that most often spoil it, and then shows how Tensho leads directly into kakie, the pushing-hands practice that tests and develops the soft hand against a partner.
:::

For the first few years of my training I disliked Tensho. I could see the point of Sanchin; it was hard, it hurt, and I could feel it making me stronger. Tensho seemed by comparison to be a sort of slow-motion hand-waving. I performed it because I was told to, and I performed it badly, with stiff wrists and a vague sense of impatience.

What changed my mind was kakie. I was paired, one evening, with a much older student, a slight man in his sixties who had trained for over thirty years. We stood with our wrists touching and began the slow pushing and circling of the drill, and within a few seconds I realised that I could not find him. When I pushed, his arm was simply not where my push went. When I pulled, he came with me, and somehow arrived inside my guard. When I tried to strike, his hand was already resting on my elbow. And all the while his arm felt heavy, warm and completely relaxed, like a length of wet rope.

Afterwards I asked him how he did it. He said, "Tensho. Every day. For thirty years." I went home and started practising it properly.

## Origins

Tensho is generally attributed to Chojun Miyagi, who is said to have developed it in the early 1920s. It is based on a Chinese exercise known as *Rokkishu*, the "six hands", which appears in the *Bubishi*, the collection of Chinese martial arts texts that circulated in Okinawa and influenced several of the island's karate traditions. Rokkishu is said to have consisted of six fundamental hand techniques, and Tensho preserves and develops these in a flowing, circular form performed with deep, controlled breathing.

The name Tensho is usually translated as "turning palms" or "rotating hands", which describes the continuous circular movement of the hands that characterises the kata. Like Sanchin, it is a heishu kata, concerned with the inside of the body. But where Sanchin emphasises the hard aspect of Goju-Ryu, Tensho emphasises the soft. The two are complementary. A student who practises only Sanchin tends to become rigid. A student who practises only Tensho tends to become soft without being strong. Practised together, they produce the balance of hardness and softness from which the style takes its name.

## What Tensho trains

Tensho develops several qualities that are essential to the classical kata and to fighting at close range.

**Muchimi.** We met muchimi in Chapter 2: the heavy, adhesive quality of the hands that allows you to stick to an opponent and control them through contact. Tensho is the main solo exercise for developing it. The slow, circular hand movements, performed with steady pressure as though pushing through thick liquid, train the hands and forearms to carry weight and to maintain pressure through every part of a movement.

**Wrist and forearm strength.** Many of the Tensho techniques involve bending the wrist strongly in different directions while maintaining pressure. This builds strength in the wrist and forearm that is invaluable for grabbing, trapping, blocking and striking with the palm and the back of the wrist.

**Circular movement.** The hands in Tensho almost never move in a straight line. They circle, rotate and spiral. This trains you to use circular paths for blocking and controlling, so that an incoming force is redirected rather than met head-on.

**Breath and relaxation under control.** Tensho is performed with breathing that is generally softer than Sanchin, closer to nogare than to full ibuki, although some lineages perform it with strong, audible breath throughout. Either way, the breath must be coordinated with the hand movements so that each technique is supported by an exhalation and each transition by an inhalation.

**The connection between the centre and the hands.** In a good Tensho, the hands are not moved by the arms alone. They are moved by the whole body, through the back and shoulder girdle, from a stable stance. This connection is exactly what is needed in kakie and in the close-range applications of the classical kata.

## The six hands

Although Tensho contains more than six techniques in its full form, it is built around a small set of hand shapes and actions, which are repeated with each hand and with both together. The names vary between dojo; the ones given here are among the most common.

![**Figure 5.1** The principal hand shapes of Tensho, shown close up. (1) Kake uke, the open hooking block, palm facing outward. (2) Shotei, the palm heel, wrist bent back and fingers pointing up. (3) Gedan shotei, the downward palm heel, fingers pointing down. (4) Tensho uke, the circular open-hand block, palm turning outward. (5) Koken, the bent wrist, striking or blocking with the top of the wrist. (6) Tora guchi, the tiger's mouth, used in mawashi uke. (7) Nukite, the spear hand. (8) The open hand pulling back, hiki te.](figures/fig5_1.png)

**Kake uke**, the hooking block, is performed with the open hand, the palm facing outward and the fingers pointing up. It hooks and controls an incoming arm, often at the wrist, and can turn into a grab.

**Shotei**, the palm heel, is a strike or push with the heel of the palm, the wrist strongly bent back and the fingers pointing up. In Tensho it is often performed slowly, as a push, with the whole body behind it.

**Gedan shotei**, the low palm heel, is a downward push or strike with the fingers pointing down, used to press down an attacking arm or to strike the lower body.

**Tensho uke**, sometimes called *mawashi kake uke*, is a circular block in which the open hand sweeps from the inside to the outside, rotating so that the palm turns outward at the end of the movement. It is the technique from which the kata takes its name.

**Koken**, the bent wrist or "crane's head", uses the top of the bent wrist to block upward or to strike. It is a strong, compact surface well suited to close-range work.

**Tora guchi**, the tiger's mouth, is the hand position used in mawashi uke, with the thumb and fingers spread to form an open jaw. In Tensho it appears in the closing mawashi uke and, in some lineages, during the kata.

## The sequence in movement groups

Like Sanchin, Tensho is performed almost entirely in sanchin dachi, with slow movements and controlled breathing. Most lineages perform it in a straight line, stepping forward and back, without the turns of Miyagi's Sanchin. The sequence below follows a widely practised form; your own dojo's version may differ in detail.

### Group one: the opening

From the ready position, step forward into sanchin dachi and raise the hands into a position similar to sanchin kamae, but with the hands open. Some lineages begin with a slow double chudan uke and an inhalation before the open-hand techniques begin.

### Group two: the single-hand cycle

The kata then works through a cycle of techniques with one hand, usually the right, while the other hand rests at the side or remains in the guard position. A typical cycle is: kake uke, rotating into shotei pushed forward; the hand circles down into gedan shotei; the hand rises and rotates through tensho uke; the wrist bends into koken; the hand opens into shotei again and pushes forward. Each technique flows into the next without stopping, though there is a slight emphasis, supported by the breath, at the completion of each.

The cycle is then repeated with the left hand.

![**Figure 5.2** The single-hand cycle of Tensho with the right hand, seen from the front, left hand held at the side. (1) Kake uke. (2) The hand rotates as it travels forward. (3) Shotei pushed forward. (4) The hand circles down. (5) Gedan shotei. (6) The hand rises through tensho uke. (7) Koken. (8) The return to kake uke.](figures/fig5_2.png)

### Group three: the double-hand cycle

The same or similar techniques are then performed with both hands at once: double kake uke, double shotei, double gedan shotei, double tensho uke and so on. The double-hand techniques demand even more from the back and shoulders, because both arms must be supported at the same time.

### Group four: alternating and stepping

In many versions, the kata includes a section in which the hands alternate, one performing kake uke while the other pushes shotei, and a series of steps forward and back. The steps follow the same principles as in Sanchin: crescent steps, the head level, the structure maintained throughout.

### Group five: the close

The kata ends with mawashi uke and the return to the ready position.

![**Figure 5.3** The double-hand cycle and the close, seen from the side. (1) Double kake uke. (2) The hands rotate forward. (3) Double shotei. (4) The hands circle down. (5) Double gedan shotei. (6) The hands rise through tensho uke. (7) The alternating action, one hand in kake uke and one in shotei. (8) Mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.](figures/fig5_3.png)

## Performing Tensho well

Tensho is easy to perform poorly and very hard to perform well. Several principles will help.

### Slow and continuous

The hands should move slowly and continuously, without stopping and without sudden changes of speed. Imagine that your hands are moving through water, or through a thick, heavy liquid. There is resistance throughout every movement, and the hands must push through it.

### Heavy, not stiff

The arms and hands should feel heavy, as though weighted. This heaviness is not the same as stiffness. A stiff arm is tense throughout and cannot adjust. A heavy arm is relaxed in the joints but carries weight and pressure, like a rope with a weight on the end. The tension, where it exists, is in the back, the shoulder girdle and the wrists, not in the fingers or the biceps.

### The wrist is the hinge

Most of the shape changes in Tensho happen at the wrist. The wrist bends strongly back for shotei, forward for koken, sideways for kake uke and tensho uke. These bends should be full and firm. A Tensho performed with limp or half-bent wrists loses most of its value, because the wrist strength and flexibility it is meant to develop are not being trained.

### The elbow stays down

In almost every technique in Tensho, the elbow should point down and stay close to the body. If the elbow lifts or swings out, the arm loses its connection to the back. This is the same principle as in Sanchin, and it is equally hard to maintain.

### Breath leads the hand

Each technique should be supported by a breath. As a general rule, the inhalation accompanies the preparatory part of a movement and the exhalation accompanies the technique itself. In a slow technique such as a shotei push, the exhalation lasts as long as the push. The breath should lead the hand slightly, so that the exhalation begins just before the hand starts its working movement. This gives the technique a feeling of being driven from inside the body.

## The faults of Tensho

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Floppy wrists**

*The fault.* Your wrists bend only partly in each technique, and the hands look loose and vague.

*The cause.* Lack of wrist flexibility and strength, or a misunderstanding of the soft quality of Tensho as meaning "loose".

*The correction.* Practise each wrist position on its own, holding it for five breaths with firm tension in the forearm. Do the wrist stretches from the junbi undo routine daily. Remember that softness in Tensho means relaxed joints and continuous movement, not limp hands.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Tense fingers**

*The fault.* Your fingers are rigid and spread, and your hands tremble during the slow movements.

*The cause.* Tension has spread from the wrist and forearm into the fingers.

*The correction.* Keep the fingers together and gently extended, but relaxed. The tension should stop at the wrist. Practise the kata once at a very low level of tension, concentrating only on keeping the fingers relaxed while the wrists bend fully.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Rushing**

*The fault.* Your Tensho takes less than two minutes. The hands move quickly between positions and the breath is short.

*The cause.* Impatience, or the habit of performing kata at a fixed tempo.

*The correction.* Time your Tensho. A good performance usually takes between three and five minutes. Try to slow each technique so that it lasts a full exhalation of four to six seconds. Concentrate on the transitions between techniques, which are where most rushing happens.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Hands leaving the centre**

*The fault.* Your hands drift outward, away from the centre line of your body, so that the techniques look wide and open.

*The cause.* The circular paths are being exaggerated, or the elbows are flaring outward.

*The correction.* Keep the hands within the width of your shoulders for almost all of the kata. The circles should be small and compact. Check in a mirror, front-on. If your hands go outside the line of your shoulders, bring them back in.
:::

## From Tensho to kakie

Tensho is a solo exercise. It develops the soft hand, but it cannot test it. For that you need a partner, and the main partner exercise for the soft hand in Goju-Ryu is *kakie*.

Kakie, sometimes written *kakie* or *kakiye*, is a pushing-hands drill in which two partners stand facing each other with the backs of their wrists in contact and push, pull and circle against each other. It looks simple, and at its most basic it is. At its most advanced, it becomes a free-form exchange in which either partner can grab, lock, strike or throw at any moment, and in which success depends almost entirely on sensitivity, structure and timing.

Kakie is covered in more depth in Chapter 17, where its relationship to joint locks, throws and close-range striking is discussed. Here I want to introduce its basic form and to show how it grows out of Tensho.

### The basic position

The two partners stand facing each other in sanchin dachi or a similar stance, each with the right foot forward. They extend their right arms so that the backs of their wrists are touching, with the elbows bent and down and the hands roughly at the height of the solar plexus. The left hands are held in a guard position, often near the right elbow.

The contact at the wrists should be light but continuous. Neither partner should be pushing hard; the arms should feel heavy and relaxed, exactly as in Tensho.

### The basic cycle

One partner pushes forward with the palm, turning their hand so that the push is directed at the other partner's chest. The receiving partner absorbs the push by bending the elbow and turning the waist slightly, drawing the push in towards their own body and then redirecting it to the side with a circular movement of the wrist, rather like a tensho uke. As the push is redirected, the receiving partner's hand turns over and they push back. The first partner then absorbs and redirects in turn.

The result is a continuous, rolling circle of pushes and redirections, with the wrists always in contact. The movement is slow at first and gradually becomes faster and more fluid.

![**Figure 5.5** Basic kakie, seen from the side. (1) The starting position, the backs of the right wrists in contact. (2) The defender pushes forward with the palm. (3) The partner absorbs the push, bending the elbow and turning the waist. (4) The partner redirects the push outward with a circular wrist action. (5) The partner's hand turns over and pushes back. (6) The defender absorbs. (7) The defender redirects. (8) The cycle begins again.](figures/fig5_5.png)

### What the basic cycle trains

The basic kakie cycle tests and develops several qualities at once.

It tests your **structure**. If your stance is weak, your partner's push will move you backward. If your shoulders are raised, your arm will collapse under pressure. If your elbow flares out, your partner will find a way past it.

It develops your **sensitivity**. Through the contact at the wrist, you begin to feel your partner's intentions: when they are about to push, how hard, in which direction. With practice, you will find that you can feel a change in their pressure before you can see it.

It develops your **muchimi**. The continuous contact and the need to absorb and redirect force train the heavy, adhesive quality of the arm that Tensho prepares.

And it develops your **timing**. You learn to receive force at the right moment and to return it at the right moment, neither too early nor too late.

::: drill
**Drill: Basic kakie progression**

Week one. Practise the basic cycle slowly, with the right hands, for three minutes. Change to the left hands for three minutes. Concentrate on maintaining contact and keeping the elbow down.

Week two. Practise as above, but now each partner tries, on the push, to find a gap in the other's guard and touch their chest lightly with the palm. The receiving partner must redirect the push so that it misses.

Week three. Add stepping. As one partner pushes, they may step forward with a crescent step; the other steps back. Maintain contact throughout.

Week four. Allow either partner, at any time, to break the rhythm by pushing harder, pulling or changing the direction of the circle. The other partner must adapt without losing contact or structure.
:::

### The faults of beginning kakie

When students first try kakie, the same faults appear in almost every pair.

The most common is **pushing too hard**. Students treat kakie as a contest of strength and push against each other with all their might. The drill becomes a static shoving match and nothing is learned. The correction is to reduce the pressure until both partners can feel each other clearly, and to concentrate on the circle rather than the push.

The second is **losing contact**. One partner's wrist slides off the other's, or one partner pulls their arm away to avoid a push. The correction is to slow down and to treat contact as the first rule of the drill: whatever else happens, the wrists stay together.

The third is **using only the arm**. The partners push and receive with their arms alone, without involving the waist and stance. The pushes are weak and the receptions are flimsy. The correction is to absorb each push with a slight turn of the waist, exactly as in the gamaku exercises of Chapter 2, and to deliver each push from the stance and back, not from the shoulder.

The fourth is **rising**. As the drill continues, both partners gradually straighten their knees and rise in their stances. The correction is to check the stance at regular intervals and to remember that kakie, like Tensho, is built on sanchin dachi.

(3) Losing contact: the wrists separated. (4) Contact maintained through the circle. (5) Using only the arm: the receiving partner's body unmoved. (6) Absorbing with the waist: the receiving partner turns slightly. (7) Rising in the stance. (8) Both partners low and rooted.](figures/fig5_6.png)

## Tensho and the classical kata

Once you begin to understand Tensho, you will see it everywhere in the classical kata. The open-hand blocks of Shisochin, the circular deflections of Sepai, the palm strikes of Kururunfa, the hooking and grabbing hands of Seisan and Suparinpei, and the closing mawashi uke of every kata all draw on the hand shapes and qualities that Tensho develops.

This is not a coincidence. Tensho was designed, in part, as a way of training the hand techniques that the classical kata require, in a form that could be practised slowly and with concentration, away from the distractions of the full sequence. When you practise Tensho well, you are practising the hands of every classical kata at once.

There is a useful exercise that makes this connection explicit. After practising Tensho, choose a classical kata you know and perform only its open-hand techniques, in sequence, slowly, with the same quality of movement and breath as Tensho. Leave out the stances, the kicks and the closed-fist strikes. You will find that the kata's hands have a coherence you may not have noticed before.

## How often to practise Tensho

For most intermediate students, I recommend practising Tensho two or three times at each session, after Sanchin. Like Sanchin, it should be performed with full attention, and the quality of each performance matters more than the number.

Once a week, practise Tensho very slowly, taking six or seven minutes over a single performance. This slow practice reveals faults that are hidden at normal speed and develops a much deeper sense of the weight and continuity of the hands.

And practise kakie whenever you have a partner. Ten minutes of kakie at the end of a session, gradually increasing in freedom and intensity over months, will do more for your close-range skill than almost anything else in the system.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Tensho is the soft complement to Sanchin. It is generally attributed to Chojun Miyagi and based on the Rokkishu of the Bubishi.
- Tensho develops muchimi, wrist and forearm strength, circular movement, controlled breathing and the connection between the centre and the hands.
- The principal hand shapes are kake uke, shotei, gedan shotei, tensho uke, koken and tora guchi.
- Perform Tensho slowly and continuously, with heavy rather than stiff arms, fully bent wrists, elbows down and the breath leading the hand.
- The commonest faults are floppy wrists, tense fingers, rushing and hands drifting away from the centre.
- Kakie is the partner exercise that tests and develops the soft hand. Begin with the basic cycle of push, absorb and redirect, and progress gradually.
- The commonest faults in beginning kakie are pushing too hard, losing contact, using only the arm and rising in the stance.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise each of the six principal hand shapes on its own, holding each for five breaths, with each hand, daily for two weeks.
2. Time your Tensho. If it takes less than three minutes, slow it down until each technique lasts a full exhalation.
3. Perform Tensho once a week at a very slow pace, taking six to seven minutes.
4. Practise Tensho in front of a mirror, front-on, and check that your hands stay within the line of your shoulders.
5. With a partner, work through the four-week basic kakie progression, practising for at least ten minutes at each session.
6. Perform only the open-hand techniques of a classical kata you know, slowly, with the quality of Tensho.
:::

# Shisochin: Open Hands in Four Directions

::: chapter-intro
Shisochin is a kata of open hands, short power and constant turning. It asks you to deliver force from the shoulder blades through the palms and fingertips, to deal with threats from four directions, and to use the elbow and forearm to break an opponent's structure at the joint. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the shoulder power that drives it, deals with its commonest faults and explains its principal applications.
:::

The first time I saw Shisochin performed well, I thought the performer was moving too slowly. The opening steps forward were taken with a measured, almost ponderous deliberation, the open hands pushing out with a strange, pressing quality that I could not quite read. Then, about a third of the way through, the performer turned, and the speed and violence of the turn made me sit up. The hands, which had been moving like a slow tide, suddenly struck, and the elbow came round with a snap that you could hear across the room.

That contrast is the heart of Shisochin. It has passages of slow, heavy, pressing movement, in which the open hands push forward with the force of the whole body behind them, and passages of fast, explosive turning, in which the same open hands and elbows strike, lock and break. Learning to move between the two, and to understand why each exists, is the work of this kata.

## The character of Shisochin

The name Shisochin is usually interpreted as "fighting in four directions" or "battle in four directions", and the kata does turn to face the four points of the compass. Some teachers suggest that the four directions also refer to four kinds of attack, or four kinds of response; others simply take the name as a description of the floor pattern. Either way, the kata trains you to turn quickly and decisively and to arrive in a new direction already working.

Shisochin is also characterised by its open hands. Almost all of its hand techniques are performed with open hands: spear-hand thrusts, palm strikes, open-hand blocks, hooking grips. Closed fists appear only occasionally. This reflects the kata's concern with grabbing, controlling and striking at close range, where an open hand is often more useful than a closed one.

Above all, Shisochin is known for a quality of power often described as "shoulder power". Its open-hand thrusts and pushes are not driven by the arm alone, nor even by the hips alone, but by a sharp, short action of the shoulder blades and the muscles of the upper back, connected to a solid stance. When this works, the palm or fingertips arrive with surprising force from a very short distance. When it does not, the techniques look soft and empty.

## Shoulder power

Before looking at the sequence, let us spend some time on the source of Shisochin's power, because without it the kata is a hollow shell.

You will remember from Chapter 2 that power in Goju-Ryu travels from the floor, through the legs, hips and back, to the hands. In most techniques, the critical link between the trunk and the arm is the shoulder girdle: the shoulder blades, collarbones and the muscles that attach them to the ribs and spine. In Sanchin, you learn to keep the shoulder girdle down and connected, so that the arm is firmly joined to the back. In Shisochin, you learn to use the shoulder girdle actively, driving the arm forward from the back with a short, sharp movement of the shoulder blade.

The movement is small. If you place your hand flat on a partner's shoulder blade as they perform a Shisochin palm push, you will feel the blade slide forward around the ribs, perhaps an inch or two, at the moment the push arrives. This sliding action, called protraction by anatomists, adds the strength of the large muscles that wrap around the ribs to the push of the arm. Combined with a stable stance and a short turn of the waist, it produces a push that is very hard to resist.

::: drill
**Drill: Finding the shoulder blade**

Stand in sanchin dachi facing a wall, about a forearm's length away. Place your right palm flat on the wall at shoulder height, with the elbow bent and down. Without straightening the elbow, push into the wall by sliding your right shoulder blade forward around your ribs. Your body should not lean. Your elbow should stay bent. The push should come only from the shoulder blade, supported by the stance.

Then relax and let the shoulder blade slide back. Repeat ten times with each hand. Then try with both hands at once. Then try it with a partner's palms instead of the wall.

When you can feel the shoulder blade working, try to keep the same feeling while letting your shoulder stay down. The shoulder blade slides forward; it does not rise. This is the difference between shoulder power and a shrug.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Shrugging instead of pushing**

*The fault.* When you try to use shoulder power, your shoulder rises towards your ear.

*The cause.* Confusing the forward slide of the shoulder blade with the upward movement of a shrug.

*The correction.* Place your other hand on top of the working shoulder as you push. If you feel it rise, stop and start again with less effort. Think of the shoulder blade sliding forward and slightly down, around the side of the ribs, towards the front of the armpit.
:::

## Group one: the three opening steps

From the ready position, the kata steps forward with the right foot into sanchin dachi and the hands rise into an open-hand version of sanchin kamae, the palms facing inward or slightly forward. The breath is drawn in.

From here, the right hand performs a slow, powerful spear-hand thrust, *nukite*, forward at chest height, with a long exhalation and the shoulder blade driving the arm. The hand then opens, turns and draws back, often hooking as it returns, to finish in the open-hand guard. This is repeated with the step forward with the left foot, and again with the right, for three steps in all.

The opening is performed slowly, with the breath and tension of Sanchin. Each nukite should feel as though it is pressing through something heavy. Some lineages perform four spear-hand thrusts in this section rather than three, giving rise to the name *yonhon nukite*, "four-finger spear hand", for the technique.

### The return of the hand

The return of the hand after each nukite is as important as the thrust itself. In a poor Shisochin, the hand simply comes back to the guard. In a good one, it hooks, grips or pulls as it returns, so that the thrust and the return form a pair: strike and seize, or push and pull. This makes sense in application, where the nukite might be followed by a grab of the opponent's clothing or arm.

## Group two: the palm strikes

After the three steps, the kata changes rhythm. In most versions, this group contains a series of short, explosive palm strikes, *shotei*, delivered from sanchin dachi or a slightly longer stance. The hands, which have been moving slowly, suddenly accelerate. The strike is short, perhaps only a few inches, and the force comes from the shoulder blade, the turn of the waist and the drop into the stance, all arriving together.

In some versions the palm strikes are delivered with both hands at once, one high and one low, or both together at chest height, as a double push.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Soft palms**

*The fault.* Your palm strikes look and feel soft, like pushes rather than strikes.

*The cause.* The wrist is not bent back fully, so that the force is not concentrated in the heel of the palm, or the shoulder blade is not driving the strike.

*The correction.* Bend the wrist strongly back so that the heel of the palm leads. Practise the palm strike on a pad or a heavy bag, from a distance of a few inches, using only the shoulder blade and the drop. When you can move the bag, take the same feeling into the kata.
:::

## Group three: the turns to four directions

The middle of the kata is its most dramatic section. You turn to the right, then to the left, then to the front, and in each direction perform a sequence of open-hand blocking, grabbing and striking that ends in an elbow strike or forearm strike, often delivered as the body turns.

In the typical sequence, you turn into the new direction with an open-hand block, often a kake uke, which catches an imagined opponent's arm. The blocking hand grips and pulls. You then step or turn, and the other arm swings round so that the elbow or forearm strikes against the opponent's arm or body. The gripping hand pulls in one direction as the elbow strikes in the other.

The turns are fast, and the elbow strikes are performed with great force. But the speed must not destroy the structure. Each turn should arrive in a solid stance, with the head level and the body upright.

(4) The right hand grips and draws back. (5) The step and the turn of the body. (6) The left elbow swings round. (7) The elbow strike as the body settles. (8) The return to guard before the next turn.](figures/fig9_4.png)

### The turn is the technique

The most important thing to understand about this group is that the turn of the body is not a way of getting from one technique to the next. The turn is the technique. When you grip an opponent's arm and then turn your body, the turn itself pulls them off balance and twists their arm. The elbow strike is simply the final stage of a movement that has been working on the opponent from the moment you gripped.

This is why the turns in Shisochin must be fast and must arrive solidly. A slow or wobbly turn loses the pull; a turn that arrives in a weak stance cannot support the elbow strike.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The pause before the elbow**

*The fault.* You turn, settle, and then strike with the elbow, so that there is a visible pause between the turn and the strike.

*The cause.* Treating the turn and the elbow strike as separate movements.

*The correction.* Practise the sequence slowly, with the instruction that the elbow must begin to move as soon as the turn begins, and must arrive at the same moment as the stance settles. Then speed up. The turn and the strike should feel like a single action.
:::

## Group four: the kick and the double push

In many versions of Shisochin, the later part of the kata contains a front kick, followed by a double palm strike or push, often delivered as you land. This is similar to the kick-and-strike combination of Saifa, but with open hands. The kick disrupts the opponent; the double palm push drives them away or knocks them down.

Some versions also include a movement in which both hands rise in a large circle and come down together, which may be interpreted as a double block or as a grip on the opponent's head.

## Group five: the close

The kata returns to its starting point and finishes with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

## The rhythm of Shisochin

Shisochin has one of the clearest contrasts of rhythm in the classical kata. The opening is slow, heavy and continuous, with Sanchin breathing. The palm strikes are short and explosive. The turns are fast and powerful, with the breath expelled sharply on each elbow strike. The close is controlled and deliberate.

A common fault is to perform the whole kata at the speed of the opening, so that it becomes ponderous and the turns lose their snap. Another is to perform the whole kata at the speed of the turns, so that the opening loses its weight. Practise the opening and the turns separately until you can produce each rhythm at will, then put them together.

## Applications

### The nukite and the return

The opening thrusts have a simple and direct application. As an opponent closes, the defender's open hand deflects their lead arm and the nukite strikes to the solar plexus, the throat or the floating ribs. As the hand returns, it hooks the opponent's arm or grips their clothing and draws them forward, onto the defender's next technique.

The nukite is a dangerous strike to the soft targets of the body, and in training it should always be practised against a pad or with a light touch. Its value in application is often less as a finishing strike than as a sharp, disruptive one, which makes the opponent flinch and creates an opening for the grip.

### The arm break with the turn

The application of group three is the most famous in Shisochin. The partner throws a straight punch with the right hand. The defender's right hand deflects it with kake uke and grips the partner's wrist. The defender then turns, pulling the partner's arm straight and across their own body, and the left elbow or forearm strikes against the back of the partner's upper arm, just above the elbow.

The combination of the pull and the strike places the partner's elbow under great stress. In a real situation, this could break the arm. In training, it is practised slowly and with control, with the forearm pressing against the muscle above the elbow rather than striking the joint, until both partners understand the mechanics.

::: note
**Note: Elbow techniques in training**

Techniques that attack the elbow joint can cause serious and lasting injury. In partner practice, always apply them slowly, with the forearm or elbow pressing against the muscle of the upper arm rather than the joint, and stop the moment your partner taps or calls. Never apply an elbow technique quickly or with force in a cooperative drill.
:::

### The double palm push

The double palm push of group four is a powerful technique for creating space. When an opponent is very close, perhaps pressing into the defender or grabbing them, the defender drops slightly, draws the hands in to the chest, and drives both palms forward into the opponent's chest or shoulders, using the shoulder blades, the turn of the waist and the drive of the back leg. A well-delivered double push can send a much larger person several steps backward, giving the defender time to escape or to follow up with a kick.

The double push is also an excellent way to practise shoulder power with a partner, because the partner can feel immediately whether the power is coming from the arms or the whole body.

## Training Shisochin

**Train the shoulder blade.** Spend time on the shoulder-blade drill before every Shisochin session. Without shoulder power, the kata cannot be performed properly.

**Separate the rhythms.** Practise the slow opening and the fast turns as separate drills, then join them.

**Turn, turn, turn.** Practise the turns of group three on their own, in all four directions, twenty times each. Pay attention to the stance on arrival.

**Test the push.** Practise the double palm push with a partner holding a large pad against their chest. Notice how far you can move them from a short distance. Gradually reduce the distance.

**Use the hojo undo.** The chi ishi exercises of Chapter 6 strengthen the wrists for the open-hand techniques, and the kongoken pushes develop the shoulder power that Shisochin needs.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Shisochin is a kata of open hands, short power and turning to four directions.
- Its characteristic power comes from the shoulder blades sliding forward around the ribs, supported by the stance. This is not a shrug.
- The opening steps contain slow, pressing nukite thrusts. The return of the hand is as important as the thrust.
- The palm strikes are short and explosive, with the wrist strongly bent back.
- In the turns, the turn itself is the technique. The grip, the turn and the elbow strike form a single action.
- The rhythm contrasts slow, heavy pressing with fast, explosive turning.
- Elbow techniques must be practised slowly and with control in partner training.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise the shoulder-blade drill against a wall, ten times with each hand and ten with both, before every Shisochin session for a month.
2. Practise the opening steps on their own, slowly, with Sanchin breathing, five times.
3. Practise the turns of group three in all four directions, twenty times each, concentrating on arriving in a solid stance with the elbow strike.
4. With a partner holding a pad, practise the palm strike and the double palm push from a short distance, gradually reducing the distance.
5. With a partner, practise the arm-break application slowly, with the forearm pressing above the elbow, ten times on each side.
6. Perform Shisochin once with the whole kata at the speed of the opening, once at the speed of the turns, and once with the correct contrast. Note the difference.
:::

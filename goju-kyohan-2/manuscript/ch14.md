# Suparinpei: One Hundred and Eight

::: chapter-intro
Suparinpei is the longest and most complete kata of Goju-Ryu. It gathers almost every principle and technique of the earlier forms into a single extended sequence, from the slow open-hand thrusts of its opening to the joint attacks, throws and dropping strikes of its later sections. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains what makes it distinct, deals with the particular difficulties of performing a long kata well and explains its principal applications.
:::

There is a tradition in some dojo that Suparinpei is not taught until a student is ready to teach. The idea is not that the kata is secret, but that it is a summary, and that a summary is only useful to someone who already knows the material it summarises. A student who learns Suparinpei too early learns a long sequence of movements. A student who learns it at the right time recognises in it almost everything they have spent years studying, arranged in a new order that reveals new connections.

I learned Suparinpei later than many of my contemporaries, and I was impatient about it at the time. Looking back, I am grateful. When I finally learned it, I found that I already knew most of it. The opening was Shisochin's, but doubled. The four-direction section was Shisochin's turns, but extended. There was Seiyunchin's drop, Kururunfa's stillness, Sepai's circles, Sanseru's knees and Seisan's speed. What was new was the way they were joined together, and the sheer length and demand of the whole.

## The character of Suparinpei

Suparinpei means one hundred and eight. The number is significant in Buddhism, where it is associated with the number of earthly desires or defilements, and it is sometimes said that the kata was designed to overcome all one hundred and eight. Another tradition holds that the kata was designed to deal with one hundred and eight opponents, or that it combines three sets of thirty-six techniques. The kata is also sometimes called *Pechurin*, an older name.

Whatever the meaning of the name, Suparinpei is the culmination of the Goju-Ryu kata syllabus. It is usually taught at fourth dan or above, though some associations introduce it at third. It is long, taking two to three minutes to perform, and it is physically very demanding.

The kata begins slowly, with Sanchin breathing and tension, in a series of steps with double open-hand thrusts. It then turns to the four directions, with a long and varied sequence of open-hand blocks, grips, strikes and kicks, repeated in each direction. Later sections bring in shiko dachi, neko ashi dachi, sagi ashi dachi and kosa dachi, along with joint locks, throws, knee strikes and dropping strikes. It ends, as always, with mawashi uke.

## The demands of length

Before looking at the sequence, it is worth considering what a long kata asks of you, because the length of Suparinpei creates problems that shorter kata do not.

The first is **endurance**. Suparinpei is tiring. By the last third of the kata, most performers are breathing hard and their legs are burning. If your conditioning is inadequate, your stances will rise, your techniques will weaken and your breathing will become ragged. The kata will deteriorate as it goes on.

The second is **concentration**. It is difficult to maintain full attention through a long sequence. Most performers find that their minds wander at some point, usually in the middle of the four-direction section, where the same sequence is repeated several times. When the mind wanders, the techniques become automatic and the kata loses its intent.

The third is **pacing**. A long kata must be paced. If you perform the opening with maximum tension and the four-direction section at maximum speed, you will have nothing left for the end. But if you hold back too much, the kata becomes flat. The art is to give each section its proper intensity while keeping something in reserve.

::: drill
**Drill: Building endurance for Suparinpei**

Perform Suparinpei once at the start of a session, then rest for two minutes, then perform it again. Note at which point in the second performance your stances begin to rise and your breathing becomes ragged. Over the following weeks, add a third performance, then a fourth, with two minutes' rest between each. Each week, try to push the point of deterioration later.

Alongside this, use the shiko dachi ladder from Chapter 8 and the sanchin kamae hold from Chapter 6 to build general stance endurance.
:::

## Group one: the opening steps

From the ready position, step forward into sanchin dachi. The hands rise into an open-hand guard. Then, with Sanchin breathing and tension, both hands perform a slow, powerful double spear-hand thrust, *morote nukite*, forward at chest height. The hands open, turn and draw back. This is repeated with steps forward, for three steps in all.

The opening is like Shisochin's, but with both hands at once. It demands great strength in the back and shoulder girdle, because both arms must be driven forward by the shoulder blades simultaneously, and great stability in the stance. Many performers find that their shoulders rise or their stance shrinks during the double thrust; both should be checked rigorously.

In some lineages, the opening also includes double palm strikes or a slow pulling movement before the kata turns.

(4) The double nukite at full extension. (5) The hands open and turn. (6) The hands draw back. (7) The step forward. (8) The second double nukite.](figures/fig14_2.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The double-thrust shrug**

*The fault.* During the double nukite, both shoulders rise, and the thrust comes from the arms rather than the back.

*The cause.* The demand of driving both arms at once overloads the back muscles, and the shoulders take over.

*The correction.* Return to the shoulder-blade drill of Chapter 9, practising with both hands at once against a wall. Then perform the double nukite with a partner pressing lightly on your shoulders. If they rise, reduce the tension until they stay down, then build it up again over several weeks.
:::

## Group two: the four directions

The long central section of Suparinpei turns to each of the four directions in turn and performs a sequence of techniques in each. The exact sequence differs between lineages, but it typically includes an open-hand block such as kake uke, a grip and pull, a palm strike or a spear hand, a front kick, and a strike such as an elbow, a back-fist or a punch. The sequence is repeated in each direction, with turns of ninety or one hundred and eighty degrees between them.

This section is where concentration most often fails. Because the sequence is repeated, it is easy to perform it automatically, without imagining an opponent. Each repetition should be performed as though for the first time, against a new opponent from a new direction.

![**Figure 14.3** Group two, the four-direction sequence to the right, seen from the front. (1) The turn to the right with the open-hand block. (2) The grip. (3) The pull and the palm strike. (4) The knee lifts. (5) The front kick. (6) The landing with the elbow strike. (7) The back-fist. (8) The turn towards the next direction.](figures/fig14_3.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Automatic repetitions**

*The fault.* By the third or fourth direction, your techniques have become mechanical, and you are thinking about what comes next rather than what you are doing.

*The cause.* Loss of concentration over a long, repetitive section.

*The correction.* Before each turn, deliberately picture a new opponent in the new direction, attacking in a specific way: a punch, a grab, a push. Perform the sequence against that opponent. Vary the opponent each time. This keeps the mind engaged and gives each repetition its own intent.
:::

## Group three: the crane and the tiger

After the four directions, many versions of Suparinpei contain a section in which the body rises into sagi ashi dachi, the crane stance, with the hands in a high position, and then drops forward with a double strike in tora guchi or a double palm. In some versions, this is preceded by a slow, pressing movement of the hands, and followed by a kick from the lifted leg.

This group combines Kururunfa's sudden explosion from stillness with the balance of the one-legged stance. It requires excellent balance and timing.

## Group four: the drop and the low attacks

The later part of Suparinpei moves into shiko dachi for a section of dropping strikes and low attacks. In a typical version, the body drops into shiko dachi with a double downward strike, or a hammer-fist to the groin height, followed by grips and pulls, and joint attacks.

This group recalls Seiyunchin and Saifa, with the weight of the body used to drive strikes downward and to pull opponents off balance.

## Group five: the arm lock and the close

The final section of the kata typically includes a technique in which one arm controls an opponent's arm while the other strikes or presses against the elbow, similar to the arm bar of Seiyunchin and Shisochin, followed by a step back into neko ashi dachi and the closing mawashi uke.

## Pacing Suparinpei

Because Suparinpei is long, it must be paced. Here is one approach that I have found useful, though your teacher may suggest a different one.

The **opening** is performed with full Sanchin breathing and moderate to strong tension, but not maximal tension. It establishes the structure and the breath for the whole kata.

The **four-direction section** is performed with speed and power, but with attention to keeping the stances and structure intact. The breath is short and sharp on the main techniques and controlled between them.

The **crane and tiger** group is performed with full stillness and full explosion. This is a moment of peak intensity.

The **drop and low attacks** are performed with weight and power, but you should be aware that you are now in the last third of the kata and must keep something in reserve.

The **close** is performed with full attention and control. Mawashi uke should be as good at the end of Suparinpei as it is at the end of Saifa.

## Suparinpei as a summary

I said that Suparinpei is a summary of the Goju-Ryu kata. It is worth making this explicit, because seeing the connections is one of the main benefits of learning it.

| Element in Suparinpei | Earlier kata in which it appears |
|---|---|
| Slow open-hand thrusts with Sanchin breathing | Sanchin, Shisochin |
| Four-direction turns with grips and strikes | Shisochin |
| Open-hand blocks and palm strikes | Tensho, Shisochin, Kururunfa |
| Front kicks and knee strikes | Saifa, Sanseru, Seisan |
| Explosion from stillness | Kururunfa |
| Crane stance | Sepai, Kururunfa |
| Dropping strikes in shiko dachi | Saifa, Seiyunchin |
| Arm locks against the elbow | Seiyunchin, Shisochin |
| Closing mawashi uke | All |

: **Table 14.1** Elements of Suparinpei and the earlier kata in which they appear.

When you perform Suparinpei, try to feel each of these connections. When you perform the opening, remember Shisochin. When you turn to the four directions, remember Shisochin's turns. When you rise into the crane stance, remember Kururunfa's stillness. In this way, Suparinpei becomes not just a long kata but a review of everything you know.

## Applications

### The double nukite

The double spear-hand thrust of the opening can be applied as a strike to two targets at once, such as the solar plexus and the throat, or the two sides of the chest. It can also be applied as a wedge, driven between an opponent's arms to break a two-handed grip or push their arms apart, in a similar way to the opening of Seiyunchin.

### The four-direction sequence

The four-direction sequence contains a complete exchange: deflect, grip, pull, strike, kick, strike. Its application follows the same pattern as many earlier kata. The partner attacks; the defender deflects and grips; the defender pulls the partner off balance and strikes; the defender kicks the partner's legs or body; the defender finishes with a close-range strike.

(3) The partner's arms are driven apart. (4) The defender's palm strike. (5) The partner punches; the defender's open-hand block deflects. (6) The grip and pull. (7) The front kick to the partner's body. (8) The elbow strike as the defender lands.](figures/fig14_6.png)

### The crane and tiger

The crane stance and double strike can be applied as a response to a low kick or a sweep: the leg lifts to avoid or check the attack, and as it lands, the defender drives forward with a double strike to the partner's head or chest.

### The arm lock

The arm lock of the final section is applied in the same way as the arm bars of Seiyunchin and Shisochin. The partner punches or grabs; the defender deflects and grips the wrist; the defender turns and presses the forearm against the back of the partner's arm, above the elbow, controlling them or driving them to the floor.

## Training Suparinpei

**Learn it in parts.** Suparinpei is too long to learn as a single sequence. Learn it group by group, and practise each group thoroughly before joining it to the others.

**Build endurance.** Use the endurance drill and the stance-holding drills to build the conditioning the kata needs.

**Keep the mind engaged.** Picture a new opponent in each direction. Vary them.

**Pace it.** Decide in advance how much intensity each section receives, and stick to it.

**Look for the connections.** Use Table 14.1 to remind yourself where each element comes from. Practise the earlier kata alongside Suparinpei to keep the connections fresh.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Suparinpei, meaning one hundred and eight, is the longest and most complete kata of Goju-Ryu. It summarises the principles and techniques of the earlier kata.
- A long kata demands endurance, concentration and pacing.
- The opening uses slow double spear-hand thrusts with Sanchin breathing. Watch for rising shoulders.
- The four-direction section repeats a complete exchange in each direction. Picture a new opponent each time to keep the mind engaged.
- The crane and tiger combine stillness, balance and explosion.
- The later sections bring back the drops of Seiyunchin and the arm locks of Shisochin.
- Learn Suparinpei group by group, and practise the earlier kata alongside it.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Write out the movement groups of your own version of Suparinpei. For each group, note the earlier kata in which its main elements appear.
2. Practise the opening double nukite with a partner pressing on your shoulders, five times, at every Suparinpei session.
3. Use the endurance drill once a week, recording the point at which your performance deteriorates.
4. Perform the four-direction section with a different imagined opponent in each direction. Describe each opponent in your training notebook.
5. Practise the crane and tiger group on its own, ten times, focusing on stillness and explosion.
6. Decide on a pacing plan for the whole kata and perform it according to the plan three times. Adjust the plan as needed.
:::

# Sanseru: Thirty-Six Hands and the Low Kick

::: chapter-intro
Sanseru brings the legs back into the fight. Where Seiyunchin had no kicks and Shisochin used them sparingly, Sanseru is built around low, close-range kicks to the knee and shin, delivered while the hands control and strike. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the joint kick and the close-range hand work that accompanies it, deals with the faults that most often appear, and explains its principal applications.
:::

I remember being taught Sanseru by a teacher who had been a policeman for many years. He was not a large man, and he was by then in his sixties, but he had a way of standing very close to you, almost uncomfortably close, and making you feel that you had no room to do anything at all. When he demonstrated the joint kick in Sanseru, he did it from that distance. His hands rested on my arms, quite lightly. I barely saw his foot move. But I felt it arrive against the side of my knee, gently, with a pressure that made it very clear what would have happened if he had meant it.

"Most fights," he said, "are not at kicking range. They are at this range. And at this range, nobody is watching your feet."

That is Sanseru. It is a kata for the range at which most people think kicking is impossible. Its kicks are short, low and fast, delivered to the joints of the legs while the hands are busy with the upper body. It teaches you to fight with all four limbs at once.

## The character of Sanseru

Sanseru means thirty-six, and like the other numerical names it is usually linked to Buddhist numerology, thirty-six being three times twelve. Various attempts have been made to connect the number to the content of the kata, for example by counting thirty-six techniques or thirty-six vital points, but none of these is convincing. It is best to treat the name as a traditional label.

The character of Sanseru, on the other hand, is very clear. It is a close-range kata, built on sanchin dachi and its relatives, and it combines strong hand techniques, often delivered in pairs, with low kicks to the joints of the legs. Its most famous technique is *kansetsu geri*, the joint kick, a short, stamping kick to the side or front of the opponent's knee. It also contains front kicks, knee strikes, elbow strikes and a range of grabs and pulls.

Sanseru also marks the point in the syllabus at which the hand techniques become noticeably more complex. Where earlier kata generally performed one hand technique at a time, Sanseru often uses both hands at once, one controlling and one striking, or both striking in rapid succession. The kicks are added on top of this, so that the performer must coordinate three or four techniques in a single moment.

## The joint kick

Before the sequence, let us look at the kata's signature technique.

Kansetsu geri is a short kick delivered with the sole or the edge of the foot to the opponent's knee, usually from the side or the front. The knee is raised only a little, perhaps to the height of the opponent's knee, and the foot is driven out and down in a stamping action. The target is the side of the knee, where a sideways force can damage the ligaments, or the front, just below the kneecap, where a forward force can hyperextend the joint. A lower version targets the shin or the top of the foot.

The kick is not a big, athletic technique. Its power comes from the drop of the body onto the kicking leg as it lands, rather than from the swing of the leg. It is delivered from very close range, often while the hands are holding the opponent, so that they cannot step away from it.

::: drill
**Drill: The joint kick against a pad**

Have a partner hold a large pad against the outside of their knee, or lean a kick shield against a heavy bag at knee height. Stand in sanchin dachi, close enough that your hands could touch your partner's shoulders. Raise your knee a little and drive your foot into the pad with a downward, stamping action, letting your body drop slightly as the foot lands. Return the foot immediately to sanchin dachi.

Do ten kicks with each leg, slowly at first. Concentrate on keeping your upper body still and upright; the kick should not be visible above the waist. Then add a hand technique, such as a punch or a grip, performed at the same moment as the kick.
:::

::: note
**Note: Safety with the joint kick**

The knee is easily injured. Joint kicks must never be practised against a partner's unprotected knee, except as a slow, light touch to show the target. Use pads and kick shields for any practice with force.
:::

## Group one: the opening steps

From the ready position, the kata steps forward into sanchin dachi and raises the arms into sanchin kamae, with Sanchin breathing. Then, in most lineages, it performs a sequence of punches: a double punch, right and then left, or both together, followed by a return to the double chudan uke. This is repeated with steps forward, for three steps in all.

Unlike Sanchin, these punches are performed with speed and snap rather than slow tension. In some versions, each step also includes a sharp exhalation on the punches and a short pause in sanchin kamae, giving the group a distinctive rhythm of step, block, punch-punch, block.

### The double punch

The double punch of Sanseru is often misunderstood. In the version with one punch after the other, it is easy to treat the two punches as a simple combination. But in Goju-Ryu the double punch usually serves a more specific purpose: the first punch is a clearing or disruptive technique, often aimed at the opponent's guard or shoulder, and the second is the real strike, delivered through the opening that the first has made. In the version with both hands punching together, the two fists typically strike at different levels, one high and one low, so that the opponent cannot block both.

Either way, the punches must be delivered from a stable stance, with the shoulders down and the back engaged. A common fault is to lean forward on the punches, losing the structure of sanchin dachi.

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Leaning on the punches**

*The fault.* Your upper body tips forward as you punch, and your weight shifts onto your front foot.

*The cause.* Reaching for a target that is too far away, or trying to add power by throwing the body forward.

*The correction.* Imagine the target is closer than you think. Punch from the stance, with the shoulders down and the body upright. Ask a partner to stand beside you with their hand flat against your back as you punch. They should feel your back stay vertical.
:::

## Group two: the turns and the joint kicks

After the opening steps, the kata turns to the side and introduces the joint kick. In a typical sequence, you turn to the right, perhaps with a block or a grip; the hands control an imagined opponent's arm or shoulders; and you deliver kansetsu geri to their knee, then stamp down and follow with a strike, often an elbow or a punch. The sequence is then repeated to the left.

Some lineages include a front kick in this section as well as, or instead of, the joint kick. Others include a short, low side kick.

The key point in this group is coordination. The hands and the kick must work together. The hands grip or press the opponent to stop them stepping back; the kick attacks their base; the strike follows as they buckle.

![**Figure 10.3** Group two, the turn and the joint kick, seen from the side. (1) The turn to the right, the hands rising to guard. (2) The left hand grips and the right hand presses. (3) The knee lifts a little. (4) The joint kick drives out and down. (5) The foot stamps down into the stance. (6) The elbow strike. (7) The turn to the left. (8) The joint kick on the other side.](figures/fig10_3.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The visible kick**

*The fault.* Before your joint kick, your upper body leans back or your hands drop, so that the kick is obvious.

*The cause.* Lifting the leg too high, or rocking the body to generate power.

*The correction.* Practise the kick with your hands resting on a partner's shoulders. They should not feel any change in your hands or your posture until the kick arrives. Keep the knee lift small and let the power come from the drop as the foot lands.
:::

## Group three: the grab and the knee

The middle of Sanseru contains a group in which both hands reach forward and grip, then pull down sharply as the knee rises. In the kata, this is often performed as a double grab at head height, drawn down to the level of the hips, with a knee strike driven upward to meet it.

This is a simple, brutal technique, and its application is obvious: you seize the opponent's head, neck or clothing and pull them down onto your rising knee. It is also the first time in the classical kata that the knee is used as a strike in its own right, rather than as a preparation for a kick.

In some lineages, the grab and knee strike are followed by a push or a throw, using the momentum of the pulled-down opponent.

![**Figure 10.4** Group three, the grab and the knee, seen from the side. (1) Both hands reach forward. (2) The grip at head height. (3) The pull down begins. (4) The knee rises to meet the pull. (5) The knee strike at the top of its rise. (6) The foot steps down. (7) The double push forward. (8) The return to sanchin dachi.](figures/fig10_4.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The knee without the pull**

*The fault.* Your knee rises strongly, but your hands are already at your hips, having finished their pull before the knee arrives.

*The cause.* Treating the grab, the pull and the knee as three separate movements.

*The correction.* The hands and the knee must meet in the middle. Practise slowly, with the instruction that the hands and the knee arrive at the same point at the same moment. Use a heavy bag held at waist height by a partner: grip the top of the bag, pull it down and drive your knee into it.
:::

## Group four: elbows and shiko dachi

Later in the kata, Sanseru moves into shiko dachi for a group of elbow strikes and downward techniques. In a typical version, you step into shiko dachi with a rising or horizontal elbow strike, followed by a back-fist or hammer-fist downward, or a low block. These are close-range techniques, delivered with the drop of the body.

This group links Sanseru to the earlier kata. The shiko dachi and the dropping strikes recall Saifa and Seiyunchin; the elbows recall Shisochin. Sanseru adds them to its repertoire of low kicks and double hand techniques, creating a kata that works at very close range with every part of the body.

## Group five: the close

The kata returns to its starting point and finishes, as always, with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

## Coordinating hands and feet

The great challenge of Sanseru is coordination. In earlier kata, the hands and feet rarely needed to do different things at the same moment. In Sanseru, the hands may be gripping and pulling while one foot kicks and the other supports the whole weight of the body. This is difficult, and it is the main reason why Sanseru tends to look ragged when students first learn it.

There are two common responses to this difficulty, both of them wrong. The first is to separate the hands and feet, so that the hands finish, then the kick happens, then the hands do something else. This makes the kata easier to perform but destroys its logic; in application, a kick delivered without the hands' control is easily avoided. The second is to rush, trying to do everything at once and doing nothing well.

The right response is to slow down. Practise each coordinated movement slowly enough that you can attend to both the hands and the feet. Gradually increase the speed while keeping the coordination. This takes time, but there is no other way.

::: drill
**Drill: Hands and feet together**

Choose one coordinated movement from Sanseru, such as the grip and joint kick. Perform it ten times at one quarter of normal speed, watching in a mirror to check that the hands and foot arrive at the same moment. Then perform it ten times at half speed. Then ten times at three quarters speed. Then ten times at full speed. If the coordination breaks down at any speed, go back to the previous speed and do ten more.

Do this for each coordinated movement in the kata, one per session, until you have worked through them all.
:::

## Applications

### The double punch inside the guard

The opening double punch has a direct application at close range. As the opponent raises their guard, the first punch strikes their lead arm or shoulder, knocking the guard aside or turning their body. The second punch strikes through the opening to the face or body. In the version where both fists punch together at different levels, one fist strikes high to draw the guard up while the other strikes low into the body.

### The joint kick under control

The joint kick of group two is applied while the hands control the opponent. In one common version, the opponent grabs the defender's jacket. The defender grips the opponent's arms, pinning them in place, and delivers the joint kick to the side of the opponent's knee. As the knee buckles, the defender's elbow or forearm strikes into the opponent's face or chest, or the defender pulls the opponent down and to the side.

(3) The second punch through the opening. (4) The partner grips the defender's jacket. (5) The defender grips the partner's arms. (6) The joint kick to the side of the partner's knee. (7) The partner's knee buckles. (8) The defender's elbow strike as the partner drops.](figures/fig10_6.png)

### The grab and the knee

The application of group three is perhaps the most straightforward in all the classical kata. The opponent is close and bent slightly forward, perhaps after a body strike or a joint kick. The defender grips the back of the opponent's head or neck with both hands, or their collar, and pulls sharply down while driving the knee up into the face or chest. The pull and the knee meet in the middle, doubling the force of the impact.

In training, this is practised against a heavy bag, a large pad held by a partner at chest height, or very slowly against a partner, with the knee stopping well short of contact.

### The elbow in shiko dachi

The elbow strikes of group four are close-range finishing techniques, often used after a joint kick or a knee strike has bent the opponent over. The drop into shiko dachi adds weight to the elbow and lowers the defender's centre of gravity, making it difficult for the opponent to push or pull them over.

![**Figure 10.7** Applications of groups three and four, with a partner. (1) The partner is bent forward. (2) The defender grips the back of the partner's neck with both hands. (3) The pull down. (4) The knee rises to meet the pull. (5) The partner pushes forward. (6) The defender drops into shiko dachi. (7) The rising elbow into the partner's chin or chest. (8) The downward hammer fist onto the partner's back.](figures/fig10_7.png)

::: note
**Note: Practising knee and elbow strikes**

Knee and elbow strikes are among the most powerful techniques in karate and can cause serious injury. Practise them against pads, bags or shields. When working with a partner, perform them slowly and stop well short of contact.
:::

## Common questions about Sanseru

*Why are the kicks so low?* Because Sanseru works at close range, where a high kick is slow, easy to see and likely to leave you standing on one leg in front of someone who can push you over. A low kick to the knee or shin is fast, hard to see and very hard to defend while the hands are busy.

*Is the joint kick legal in competition?* In most karate competitions, kicks to the knee joint are prohibited, for good reason. This is one of several ways in which competition karate and classical kata diverge. Train the technique for its own sake and for self-protection, not for the competition mat.

*Why do some versions include a front kick instead of the joint kick?* Over time, and in different lineages, techniques have sometimes been altered to make them safer, easier to perform or more in keeping with the style's other kata. The front kick and the joint kick serve a similar purpose in this part of the kata: to attack the opponent's base while the hands control them.

*How close is close?* Close enough that you could rest your forearms on your partner's shoulders without stretching. That is the range for which Sanseru was built.

## Training Sanseru

**Build the joint kick.** Practise kansetsu geri against a pad or shield at every Sanseru session, twenty times with each leg.

**Slow the coordination.** Use the hands and feet together drill to build coordination gradually.

**Close the distance.** Practise the applications at genuinely close range, closer than feels natural. Sanseru is not a kata for long distances.

**Keep the stance.** Sanseru's kicks are delivered from sanchin dachi and its relatives. Check that your stance does not shrink or rise during the kicks.

**Use the bag.** The knee strikes, elbows and joint kicks of Sanseru can all be practised against a heavy bag. Bag work will show you whether your techniques have real power.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sanseru is a close-range kata that combines double hand techniques with low kicks to the joints of the legs.
- Its signature technique is kansetsu geri, a short, stamping kick to the knee, delivered while the hands control the opponent.
- The double punch typically uses the first punch to clear the guard and the second to strike.
- The grab and knee strike require the hands and the knee to meet in the middle.
- The greatest challenge of Sanseru is coordinating hands and feet. Slow down until the coordination is correct, then build speed.
- Practise joint kicks, knees and elbows against pads, never against a partner's unprotected joints.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise the joint kick against a pad, twenty times with each leg, at every Sanseru session for a month.
2. Practise the joint kick with your hands resting on a partner's shoulders, and ask them to tell you whether they can feel it coming.
3. Use the hands and feet together drill on one coordinated movement from Sanseru per session.
4. Practise the grab and knee against a heavy bag held by a partner, ten times on each side.
5. With a partner, work through the joint kick application slowly, with a pad held against the partner's knee.
6. Perform Sanseru at a quarter of normal speed, checking the coordination of every movement, once a week.
:::

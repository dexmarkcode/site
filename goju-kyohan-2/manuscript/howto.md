# How to Use This Book

This is a textbook, and like any good textbook it rewards being used rather than simply read. A few notes on how it is arranged will help you get the most from it.

## The six parts

The book is divided into six parts, each of three chapters.

**Part One, The Second Stage of Training**, sets the ground. It explains how the classical kata fit together, describes the body mechanics that distinguish Goju-Ryu from other styles, and examines the stances and transitions on which the advanced forms depend. Even if you are keen to get to the kata, please do not skip it. Most of the faults described in later chapters have their roots here.

**Part Two, Tension, Breath and Conditioning**, deals with Sanchin, Tensho and hojo undo. These are the three pillars of Goju-Ryu physical training, and they are also where students most often injure themselves or waste years doing something that looks right but isn't.

**Parts Three, Four and Five** take the eight kaishu kata in the order in which they are usually taught: Saifa, Seiyunchin and Shisochin; then Sanseru, Sepai and Kururunfa; then Seisan and Suparinpei. The final chapter of Part Five steps back to consider how the whole system fits together over a lifetime of training.

**Part Six, Classical Applications**, explains how to read a kata for its fighting content, covers the close-range skills of kakie, joint locking and throwing on which most Goju applications depend, and sets out a structured method for turning applications into reliable skill through partner drills and pressure testing.

## Inside each chapter

Each chapter opens with a short italic introduction telling you what it covers and why it matters. The main text is broken into sections with clear headings. Within the text you will find several kinds of panel:

- **Fault and Correction** panels describe a specific, common problem, explain its cause and give you a way to fix it.
- **Drill** panels give you a focused exercise to isolate one quality, with sets, repetitions or durations where these are useful.
- **Note** panels add historical background, variations between lineages, or safety points.

At the end of every chapter there is a summary of **Key Points** and a set of **Practice Tasks**. The tasks are graded so that the first few can be done alone, and the later ones need a partner or a teacher.

## Reading the kata chapters

The kata chapters do not attempt to replace your teacher. A form cannot be learned from a book, any more than a language can be learned from a dictionary. What these chapters do instead is give you a map of each kata in *movement groups*, short sections of the form that share a purpose, so that you can understand what you are practising and remember it more easily. For each group you will find the key stances and techniques, the faults that most commonly appear, and at least one classical application.

The figures for each kata follow the floor pattern, or *embusen*, from the starting position. Unless a caption says otherwise, you begin facing the top of the page in the floor diagrams and facing the reader in front-view drawings. Side-view drawings show you from your right-hand side, so that you are moving from left to right across the page. In drawings with two people, the defender, the person performing the kata movement, wears the dark belt and the partner wears the light belt.

## Terminology

Japanese terms are given in italics the first time they appear in each chapter and in roman type thereafter. There is a full glossary at the back. I have kept to the Japanese names that are most widely used in Okinawan Goju-Ryu, and I have given the alternatives where they are common. Spellings of kata names vary, so you will see *Seiyunchin* and *Seienchin*, *Sanseru* and *Sanseiru*, *Sepai* and *Seipai*. I have used one spelling consistently and listed the others in the glossary.

## Left and right

Directions are always given from the point of view of the person performing the technique. "Step forward with the right foot" means your right foot. "Your partner's left wrist" means their left, not the one on your left. In partner drills the person performing the technique or application is called the *defender* and the person providing the attack is called the *partner*. In the old terminology these are *tori* and *uke*, but since uke also means block, I have avoided it to prevent confusion.

## How long it will take

There is no honest answer to this. A diligent student training three or four times a week will usually spend between eight and twelve years working through the material in this book to a reasonable standard, and the rest of their life refining it. That is not a discouragement. Every chapter will give you something you can use in your next class. Read a chapter, train with it for a month, and then read it again. You will find it says something different the second time, because you will be a different student.

## The troubleshooting index

Appendix C lists the most common faults in alphabetical order by symptom, such as "shoulders rise in Sanchin", "losing balance on turns" or "applications fail against resistance", with a reference to the chapter and panel where the correction appears. If you are pressed for time or have a particular problem nagging at you, start there.

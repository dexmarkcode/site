# Kururunfa: Stillness and Sudden Force

::: chapter-intro
Kururunfa is the kata of the cat stance and the sudden strike. It alternates long, held moments of stillness in neko ashi dachi with explosive bursts of palm strikes, kicks and whole-body turns that break an opponent's balance. This chapter sets out the kata in movement groups, explains the principles of stillness and timing on which it depends, deals with its commonest faults and explains its principal applications, including the takedowns for which it is known.
:::

Of all the classical kata, Kururunfa is the one I most enjoy watching performed by a really good karateka. There is a quality of stillness in it that no other kata quite matches. The performer stands in neko ashi dachi, hands extended, utterly motionless, for what feels like several seconds. You find yourself leaning forward, waiting. And then the performer explodes, the front foot snapping out, the palms striking, the whole body turning and dropping, and it is over before you have quite seen it. Then stillness again.

I once asked a senior teacher what he was doing during those still moments. He thought about it for a while and said, "Listening." I asked what he was listening for. He said, "For the moment."

That answer frustrated me at the time. It no longer does. Kururunfa is a kata about timing: about waiting, apparently doing nothing, for exactly the right instant, and then acting with total commitment. The stillness is not empty. It is full of attention.

## The character of Kururunfa

The name Kururunfa is usually interpreted as "holding on long and striking suddenly", or "forever, stillness, and tearing". The first interpretation describes the kata's rhythm very well. Long periods of held, alert stillness alternate with sudden, explosive action.

Kururunfa is dominated by neko ashi dachi, which appears more often in this kata than in any other. It uses the stance's lightness and mobility to full effect: the front foot is constantly available for kicking, checking and sweeping, and the body can move backward or turn very quickly from it. The hands are mostly open, performing palm strikes, open-hand blocks and grips. There are also kicks, knee strikes, elbow strikes and several movements that are widely understood as takedowns, in which the opponent's head, neck or arm is twisted and turned to throw them to the floor.

Kururunfa is a technically demanding kata. Its stillness requires perfect balance in neko ashi dachi. Its sudden movements require speed and explosive power. And its takedowns require a clear understanding of how to break an opponent's balance with the turning body.

## The stillness of neko ashi dachi

Before the sequence, let us consider the stance on which Kururunfa depends. In Chapter 3 we looked at neko ashi dachi in some detail. Kururunfa makes particular demands on it.

First, you must be able to hold the stance perfectly still for several seconds, with the weight almost entirely on the rear leg, the front foot light, and the body completely balanced. Any wobble or adjustment destroys the effect of the stillness and, in application, betrays your intention.

Second, you must be able to move from the stance instantly, in any direction: forward with a kick or a step, backward with a retreat, or round with a turn. This requires the rear leg to be loaded like a spring, the knee bent and the hips low, so that the push can come at once.

Third, your upper body must stay relaxed and alert while your legs hold the stance. Tension in the shoulders or arms will slow the sudden movements and spoil the stillness.

::: drill
**Drill: The still cat**

Stand in neko ashi dachi with your hands extended in the guard position. Hold the stance for ten seconds, perfectly still. Then, without any preparatory movement, explode into a front kick with the front leg, land, and return immediately to neko ashi dachi. Hold for ten seconds again. Repeat ten times on each side.

Then change the explosion: a step forward with a palm strike, a step back, a turn of ninety degrees. In each case, the stillness must be complete, and the movement must begin without any preparatory shift.

Ask a partner to watch for the smallest movement before each explosion. A shift of the hips, a twitch of the shoulder or a change of breath will all give you away.
:::

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: The telegraphed explosion**

*The fault.* Before each sudden movement, your body shifts slightly: the hips drop, the shoulders rise, or you take a visible breath.

*The cause.* Your body is not ready to move from where it is, so it has to prepare first.

*The correction.* Be ready before you need to be. Keep the rear knee bent and loaded, the weight settled, the breath steady and low. Practise the still cat drill with a partner watching, and do not move until they can no longer see your preparation.
:::

## Group one: the opening

From the ready position, the kata moves to the right diagonal and settles into neko ashi dachi. The hands perform a slow open-hand sequence, typically a kake uke with one hand and a pressing or grasping action with the other, ending with a palm strike or push, all performed slowly and with controlled breathing. The movement ends in stillness.

The sequence is then repeated to the left diagonal, and in some versions a third time to the front.

This opening sets the tone of the kata: slow, precise hand movements from a perfectly balanced stance, ending in stillness. The power is held in reserve.

## Group two: the sudden strike

After the opening, the kata breaks its stillness for the first time. From neko ashi dachi, the front knee lifts and a front kick snaps out, followed, as the foot lands, by a burst of palm strikes or a double palm strike. In some versions the knee lifts without kicking, as a check or a knee strike, before the foot steps down into a forward stance with the strikes.

The contrast with the opening could not be sharper. Where the opening was slow and held, this group is explosive. The kick, the landing and the strikes should flow together as a single action.

## Group three: the palm strikes in shiko dachi

The middle of Kururunfa turns to the sides and drops into shiko dachi for a series of powerful palm strikes. In a typical version, you turn, drop into shiko dachi and strike with both palms at once, one high and one low, or both at the same level, with a sharp exhalation and the full drop of the body. This is repeated to the other side.

These palm strikes are among the most powerful techniques in the kata. They combine the shoulder power of Shisochin with the drop of Seiyunchin, delivered through the heels of both palms at once.

![**Figure 12.4** Group three, the double palm strike in shiko dachi, seen from the front. (1) The turn to the right. (2) The hands draw back. (3) The drop into shiko dachi begins. (4) The double palm strike, one high and one low. (5) The return. (6) The turn to the left. (7) The double palm strike on the left. (8) The return to neko ashi dachi.](figures/fig12_4.png)

::: fault
**Fault and Correction: Palms without a drop**

*The fault.* Your double palm strikes are fast, but they land on top of a shiko dachi that has already settled.

*The cause.* Treating the drop and the strike as separate events, as in the floating shiko dachi of Saifa.

*The correction.* Begin the strike from a higher position and let the palms and the drop arrive together. The top of your head and the heels of your palms should stop at the same instant.
:::

## Group four: the turning takedown

The most distinctive group in Kururunfa contains a turning movement in which the hands appear to grasp something at head height and twist it, as the body turns and steps back, often into neko ashi dachi. In many lineages, this is understood as a takedown in which the opponent's head is gripped and turned, so that their body follows it to the floor.

There are several versions of this group, and the hand positions differ considerably. In one common form, one hand is placed high, as if on the top or back of the head, and the other low, as if on the chin or jaw. The body turns sharply, and the hands rotate in opposite directions, turning the imagined head. In another form, one hand grips the opponent's arm and the other presses on their shoulder or neck, and the turn of the body drives them down.

Whatever the form, the principle is the same: the opponent's balance is broken by rotating a part of their body that the rest must follow, using the turn and the retreat of the defender's body to supply the force.

::: note
**Note: Head and neck techniques**

Techniques that twist the head and neck are among the most dangerous in karate and can cause catastrophic injury. In training, they must never be applied with force or speed. Practise them only with the hands placed lightly on the partner's head and shoulder, moving slowly, with the partner moving with the technique and falling under their own control. Many teachers prefer to practise the takedown with the hands on the arm and shoulder instead, which teaches the same principle of turning the body more safely.
:::

## Group five: the close

The kata returns to the starting point and finishes with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi. In Kururunfa the closing mawashi uke feels especially at home, since the kata has spent so much of its time in the closing stance.

## Timing and the moment

Kururunfa is above all a kata about timing. Its stillness and its sudden action are two halves of a single skill: the ability to wait without tension and to act without hesitation at exactly the right moment.

In fighting, this skill is invaluable. An opponent who is attacking you is committing to a movement. At the moment they commit, they cannot easily change direction. If you act at that moment, they cannot respond. If you act too early, they can adjust; if too late, their attack has already arrived. The right moment is often very short.

Kururunfa trains you to find it. During the still moments, imagine an opponent in front of you, preparing to attack. Wait, relaxed and alert, for the moment of their commitment. Then explode. Over time, this practice will sharpen your sense of timing in partner work and in kumite.

::: drill
**Drill: Waiting for the moment**

Stand in neko ashi dachi facing a partner, at a distance where they can reach you with a step and a punch. Your partner will attack at a time of their choosing, with a single straight punch. Your task is to wait, perfectly still, and then respond with a single technique from Kururunfa, such as a front kick or a palm strike, at the instant they commit to the attack.

Begin with your partner attacking slowly and obviously. As your timing improves, ask them to attack more quickly and with less warning. Pay attention to the signs that tell you they are about to attack: a shift of weight, a change of breath, a slight movement of the shoulder.
:::

## Applications

### The open-hand control and palm strike

The opening group's application is a deflection and control of an attacking arm, followed by a palm strike. The partner punches or grabs; the defender, in neko ashi dachi, deflects the arm with kake uke and grips it, pressing with the other hand; the defender then strikes with the palm to the partner's jaw, chest or ribs. Because the defender is in neko ashi dachi, they can also kick with the front foot at any point.

### The kick and the double palm

The application of group two is a counter to an opponent who is closing in. The defender, waiting in neko ashi dachi, kicks at the moment the partner commits, striking the groin, the abdomen or the knee. As the partner bends forward from the kick, the defender lands and strikes with one or both palms to the head or chest.

### The double palm in shiko dachi

The double palm strike of group three can be used against an opponent at close range, particularly one who has gripped the defender or is pressing into them. The defender drops into shiko dachi, which lowers their centre and breaks any upward pull, and drives both palms into the partner's body, one to the chest or jaw and one to the abdomen. The combination of the drop and the double strike can send the partner backward with great force.

### The turning takedown

The application of group four, practised safely, uses the partner's arm and shoulder rather than the head. The partner grips the defender's jacket. The defender takes the partner's wrist with one hand and places the other on the partner's shoulder or upper arm. The defender turns and steps back into neko ashi dachi, rotating the partner's arm and pressing down on the shoulder, so that the partner is turned and drawn down to the floor.

## Training Kururunfa

**Master the cat.** Practise neko ashi dachi until you can hold it perfectly still for thirty seconds on each side and move from it instantly.

**Practise the still cat.** Use the still cat drill at every Kururunfa session.

**Separate the rhythms.** Practise the slow, still sections and the explosive sections separately, then join them, paying attention to the transitions.

**Train the timing.** Practise the waiting for the moment drill with a partner regularly.

**Practise the takedowns safely.** Practise the turning takedown with the hands on the arm and shoulder, slowly, on mats, under supervision.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Kururunfa means "holding on long and striking suddenly". It alternates long moments of alert stillness with explosive action.
- It is dominated by neko ashi dachi, which must be held perfectly still and left instantly.
- The opening group is slow and precise, ending in stillness. The second group breaks the stillness with a kick and palm strikes.
- The double palm strikes in shiko dachi combine shoulder power with the drop of the body.
- The turning takedown breaks the opponent's balance by rotating part of their body. It must be practised with great care.
- Kururunfa trains timing: waiting without tension and acting without hesitation at the moment of the opponent's commitment.
:::

::: practice
### Practice Tasks

1. Practise holding neko ashi dachi perfectly still for thirty seconds on each side, three times, at every session for a month.
2. Practise the still cat drill ten times on each side, with a partner watching for telegraphing.
3. Practise the double palm strike in shiko dachi against a heavy bag or a pad, ten times on each side.
4. With a partner, practise the waiting for the moment drill for five minutes at every Kururunfa session.
5. On mats and under supervision, practise the turning takedown using the arm and shoulder, slowly, ten times on each side.
6. Perform Kururunfa with the slow sections slower and the fast sections faster than you normally would. Note what changes.
:::

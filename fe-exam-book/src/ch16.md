# Exam-Day Strategy and the Reference Handbook

::: {.objectives}
- The three-pass method for managing 110 questions in 320 minutes
- Recognising question types and the fastest route to each answer
- Searching the *FE Reference Handbook* efficiently
- Calculator techniques that save minutes, not seconds
- Educated guessing and the use of answer options
- Managing fatigue, nerves and the scheduled break
- What to do in the last week, the night before and on the morning
- A 56-question timed drill across every knowledge area
:::

By now you have worked through more than five hundred questions across every knowledge area of the exam. You know the material. This chapter is about something different: delivering what you know, at the right speed, for six hours, in an unfamiliar room, with your future riding on the result.

That is a skill in its own right, and it is the one most candidates practise least. I have seen strong engineers fail because they spent twelve minutes on question fourteen and then rushed the last twenty questions in a panic. I have seen less polished candidates pass comfortably because they knew exactly when to move on, where to find a formula, and how to keep their heads. The difference between them was not knowledge. It was strategy.

## Thinking About Time

The arithmetic from Chapter 1 bears repeating: 320 minutes for 110 questions is **2 minutes 55 seconds per question** on average. But "average" hides the real shape of the exam. Questions are not equally difficult, and your time should not be spent equally.

Roughly speaking, the questions fall into three groups:

- **Quick wins** (about 40%): definitions, single-formula calculations, conversions and recall. These should take one to two minutes each.
- **Standard problems** (about 45%): two- or three-step calculations of the kind you have practised throughout this book. Three to four minutes each.
- **Long or unfamiliar problems** (about 15%): multi-step problems, unusual geometries, topics you are weak on. Five minutes or more, if you attempt them at all on the first pass.

If you answer the quick wins briskly, you bank time for the harder questions. If you get bogged down early, you borrow time from the end of the exam, when you are most tired. Figure 16.1 shows a sensible time budget for one section of 55 questions.

That leaves about fifteen minutes for flagged questions. Glance at the on-screen clock at each checkpoint. If you are behind, speed up by flagging more aggressively; if you are ahead, use the time to double-check calculations. Do not look at the clock after every question—that breeds anxiety without improving pace.

## The Three-Pass Method

The most effective single technique for managing time is to make more than one pass through each section. The exam interface lets you **flag** questions for review, and you should use that feature without hesitation. Figure 16.2 shows the method.

![The three-pass method. Answer what you can quickly, flag what will take time, and return to flagged questions with the time you have banked. Never leave a question unanswered.](figures/fig16_triage.png){width=88%}

**Pass one.** Read each question once. If you can see the route to the answer, solve it. If you can see the route but it will take more than about four minutes, make a quick best guess, flag it and move on. If you have no idea, guess, flag it and move on. Do not wrestle with anything on the first pass.

**Pass two.** Return to the flagged questions in order. With the pressure of the unseen questions gone, you will often find that a question that baffled you an hour ago now looks routine—your mind has been chewing on it in the background. Work the long problems you deferred.

**Pass three.** With any remaining time, revisit questions where you were uncertain, check units and decimal points, and make sure every question has an answer.

The rule that matters most: **always enter an answer before you move on**, even if it is a guess you intend to revisit. If time runs out, an educated guess scores 25% on average; a blank scores nothing.

::: {.trap}
Do not change answers on the third pass without a clear reason. Studies of multiple-choice exams show that second thoughts help only when you have spotted a specific error. A vague feeling that another option "looks better" is more likely to turn a right answer into a wrong one.
:::

## Reading the Question

Many marks are lost before a single calculation is done, because the candidate answered a different question from the one asked. A few habits prevent this:

- **Read the last sentence first.** It tells you what is being asked for—a current, a power, an angle, a percentage—so that you read the rest of the question looking for the right information.
- **Underline the qualifiers mentally.** *RMS* or *peak*? *Line* or *phase*? *Sample* or *population*? *Maximum* or *average*? *Lagging* or *leading*? Each is a fork in the road.
- **Note the units of the answer options** before you calculate. If the options are in milliamps, you know the order of magnitude to expect.
- **Distrust extra information.** The exam sometimes includes data you do not need—a power factor in a question about resistance, or a frequency in a DC problem. Unneeded data can tempt you down the wrong path.

## Using the Answer Options

The options are information. Use it.

**Estimate first.** If you can bracket the answer roughly—"it must be a few hundred ohms"—you can often eliminate two options immediately, and you have a powerful check on your final calculation.

**Look at the spacing.** When options differ by factors of $\sqrt2$, $\sqrt3$, 2, $2\pi$ or 1000, the question is testing a specific trap: peak versus RMS, line versus phase, a missing half, $\omega$ versus $f$, or a unit prefix. Throughout this book the distractors have been built from exactly these errors, so you have already learned to recognise them.

**Check dimensions and limits.** For a formula-choice question, test each option at an extreme: what happens as $R\to0$, or $\omega\to\infty$? An option that gives an infinite current when the resistance is infinite is wrong.

**Back-substitute.** For algebraic questions—"which value of $x$ satisfies…"—it is sometimes faster to substitute the options than to solve the equation.

### Educated guessing

When you must guess, guess intelligently. Eliminate any option that is dimensionally impossible, physically absurd or the wrong sign. Between the remaining options, prefer the one consistent with your rough estimate. Avoid options that exactly match an intermediate value you calculated on the way—a sign that the question setter anticipated someone stopping one step early. Even eliminating one option raises your expected score on a guess from 25% to 33%; eliminating two raises it to 50%.

## Mastering the Reference Handbook

The Handbook is your only formula sheet on the day, and it appears as a searchable PDF beside the question. Treat learning to navigate it as a study topic in its own right.

**Know its structure.** The Handbook is organised by discipline, and the sections relevant to you include Units and Conversion Factors, Mathematics, Probability and Statistics, Ethics and Professional Practice, Engineering Economics, Electrical and Computer Engineering (by far the largest section for you), and parts of Instrumentation and Controls. Within the electrical section, topics appear in roughly the same order as this book. Spend an hour scrolling through the relevant sections so that you know what is there and roughly where.

**Search for distinctive words.** The search function finds exact text. Searching for "power" will return hundreds of hits; searching for "Carson" or "skin depth" or "per-unit" goes straight to the target. Before the exam, make a list of the distinctive search terms for each topic you might need, and test them in the PDF.

**Learn the Handbook's notation.** The Handbook sometimes uses symbols or forms that differ from your textbook: different subscripts, a different definition of a constant, a formula arranged differently. If you have practised with the Handbook's version, you will not lose time translating on the day.

**Do not search for what you know.** If you know a formula cold—Ohm's law, the voltage divider, $P = \sqrt3V_LI_L\cos\theta$—do not waste a minute confirming it. Save the Handbook for what you genuinely need to look up.

::: {.handbook}
Bookmark the following sections mentally and practise finding each one in under thirty seconds: the unit normal table; the interest-factor tables; the Laplace transform pairs; the second-order control-system response formulae; the three-phase relationships; the transmission-line equations; the op-amp configurations; the flip-flop characteristic tables; the table of average and RMS values. Together they answer a large share of the exam's lookup questions.
:::

## Calculator Technique

Your calculator should be second nature by now. A few techniques save real time:

- **Complex mode** for every AC circuit problem. Enter impedances in polar or rectangular form as convenient, and let the calculator convert.
- **Equation solver** for simultaneous equations from mesh or nodal analysis.
- **Memory registers** for intermediate values. Retyping a six-figure intermediate result is slow and error-prone; store it.
- **Answer recall** (the "Ans" key) to chain calculations.
- **Statistics mode** for mean and standard deviation of a data set.
- **Base conversion** (on models that have it) for number-system questions.

And one ritual: **check degree and radian mode** before any question involving angles. Put a small mark at the top of your writing booklet to remind yourself.

## Managing Yourself

Six hours is a long time to concentrate. Your mental state at question ninety matters as much as at question one.

**The break.** Take the scheduled break between the two sections, even if you feel fine. Leave the room, drink water, eat something with slow-release energy, and do not think about the questions you have just answered—they are submitted and beyond your control. Ten minutes of real rest will repay itself in the second section.

**Nerves.** Everyone feels them. A little adrenaline sharpens performance; too much clouds thinking. If you feel panic rising, stop for thirty seconds: put the marker down, take three slow breaths, and remind yourself that you have prepared for exactly this. Then skip to a question you know you can answer, and rebuild momentum.

**A bad patch.** At some point you will meet a run of questions you cannot do. This is normal and says nothing about your overall result. Every candidate experiences it. The questions are not presented in order of difficulty, and a cluster of hard ones is usually followed by easier ground. Flag, guess and move on.

**Fatigue.** If your concentration flags late in a section, slow your reading slightly and lean harder on the checks: units, signs, magnitudes. Tired candidates make arithmetic and reading errors, not conceptual ones.

## The Last Week and the Day Itself

**The last week.** Sit your final mock exam no later than five days before the real one, under strict timed conditions. Spend the following days repairing what it reveals and rereading your error log and the formula summary in Appendix A. Do not start new topics. Taper your study in the final two days, as an athlete tapers training before a race.

**The night before.** Pack your identification and calculator (with a spare battery if it uses one). Check the test centre's location and how long it takes to get there. Then stop studying, eat well and sleep. A tired brain cannot retrieve what a rested brain knows perfectly well.

**The morning.** Eat a proper breakfast. Arrive at least thirty minutes early: check-in includes identification, photographs and security procedures, and you do not want to begin flustered. Leave everything you are not allowed to take into the room in the locker provided.

**In the first five minutes.** Use the tutorial time to settle in: adjust your chair, check the screen, find the Handbook search box and the flag button, and write your personal reminders at the top of the writing booklet—"peak or RMS?", "√3!", "DEG mode", "convert units", "never leave a blank". Then begin.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *Question 37 on your first pass asks for the line current drawn by a three-phase induction motor of 50 hp, 460 V, 0.88 power factor and 92% efficiency.*

**Triage.** You recognise it immediately: a standard power problem with one trap (efficiency). It should take about two minutes. Solve it now.

**Solve.** Output power $50\times746 = 37.3$ kW. Input power $37.3/0.92 = 40.5$ kW. Line current $I = 40\,500/(\sqrt3\times460\times0.88) = 57.8$ A.

**Check the options.** If the options include 53.2 A (efficiency ignored), 100 A (the $\sqrt3$ omitted) and 33.4 A (an extra $\sqrt3$), you can see exactly which slips the question is built to catch—and you know you have avoided all three.

**Move on.** Two minutes, a confident answer and no flag. Questions like this are the backbone of a passing score.

## A Personal Exam-Day Card

Before the exam, write your own one-page card of reminders—not formulae, which are in the Handbook, but the habits that protect you from your own typical mistakes, drawn from your error log. On exam day you cannot take the card in, but you can reproduce it from memory on the first page of your writing booklet during the tutorial minutes. Writing it out settles the nerves, and having it in front of you for six hours is surprisingly powerful.

## The Timed Drill

The 56 questions that follow are mixed across every knowledge area in roughly the proportions of the real exam—about half a section. Set a timer for **2 hours 40 minutes** and work them as you would on the day, using the three-pass method and the Handbook. Do not look at the solutions until you have finished all 56.

When you mark the drill, record two things for each question: whether you got it right, and roughly how long it took. Questions that took more than five minutes, even if you got them right, deserve a line in your error log: they are where your exam-day time will go.

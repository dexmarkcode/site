Every communication system—a broadcast radio, a mobile phone, a fibre-optic backbone, a Wi-Fi link—solves the same problem: how to move information from one place to another through a channel that is noisy, limited in bandwidth and shared with other users. The FE exam's five to eight communications questions test whether you understand the main tools for solving it and can carry out the standard calculations: the power in an AM signal, the bandwidth of an FM signal, the capacity of a noisy channel, the noise in a receiver and the power arriving at the end of a radio link.

Communications draws heavily on Chapter 7. The spectrum of a signal, the effect of multiplying by a cosine, the sampling theorem and the decibel are all in daily use here. If any of those still feel shaky, it is worth rereading the relevant sections first.

## The Communication System

A communication system has three parts: a **transmitter** that converts the message into a signal suited to the channel, the **channel** itself, which attenuates, distorts and adds noise, and a **receiver** that recovers the message. The central figure of merit is the **signal-to-noise ratio** (SNR) at the receiver, the ratio of signal power to noise power, usually in decibels.

Why modulate at all? A speech signal occupies roughly 300 Hz to 3.4 kHz. Transmitting it directly from an antenna would require an antenna comparable in size to its wavelength—tens of kilometres—and every station would occupy the same band. **Modulation** shifts the message to a high carrier frequency, where antennas are practical and different users can occupy different frequency slots.

## Amplitude Modulation

In conventional AM, the carrier's amplitude follows the message. For a sinusoidal message of frequency $f_m$:

$$s(t) = A_c\left[1 + m\cos(2\pi f_mt)\right]\cos(2\pi f_ct)$$

where $m$ is the **modulation index**. Figure 12.1 shows the waveform: the envelope traces the message as long as $m \le 1$. Beyond that, **overmodulation** distorts the envelope and the simple envelope detector fails.

![An AM waveform with modulation index 0.5. The envelope (dashed) follows the message. The modulation index can be measured from the maximum and minimum envelope amplitudes.](figures/fig12_am.png){width=90%}

From an oscilloscope trace, the modulation index is

$$m = \frac{V_{max} - V_{min}}{V_{max} + V_{min}}$$

using either peak or peak-to-peak envelope values, provided both are measured the same way.

### Spectrum and bandwidth

Expanding the product with the identity $\cos A\cos B = \frac12[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]$ reveals three components: the carrier at $f_c$ and two **sidebands** at $f_c \pm f_m$, as Figure 12.2 shows. For a message of bandwidth $W$, the AM bandwidth is therefore

$$B_{AM} = 2W$$

A 5 kHz audio signal needs 10 kHz of spectrum, which is why medium-wave broadcast stations are spaced roughly 9 or 10 kHz apart.

![The spectrum of an AM signal: a carrier with upper and lower sidebands. Each sideband carries the full message, which is why single-sideband transmission can halve the bandwidth.](figures/fig12_spectrum.png){width=82%}

### Power

The total power of an AM signal with sinusoidal modulation is

$$P_t = P_c\left(1 + \frac{m^2}{2}\right)$$

where $P_c$ is the unmodulated carrier power. The sidebands together carry $P_cm^2/2$, split equally, so each carries $P_cm^2/4$. Even at 100% modulation only one third of the total power is in the sidebands—and only the sidebands carry information. Conventional AM is wasteful, but its receivers are cheap and simple, which explains its long life in broadcasting.

### DSB-SC and SSB

Suppressing the carrier gives **double-sideband suppressed-carrier** (DSB-SC) modulation, which puts all the power into the sidebands but requires a more complex coherent receiver. Removing one sideband as well gives **single-sideband** (SSB), which needs only bandwidth $W$ and is widely used in long-distance HF radio. **Vestigial sideband** (VSB), a compromise, was used for analogue television.

## Frequency and Phase Modulation

In **frequency modulation**, the instantaneous frequency of the carrier varies with the message:

$$f_i(t) = f_c + k_fm(t)$$

The maximum departure from $f_c$ is the **peak frequency deviation** $\Delta f$. For a sinusoidal message, the **modulation index** is

$$\beta = \frac{\Delta f}{f_m}$$

Unlike AM, an FM signal has an infinite number of sidebands in principle, at $f_c \pm nf_m$, with amplitudes given by Bessel functions. In practice almost all the power lies within a finite band, estimated by **Carson's rule**:

$$B_{FM} \approx 2(\Delta f + f_m) = 2f_m(\beta + 1)$$

Commercial FM broadcasting uses $\Delta f = 75$ kHz and $f_m$ up to 15 kHz, giving $B \approx 180$ kHz, within the 200 kHz channel spacing.

FM's great advantage is noise immunity. Noise mostly disturbs a signal's amplitude, which an FM receiver ignores, and wideband FM trades extra bandwidth for improved output SNR. The total power of an FM signal is constant, equal to the unmodulated carrier power, regardless of the modulation—a useful conceptual point.

**Phase modulation** varies the carrier's phase in proportion to the message. PM and FM are closely related: frequency is the derivative of phase, so FM of a message is PM of its integral.

## Pulse-Code Modulation

To transmit an analogue signal digitally, it is sampled (at least at the Nyquist rate, Chapter 7), quantised to one of $2^n$ levels, and each level coded as an $n$-bit word: **pulse-code modulation** (PCM). The resulting bit rate is

$$R_b = n f_s\ \text{bit/s}$$

per channel. Telephone-quality speech sampled at 8 kHz with 8 bits per sample needs 64 kbit/s—the basic rate of the digital telephone network. CD audio, at 44.1 kHz, 16 bits and two channels, needs 1.41 Mbit/s.

Each extra bit per sample improves the signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio by about 6 dB but increases the bit rate, and hence the bandwidth, by $1/n$. That is the fundamental trade-off of digital transmission.

## Digital Modulation

Digital modulation maps bits onto changes in a carrier's amplitude, frequency or phase:

- **ASK** (amplitude-shift keying) switches the amplitude, often simply on and off (OOK).
- **FSK** (frequency-shift keying) switches between frequencies.
- **PSK** (phase-shift keying) switches the phase. BPSK uses two phases (1 bit per symbol); QPSK uses four (2 bits per symbol).
- **QAM** (quadrature amplitude modulation) varies both amplitude and phase. 16-QAM has 16 points in its **constellation**, carrying 4 bits per symbol; 64-QAM carries 6; 256-QAM carries 8.

Figure 12.3 shows a 16-QAM constellation. The general relationship between symbol rate $R_s$ (in baud) and bit rate is

$$R_b = R_s\log_2M$$

where $M$ is the number of distinct symbols.

![A 16-QAM constellation. Each point represents one of 16 symbols, carrying 4 bits. Points closer together pack in more bits per symbol but are more easily confused by noise.](figures/fig12_qam.png){width=62%}

Higher-order modulation packs more bits into the same bandwidth, but the constellation points are closer together, so a higher SNR is needed for the same error rate. Modern systems adapt their modulation to the channel conditions, dropping from 256-QAM to QPSK as the signal fades.

## Channel Capacity

Two classic results bound the data rate of a channel of bandwidth $B$.

For a noiseless channel using $M$ signal levels, **Nyquist's** limit is

$$C = 2B\log_2M$$

For a channel with additive white Gaussian noise, **Shannon's** capacity theorem gives the maximum error-free rate, whatever the modulation:

$$C = B\log_2(1 + \text{SNR})$$

with SNR as a linear ratio, **not** in decibels. A 1 MHz channel with an SNR of 30 dB (a ratio of 1000) has a capacity of $10^6\log_2(1001) \approx 9.97$ Mbit/s. Shannon's limit can be approached by good coding but never exceeded.

::: {.trap}
In the Shannon formula, convert the SNR from decibels to a ratio first: $\text{SNR} = 10^{\text{dB}/10}$. Substituting "30" directly gives $\log_2 31 \approx 4.95$, half the correct capacity in this example, and a tempting distractor. Also note that the logarithm is base 2.
:::

## Noise

### Thermal noise

Every resistor at a temperature above absolute zero generates random noise. The available noise power in a bandwidth $B$ is

$$N = kTB$$

where $k = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann's constant. At the standard reference temperature $T_0 = 290$ K, $kT_0 = 4\times10^{-21}$ W/Hz, which is $-174$ dBm/Hz. So in decibel form,

$$N_{dBm} = -174 + 10\log_{10}B$$

A 1 MHz bandwidth therefore has a noise floor of $-174 + 60 = -114$ dBm. Narrowing the bandwidth reduces the noise in proportion, which is why receivers use the narrowest bandwidth the signal allows.

### Noise figure

Real amplifiers add noise of their own. The **noise factor** $F$ is the ratio of input SNR to output SNR (always at least 1), and the **noise figure** is $NF = 10\log_{10}F$ dB. For a cascade of stages, **Friis's formula** gives the overall noise factor:

$$F = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1G_2} + \cdots$$

with all quantities as linear ratios. The first stage dominates: a low-noise, high-gain first amplifier masks the noise of everything after it. This is why a **low-noise amplifier** (LNA) is placed as close to the antenna as possible.

## Link Budgets

A link budget tracks power from transmitter to receiver in decibels, where gains and losses simply add:

$$P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L_{path} - L_{other}$$

with powers in dBm or dBW, antenna gains in dBi, and losses in dB. The **effective isotropic radiated power** is $\text{EIRP} = P_t + G_t$ (minus any feeder losses).

In free space, the path loss is

$$L_{fs} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right) = 20\log_{10}d_{km} + 20\log_{10}f_{MHz} + 32.44\ \text{dB}$$

Each doubling of distance adds 6 dB of loss, as does each doubling of frequency (for fixed-gain antennas). Comparing the received power with the noise floor gives the SNR, and comparing the SNR with what the modulation needs gives the **link margin**.

::: {.example}
A 2.4 GHz link over 2 km uses a 20 dBm transmitter and two 6 dBi antennas.

$L_{fs} = 20\log(2) + 20\log(2400) + 32.44 = 6.02 + 67.6 + 32.44 = 106.1$ dB.

$P_r = 20 + 6 + 6 - 106.1 = -74.1$ dBm. With a 20 MHz bandwidth, the noise floor is $-174 + 73 = -101$ dBm, before adding the receiver's noise figure, so the SNR is about 27 dB minus that noise figure.
:::

## Multiplexing and Multiple Access

Several signals can share one channel by dividing it:

- **Frequency-division multiplexing** (FDM) gives each signal its own frequency band, separated by guard bands. Broadcast radio and cable television use FDM; **OFDM**, used in Wi-Fi and 4G/5G, is a sophisticated variant with many overlapping orthogonal subcarriers.
- **Time-division multiplexing** (TDM) gives each signal its own time slot in a repeating frame. The classic example is the T1 carrier, which multiplexes 24 telephone channels of 64 kbit/s, plus framing, into 1.544 Mbit/s.
- **Code-division multiple access** (CDMA) lets every user transmit across the whole band simultaneously, each spread by a unique code; the receiver separates them by correlation.

## Receivers

The **superheterodyne** receiver, used in almost all radios for a century, mixes the incoming signal with a local oscillator to shift it to a fixed **intermediate frequency** (IF), where sharp filters and most of the gain are provided. Tuning simply changes the local oscillator frequency. Its known weakness is the **image frequency**, $2f_{IF}$ away from the wanted signal, which also mixes down to the IF and must be rejected by filtering before the mixer.

In digital receivers, the **matched filter**—whose impulse response is the time-reversed transmitted pulse—maximises the SNR at the sampling instant and is the optimum detector in white noise.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A receiver has a 200 kHz bandwidth and an overall noise figure of 8 dB. What minimum received signal power gives an SNR of 15 dB at the output?*

**Noise floor at the input.** $N = -174 + 10\log_{10}(200\,000) = -174 + 53.0 = -121.0$ dBm.

**Account for the receiver.** The noise figure degrades the SNR by 8 dB, so it is as though the noise floor were $-121.0 + 8 = -113.0$ dBm.

**Required signal.** For a 15 dB SNR, $S = -113.0 + 15 = -98.0$ dBm.

**Reflect.** Everything is addition and subtraction in decibels. Converting to watts and back would give the same answer, but with three times the chance of an arithmetic slip. Stay in dB whenever the question allows.

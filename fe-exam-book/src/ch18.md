# Mock Exam Two and Final Readiness

::: {.objectives}
- A second complete 110-question mock exam, with entirely different questions
- How to compare your two mock results and judge your readiness
- A final-week plan and a last-minute review checklist
- What to do if the real exam does not go your way
- Next steps after you pass: EI certification and the road to the PE
:::

If you are reading this chapter, you have come a long way. You have rebuilt your mathematics, worked through every knowledge area on the specification, solved several hundred problems, sat a full mock exam and repaired what it revealed. This second mock exam is your final check: an independent measurement, with a completely new set of questions, of whether you are ready.

Sit it under exactly the same conditions as Mock Exam One—two timed sections of 2 hours 40 minutes, a break between them, the Handbook on screen, your approved calculator and nothing else. Ideally, sit it about a week before your real exam: late enough to reflect your full preparation, early enough to leave time for a final round of repairs.

## Comparing Your Two Results

When you have marked this exam and completed the scoring sheet, set it beside the sheet from Mock Exam One. Three comparisons are worth making.

**The total.** An improvement of five to ten percentage points between the two mocks is typical for candidates who repaired their weak areas thoroughly. If your score has held steady or fallen, look at the areas that dropped: often a strong area has gone stale while you worked on weaker ones, which is a reminder that mixed review matters right to the end.

**The weak areas.** Did the areas you repaired after Mock One improve? If so, the method is working; repeat it for any new weaknesses. If not, the repair was probably too passive—rereading rather than solving. Make the next round active: work problems from scratch, without looking at the solutions until you have committed to an answer.

**The error categories.** Compare how many errors were knowledge gaps, method errors, careless slips and time problems. By the second mock, knowledge gaps should be shrinking. If careless slips remain your biggest category, the cure is not more study but more care: slower reading of the question, a sanity check on every answer, and your exam-day reminders written at the top of the booklet.

## Are You Ready?

There is no certainty in any exam, but the following signs are good indicators of readiness:

- You scored comfortably above about 70% on Mock Exam Two under strict timing.
- You finished each section with a few minutes in hand for flagged questions.
- No knowledge area fell below about half marks.
- You can find the key tables and formulae in the Handbook in under a minute.
- Your error log shows the same mistakes appearing less and less often.

If most of these are true, go ahead with confidence. If several are not, consider whether moving your exam date back by two or three weeks would let you close the gap. There is no shame in postponing; there is real cost in sitting the exam before you are ready.

## The Final Week

The final week is for consolidation, not new learning.

- **Days 7–5:** Sit Mock Exam Two (if you have not already) and carry out the full post-mortem from Chapter 17.
- **Days 4–3:** Targeted repair of the weakest two or three areas. Rework the questions you got wrong in both mock exams.
- **Day 2:** Light review only. Read your error log, the chapter summaries and Appendix A. Run through your Handbook search terms. Work ten or fifteen mixed questions to stay sharp, and stop while you are getting them right.
- **Day 1:** No serious study. Prepare your identification and calculator, confirm the route and timing to the test centre, eat well and go to bed at a sensible time.

::: {.checklist title="Last-Minute Review"}
- Peak or RMS? Line or phase? $\omega$ or $f$?
- $\sqrt3$ appears in three-phase power and in wye voltages and delta currents
- Calculator in the right angle mode; complex mode for AC
- Units converted to base SI before substituting
- Sample standard deviation divides by $n - 1$
- Shannon's formula uses SNR as a ratio, not in dB
- Steady-state error: $1/(1 + K_p)$ for a step, $1/K_v$ for a ramp
- Usable hosts on a subnet: $2^h - 2$
- Public safety is paramount
- Never leave a question blank
:::

## If It Does Not Go Your Way

Some readers will not pass at the first attempt. If that happens to you, it is disappointing, but it is not a verdict on your ability as an engineer. Many excellent engineers have needed two attempts, and the second attempt is almost always much stronger, for good reasons: you have seen the real exam, you know how it feels, and you have a diagnostic report showing exactly where the marks were lost.

When you receive your result, give yourself a day or two. Then sit down with the diagnostic report, which shows your performance in each knowledge area relative to what passing candidates typically achieve. Compare it with your mock-exam scoring sheets. Usually, the weak areas on the report will match the weak areas in your mocks, and you will know exactly what to do: repair those areas with the methods in this book, resit both mock exams under strict conditions, and book your next attempt as soon as the rules allow. Check the current NCEES retake policy before you plan.

If the report shows weaknesses spread evenly across many areas, the issue is more likely to have been time management or nerves than knowledge. In that case, put extra effort into Chapter 16 and into sitting timed sets of mixed questions until your pacing is automatic.

## After You Pass

When the email arrives telling you that you have passed, celebrate properly: it is a real achievement. Then take care of the practical steps.

**Apply for your EI or EIT designation.** In most states, passing the FE exam with an approved degree allows you to apply to the state board for Engineer Intern or Engineer-in-Training status. Requirements and procedures vary, so check with the board in the state where you intend to practise.

**Set up your NCEES Record.** An NCEES Record stores your verified education, exam results, experience and references in one place. It makes applying for licensure—and later for licences in other states by comity—far simpler.

**Start documenting your experience now.** Most states require around four years of progressive engineering experience under the supervision of a licensed engineer before you can sit the PE exam. Keep a simple log of your projects, your responsibilities and your supervisors from the start. Reconstructing four years of experience from memory later is far harder.

**Keep the habits.** The study methods that carried you through this exam—spaced practice, an error log, timed mock exams and active problem-solving—work just as well for the PE. When the time comes, you will know how to prepare.

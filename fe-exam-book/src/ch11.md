Control engineering is about making systems do what we want, reliably, despite disturbances and imperfect knowledge of the plant. A thermostat holding a room at 21 °C, a cruise control holding a car at 70 mph, a voltage regulator holding a generator's terminal voltage steady while load swings: each measures what it is doing, compares it with what it should be doing, and corrects the difference. That loop of measurement and correction is **feedback**, and this chapter is its toolkit.

The knowledge area is worth six to nine questions. Like signal processing, it is pleasantly formulaic. The transfer functions on the exam are almost always first- or second-order, the questions ask for a handful of standard quantities, and the relevant relationships are in the Handbook. What distinguishes a fast candidate from a slow one is the ability to look at $\omega_n^2/(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_ns + \omega_n^2)$ and read off $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$ in a few seconds, and to know which formula answers which question. That is what we will practise.

## Open Loop and Closed Loop

An **open-loop** controller acts without measuring the result: a toaster runs for a set time whether the bread is pale or black. It is simple and cannot become unstable through its own action, but it cannot correct for disturbances or for errors in its model of the plant.

A **closed-loop** (feedback) controller measures the output, compares it with the desired value (the **reference** or **set point**) and acts on the difference (the **error**). Figure 11.1 shows the standard structure.

![The standard negative-feedback loop. The controller and plant form the forward path $G(s)$; the sensor forms the feedback path $H(s)$. The summing junction subtracts the measured output from the reference.](figures/fig11_feedback.png){width=88%}

Feedback brings four great benefits: it reduces sensitivity to changes in the plant, it rejects disturbances, it can speed up a sluggish system, and it can stabilise an unstable one. Its one great danger is that it can also *destabilise* a stable system if the loop gain is too high or the delays too long. Much of control theory is about getting the benefits without the danger.

## Block-Diagram Algebra

Systems are represented by blocks containing transfer functions, joined by signal lines. Three rules reduce almost any diagram:

- **Blocks in series** multiply: $G_1G_2$.
- **Blocks in parallel** add: $G_1 + G_2$.
- **A negative-feedback loop** with forward path $G$ and feedback path $H$ reduces to
$$T(s) = \frac{C(s)}{R(s)} = \frac{G(s)}{1 + G(s)H(s)}$$

This last result is the single most important formula in the chapter. For positive feedback, the plus in the denominator becomes a minus. The product $G(s)H(s)$ is the **open-loop transfer function** or **loop gain**, and $1 + G(s)H(s) = 0$ is the **characteristic equation** whose roots are the closed-loop poles.

When $GH$ is large, $T \approx 1/H$: the closed-loop behaviour is set by the feedback path rather than the forward path. This is why op-amp circuits are so precise (Chapter 8)—their gain depends on resistor ratios rather than on the op-amp's wildly variable open-loop gain.

::: {.tip}
For more complicated diagrams, move summing junctions and pick-off points using the standard rules in the Handbook, or apply Mason's gain formula. On the exam, though, most diagrams reduce with the three rules above in a minute or two. Work from the innermost loop outwards.
:::

## Time Response

### First-order systems

A first-order system has the form

$$G(s) = \frac{K}{\tau s + 1}$$

Its response to a step of size $A$ is $y(t) = KA(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$: it rises to its final value $KA$ with time constant $\tau$, reaching 63.2% after one time constant and settling (within 2%) after about four. The 10–90% **rise time** is $2.2\tau$.

Placing a first-order plant $K/(s + a)$ in a unity-feedback loop gives

$$T(s) = \frac{K}{s + a + K}$$

The closed-loop pole moves from $-a$ to $-(a + K)$: feedback makes the response faster. But the DC gain falls from $K/a$ to $K/(a + K)$, less than 1, so the output never quite reaches the reference. That residual difference is a **steady-state error**, discussed below.

### Second-order systems

The standard second-order closed-loop transfer function is

$$T(s) = \frac{\omega_n^2}{s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_ns + \omega_n^2}$$

with undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$ and damping ratio $\zeta$. To identify them, match coefficients: $\omega_n$ is the square root of the constant term, and $\zeta$ is the coefficient of $s$ divided by $2\omega_n$. For $s^2 + 4s + 25$, $\omega_n = 5$ rad/s and $\zeta = 4/10 = 0.4$.

For an underdamped system ($0 < \zeta < 1$), the step response overshoots and rings, as Figure 11.2 shows. The key specifications, all in the Handbook, are:

| Specification | Formula | Depends on |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Damped frequency | $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ | $\zeta$, $\omega_n$ |
| Peak time | $T_p = \pi/\omega_d$ | $\zeta$, $\omega_n$ |
| Percentage overshoot | $\%OS = 100\,e^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}$ | $\zeta$ only |
| Settling time (2%) | $T_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ | $\zeta\omega_n$ |

Table: Second-order step-response specifications.

![A second-order underdamped step response annotated with peak time, percentage overshoot and settling time. Overshoot depends only on the damping ratio; settling time depends on the real part of the poles, $\zeta\omega_n$.](figures/fig11_step_specs.png){width=88%}

Some reference values for overshoot are worth carrying in your head: $\zeta = 0.3$ gives about 37%; $\zeta = 0.5$ about 16%; $\zeta = 0.7$ about 5%; $\zeta = 1$ none. Many practical control loops are tuned for $\zeta$ between 0.5 and 0.7, accepting a little overshoot in exchange for a brisk response.

Notice the elegant link to the s-plane: the poles are at $s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_d$. The real part sets the decay rate (and so the settling time); the imaginary part sets the oscillation frequency; and the angle of the pole from the negative real axis, $\cos^{-1}\zeta$, sets the overshoot.

## Steady-State Error

How closely does the output follow the reference once transients have died away? For a unity-feedback system, the error $E(s) = R(s)/(1 + G(s))$, and the final value theorem gives

$$e_{ss} = \lim_{s\to0}\frac{sR(s)}{1 + G(s)}$$

The answer depends on the input and on the **system type**: the number of pure integrators (poles at $s = 0$) in the open-loop transfer function $G(s)$.

| System type | Step input | Ramp input | Parabola input |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 0 | $\dfrac{1}{1 + K_p}$ | $\infty$ | $\infty$ |
| 1 | 0 | $\dfrac{1}{K_v}$ | $\infty$ |
| 2 | 0 | 0 | $\dfrac{1}{K_a}$ |

Table: Steady-state error for unit inputs.

with the error constants

$$K_p = \lim_{s\to0}G(s), \qquad K_v = \lim_{s\to0}sG(s), \qquad K_a = \lim_{s\to0}s^2G(s)$$

The pattern is easy to remember. Each integrator in the loop removes the error for one more class of input. A type 0 system has a finite error to a step; a type 1 system follows a step perfectly but lags a ramp; a type 2 system follows a ramp perfectly.

::: {.trap}
For a type 0 system the step error is $1/(1 + K_p)$, not $1/K_p$. For a type 1 system the ramp error is $1/K_v$, not $1/(1 + K_v)$. The "1 +" belongs only to the step case. Both wrong forms appear routinely as distractors.
:::

## Stability

A linear system is stable if all its closed-loop poles lie in the left half of the s-plane, as we established in Chapter 6. For a low-order characteristic equation you can simply find the roots. For higher orders, or when a gain $K$ is to be chosen, the **Routh–Hurwitz criterion** tells you how many roots lie in the right half-plane without solving for them.

### The Routh array

For $a_ns^n + a_{n-1}s^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$, write the coefficients in two rows, alternating, and compute each subsequent row from the two above it. For a cubic $s^3 + a_2s^2 + a_1s + a_0$:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $s^3$ | 1 | $a_1$ |
| $s^2$ | $a_2$ | $a_0$ |
| $s^1$ | $\dfrac{a_2a_1 - a_0}{a_2}$ | 0 |
| $s^0$ | $a_0$ | |

The number of sign changes in the first column equals the number of right-half-plane roots. So a cubic with all positive coefficients is stable if and only if $a_2a_1 > a_0$.

::: {.example}
The characteristic equation $s^3 + 6s^2 + 11s + K = 0$ is stable for $0 < K < 66$. At $K = 66$ the $s^1$ row vanishes; the auxiliary equation from the row above, $6s^2 + 66 = 0$, gives the frequency of the resulting sustained oscillation, $\omega = \sqrt{11} = 3.32$ rad/s.
:::

A necessary (but not sufficient) condition for stability is that every coefficient of the characteristic polynomial is present and has the same sign. If any coefficient is missing or negative, the system is unstable without further work.

## Root Locus

The **root locus** plots how the closed-loop poles move in the s-plane as the loop gain $K$ rises from zero to infinity. Its basic rules, sufficient for the exam, are:

- The locus begins ($K = 0$) at the open-loop **poles** and ends ($K\to\infty$) at the open-loop **zeros** or at infinity.
- It is symmetric about the real axis.
- On the real axis, the locus exists to the left of an odd number of real poles and zeros.
- Branches heading to infinity follow asymptotes centred on $\sigma_a = (\sum\text{poles} - \sum\text{zeros})/(n - m)$, at angles $(2k + 1)180°/(n - m)$.

Figure 11.3 shows the root locus of a simple third-order system. As $K$ rises, two poles approach each other on the real axis, break away into the complex plane—the response becomes oscillatory—and eventually cross the imaginary axis into instability.

![Root locus of $G(s)H(s) = K/[s(s+2)(s+4)]$. Increasing gain first makes the response faster, then oscillatory, and finally unstable where the locus crosses the $j\omega$ axis.](figures/fig11_root_locus.png){width=78%}

Root-locus questions on the exam are usually conceptual: where the branches start and end, whether the system becomes unstable at high gain, or which region a design should target.

## Frequency Response

Instead of a step, drive the system with sinusoids of every frequency and record the gain and phase shift of the output: that is its **frequency response**, $G(j\omega)$. A **Bode plot** shows the magnitude in decibels and the phase in degrees, both against log frequency.

Bode plots are built from simple pieces:

- A constant $K$ contributes a flat $20\log_{10}K$ dB.
- A pole at the origin ($1/s$) contributes a line falling at 20 dB/decade, with constant phase $-90°$.
- A real pole at $s = -a$ is flat below $\omega = a$, then falls at 20 dB/decade; its phase goes from 0° to $-90°$, passing $-45°$ at $\omega = a$.
- A real zero does the opposite: rising 20 dB/decade and adding up to $+90°$.
- A complex pole pair contributes $-40$ dB/decade and $-180°$, with a resonant peak if $\zeta$ is small.

The high-frequency slope is $-20(n - m)$ dB/decade, where $n$ is the number of poles and $m$ the number of zeros. The DC gain of a type 0 system is found by setting $s = 0$.

### Gain and phase margins

The Bode plot of the *open-loop* function $G(j\omega)H(j\omega)$ tells us how close the *closed-loop* system is to instability. Instability occurs where the loop gain is exactly $-1$: magnitude 1 (0 dB) and phase $-180°$. The margins measure the distance from that point, as Figure 11.4 shows:

- **Gain margin:** at the frequency where the phase is $-180°$, how much the gain could rise before reaching 0 dB. $GM = -20\log_{10}|GH|$ at the phase crossover.
- **Phase margin:** at the frequency where the gain is 0 dB, how much additional phase lag would bring the phase to $-180°$. $PM = 180° + \angle GH$ at the gain crossover.

![Bode plot of an open-loop transfer function showing the gain margin (measured at the phase-crossover frequency) and the phase margin (measured at the gain-crossover frequency).](figures/fig11_bode.png){width=90%}

Positive margins mean stability. Typical design targets are a gain margin of at least 6 dB and a phase margin of 30° to 60°. Phase margin is closely related to damping: for a second-order-like closed loop, $\zeta \approx PM/100$ for phase margins up to about 70°.

## PID Control

The proportional–integral–derivative controller is by far the most widely used in industry:

$$G_c(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s} + K_ds$$

Each term has a characteristic effect:

| Term | Main effect | Side effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proportional ($K_p$) | Faster response, smaller steady-state error | More overshoot; cannot remove step error in a type 0 plant |
| Integral ($K_i$) | Removes steady-state error (raises system type) | Slower, more oscillatory; risk of integrator wind-up |
| Derivative ($K_d$) | Adds damping, reduces overshoot | Amplifies high-frequency noise |

Table: The three terms of a PID controller.

In frequency-response terms, a **lead compensator** adds phase near the crossover frequency (like derivative action) to improve the phase margin; a **lag compensator** raises low-frequency gain (like integral action) to reduce steady-state error.

## State-Variable Models

Modern control represents a system by first-order differential equations in matrix form:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}u, \qquad y = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} + Du$$

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$ are the system's poles, so stability can be read from them directly. The transfer function follows from $G(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + D$. Exam questions on state space are usually limited to finding the eigenvalues of a $2\times2$ matrix (Chapter 2) or converting a simple second-order differential equation into state-variable form.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A unity-feedback system has open-loop transfer function $G(s) = K/[s(s + 10)]$. Find the value of $K$ that gives a closed-loop damping ratio of 0.5, and the resulting steady-state error to a unit ramp.*

**Closed loop.** $T(s) = G/(1 + G) = K/(s^2 + 10s + K)$.

**Match to the standard form.** $\omega_n^2 = K$ and $2\zeta\omega_n = 10$. With $\zeta = 0.5$, $\omega_n = 10$ rad/s, so $K = 100$.

**Ramp error.** The open loop has one integrator, so the system is type 1 and the step error is zero. $K_v = \lim_{s\to0}sG(s) = K/10 = 10$, so $e_{ss} = 1/K_v = 0.1$.

**Reflect.** The overshoot is about 16% and the 2% settling time about $4/(0.5\times10) = 0.8$ s. Raising $K$ would cut the ramp error but lower the damping—the classic trade-off between accuracy and stability that compensators are designed to escape.

## Common Traps in Control Systems

**Open-loop versus closed-loop.** Steady-state error constants, system type, gain margin and phase margin are all read from the *open-loop* transfer function $G(s)H(s)$. Overshoot, settling time, natural frequency and damping ratio describe the *closed-loop* response. Many wrong answers come from computing the right quantity from the wrong transfer function.

**System type.** Count only the poles exactly at $s = 0$ in the open-loop function. A pole at $s = -0.01$ is not an integrator, however close to the origin it lies.

**Positive and negative feedback.** The standard formula $G/(1 + GH)$ assumes negative feedback. If a block diagram shows a plus sign at the summing junction for the feedback signal, the denominator becomes $1 - GH$.

**Settling-time criteria.** The approximation $T_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ uses a 2% band; a 5% band gives roughly $3/(\zeta\omega_n)$. If a question specifies the band, use the matching constant.

**Margins with signs.** A negative gain margin or phase margin indicates an unstable closed loop. Read the Bode plot carefully at the correct crossover frequency: gain margin at the phase crossover ($-180°$), phase margin at the gain crossover (0 dB).

**Routh arrays with a zero in the first column.** If a zero appears in the first column but the rest of that row is not zero, replace it with a small positive number $\varepsilon$ and continue. If the entire row is zero, the system has roots symmetric about the origin—often a pair on the imaginary axis—and you form an auxiliary polynomial from the row above. On the FE exam, a whole row of zeros almost always marks the boundary of stability, which is the value of $K$ the question is looking for.

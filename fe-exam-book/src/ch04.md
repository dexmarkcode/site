# DC Circuit Analysis

::: {.objectives}
- Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws and the sign conventions that keep them honest

- Resistance from geometry: resistivity, temperature coefficient and wire sizing

- Maximum power transfer

- Wye–delta conversion and the Wheatstone bridge

When I ask candidates which topic they feel most confident about, the answer is usually DC circuits. When I look at their practice-exam results, DC circuits is often where they are losing the most marks. The reason is not ignorance—everyone knows Ohm's law—but speed and carelessness. A sign slip in a mesh equation, a resistor that looked parallel but was not, a divider applied to a loaded circuit as though it were unloaded: these are the errors that cost marks, and they are the errors this chapter is designed to train out of you.

There is a broader reason to master this material. The techniques you will use here—reducing networks, writing node equations, finding Thévenin equivalents—transfer unchanged to AC circuits in Chapter 5, where resistances simply become impedances. They return in electronics, where a transistor amplifier is analysed with its small-signal equivalent circuit, and in power systems, where a whole grid is reduced to a Thévenin source behind a reactance to calculate fault current. Every minute you invest in DC circuit fluency pays off three or four times over.

## The Laws That Everything Rests On

### Ohm's law and power

For a resistor, voltage and current are proportional:

$$V = IR$$

and the power it absorbs is

$$P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

Choose the form that uses the quantities you already know. If you have current and resistance, use $I^2R$; if you have voltage and resistance, use $V^2/R$. Converting to the third quantity first is a wasted step and a fresh chance to slip.

### The passive sign convention

When current enters the positive terminal of an element, the product $VI$ is the power *absorbed* by that element. A resistor always absorbs power. A source delivering power has current leaving its positive terminal, so by this convention its absorbed power is negative. Keeping to one convention throughout a problem guarantees that the powers balance: the sum of the power absorbed by every element in a circuit is zero. That balance makes an excellent check on a complicated answer.

### Kirchhoff's laws

**Kirchhoff's current law (KCL):** the algebraic sum of currents leaving any node is zero. Charge does not pile up at a junction.

**Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL):** the algebraic sum of voltage drops around any closed loop is zero. Energy per unit charge is conserved around a loop.

These two laws, together with the element equations, are all of circuit analysis. Every method in this chapter is simply an organised way of applying them with as few equations as possible.

## Resistance from Materials

The resistance of a uniform conductor of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$ and resistivity $\rho$ is

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A}$$

For a round wire of diameter $d$, $A = \pi d^2/4$. Copper has a resistivity of about $1.72\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot$m at 20 °C, and aluminium about $2.8\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot$m. Two classic errors appear in exam distractors: using the diameter as though it were the radius (which divides the answer by four) and failing to convert millimetres to metres (which is out by a factor of a million when squared).

Resistance changes with temperature. Over moderate ranges,

$$R_T = R_0\left[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)\right]$$

where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistance at the reference temperature $T_0$. For copper, $\alpha \approx 0.00393$ per °C at 20 °C. Metals have positive coefficients; carbon and semiconductors have negative ones. A useful consequence: the resistance of a copper winding rises by roughly 20% between a cold start and a running temperature of 70 °C, which is how engineers estimate winding temperatures from resistance measurements.

::: {.example}
A wire drawn out to twice its length, with its volume unchanged, has its area halved. Its resistance therefore rises by a factor of $2 \times 2 = 4$. Questions like this test whether you think about *both* geometric changes.
:::

## Series and Parallel Reduction

Resistors in series carry the same current and add:

$$R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_n$$

Resistors in parallel share the same voltage, and their conductances add:

$$\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \cdots, \qquad R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

The product-over-sum shortcut works only for two resistors at a time. A parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest member, which makes an instant sanity check.

The genuinely difficult part of series–parallel reduction is not the arithmetic but the *seeing*. Two elements are in parallel only if they share **both** end nodes. Two elements are in series only if the node between them connects to nothing else. When a circuit is drawn awkwardly, redraw it: label every node with a letter, then place each element between its two lettered nodes. Thirty seconds of redrawing prevents the commonest error in this area.

## The Divider Rules

### Voltage divider

When a voltage $V_s$ is applied across resistors in series, it divides in proportion to the resistances. For two resistors, Figure 4.1:

$$V_2 = V_s\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

![A voltage divider, unloaded (left) and loaded (right). Connecting $R_L$ places it in parallel with $R_2$, so the output must be recalculated using $R_2\parallel R_L$.](figures/fig04_divider.png){width=88%}

::: {.trap}
The divider rule applies only to elements carrying the *same* current. As soon as a load is connected across $R_2$, the load and $R_2$ form a parallel combination, and you must use $R_2\parallel R_L$ in place of $R_2$. The unloaded answer will always be among the options, and it will always be too high.
:::

### Current divider

When a current $I$ divides between two parallel resistors,

$$I_1 = I\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

Notice that the resistor in the numerator is the *other* one. Current prefers the easier path, so the smaller resistor carries the larger share. If your answer gives more current to the larger resistor, you have swapped them.

Both dividers are worth knowing cold. They are not formally listed in the Handbook in this convenient form, and they are faster than any systematic method for the many exam questions that involve only two or three resistors.

## Nodal Analysis

For anything beyond simple series–parallel networks, you need a systematic method. Nodal analysis is usually the better choice.

1. Choose a reference node (ground)—usually the node with the most connections.
2. Label the voltage of every other node.
3. Write KCL at each non-reference node, expressing each branch current as the voltage difference across it divided by its resistance.
4. Solve the resulting equations.

The method produces one equation for each unknown node voltage. Consider Figure 4.2: two sources feed a single central node through $R_1$ and $R_3$, with $R_2$ to ground.

![A two-source circuit. Nodal analysis needs only one equation, at node x, whereas mesh analysis would need two.](figures/fig04_nodal.png){width=80%}

Summing currents *leaving* node x:

$$\frac{V_x - V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_x}{R_2} + \frac{V_x - V_2}{R_3} = 0$$

and therefore

$$V_x = \frac{V_1/R_1 + V_2/R_3}{1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + 1/R_3}$$

This result has a pleasing interpretation, sometimes called **Millman's theorem**: the node voltage is a conductance-weighted average of the source voltages, with the grounded resistor contributing a "source" of 0 V. Once you have seen it, you will solve many two-source questions in a single line.

::: {.tip}
Write every branch current as *leaving* the node, even when you suspect it enters. If the guess is wrong, the current simply comes out negative. Consistency is far more important than guessing directions correctly.
:::

When a voltage source connects two non-reference nodes directly, the current through it cannot be written from Ohm's law. Enclose both nodes in a **supernode**: write one KCL equation for the combined region, and add the constraint that the two node voltages differ by the source voltage.

## Mesh Analysis

Mesh analysis is the dual of nodal analysis. Assign a clockwise current to each window (mesh) of a planar circuit, write KVL around each, and solve for the mesh currents. A resistor shared by two meshes carries the difference of their currents.

For the circuit of Figure 4.2, the two mesh equations are

$$V_1 = I_1R_1 + (I_1 - I_2)R_2, \qquad 0 = I_2R_3 + V_2 + (I_2 - I_1)R_2$$

and the current down through $R_2$ is $I_1 - I_2$.

### Choosing a method

Count the unknowns. A circuit with *n* nodes needs $n - 1$ node equations; a planar circuit with *m* windows needs *m* mesh equations. Choose whichever gives fewer equations. As a rule of thumb, nodal analysis wins when there are many parallel branches and current sources; mesh analysis wins when there are many series elements and voltage sources. On the FE exam, nodal analysis is more often the faster choice.

## Superposition

In a **linear** circuit—one built from resistors, linear dependent sources and independent sources—the response to several sources acting together is the sum of the responses to each source acting alone. To find each contribution:

- turn off every other independent **voltage** source by replacing it with a **short circuit**;
- turn off every other independent **current** source by replacing it with an **open circuit**;
- leave dependent sources in place.

Superposition is rarely the fastest way to find a total answer, but the exam likes to ask for the contribution of one particular source, which you can only get this way. Remember that power does not superpose, because it is not linear in the source values: you must find the total current first, and then calculate $I^2R$.

## Thévenin and Norton Equivalents

Any linear two-terminal network, however complicated, behaves at its terminals exactly like a single voltage source $V_{Th}$ in series with a resistance $R_{Th}$—or, equivalently, a current source $I_N$ in parallel with $R_N = R_{Th}$. Figure 4.3 shows the idea.

![Thévenin and Norton equivalents. Any linear network seen from terminals a–b can be replaced by either form. The two are related by $V_{Th} = I_NR_{Th}$.](figures/fig04_thevenin.png){width=95%}

### Finding the equivalent

1. **Open-circuit voltage.** Remove the load and find the voltage across the open terminals. This is $V_{Th}$.
2. **Equivalent resistance.** Turn off all independent sources (shorts for voltage sources, opens for current sources) and find the resistance seen looking back into the terminals. This is $R_{Th}$.
3. **Alternatively, short-circuit current.** Short the terminals and find the current through the short. This is $I_N$, and $R_{Th} = V_{Th}/I_N$. This route is essential when the circuit contains dependent sources, because they cannot simply be switched off.

::: {.example}
A 24 V source feeds $R_1 = 6$ kΩ, then a node with $R_2 = 3$ kΩ to ground, then $R_3 = 1$ kΩ to terminal a, with terminal b at ground.

No current flows in $R_3$ on open circuit, so $V_{Th}$ is the divider output: $24 \times 3/9 = 8$ V. Killing the source, $R_1$ and $R_2$ appear in parallel (2 kΩ) in series with $R_3$, so $R_{Th} = 3$ kΩ. A 5 kΩ load would then carry $8/8 = 1$ mA.
:::

The power of the Thévenin equivalent is that it turns a "what if the load changes?" question into one line of arithmetic. If the exam asks for the load current at three different load resistances, find the equivalent once and reuse it.

### Source transformation

A voltage source $V$ in series with $R$ can be replaced by a current source $V/R$ in parallel with the same $R$, and vice versa. Repeated source transformations can collapse a ladder network to a single source surprisingly quickly, and they are a good way to check a Thévenin calculation.

## Maximum Power Transfer

A source with Thévenin resistance $R_{Th}$ delivers the most power to a resistive load when $R_L = R_{Th}$. The load then receives

$$P_{max} = \frac{V_{Th}^2}{4R_{Th}}$$

Figure 4.4 plots load power against load resistance. Notice how flat the curve is near its peak: a load within a factor of two of $R_{Th}$ still receives nearly 90% of the maximum.

![Power delivered to a load as a function of $R_L/R_{Th}$. The maximum occurs at $R_L = R_{Th}$, where the efficiency is only 50%.](figures/fig04_maxpower.png){width=82%}

At maximum power transfer, exactly half the power generated is lost in the source resistance. That is acceptable for a small-signal circuit such as an antenna feeding a receiver, where the aim is to extract every microwatt; it is wholly unacceptable for a power station, which operates with a load resistance far larger than the source resistance to achieve high efficiency. Expect a conceptual question on this distinction.

## Capacitors and Inductors in DC Steady State

After a DC circuit has settled—"after a long time" in exam language—nothing is changing, so:

- a **capacitor** carries no current ($i = C\,dv/dt = 0$) and behaves as an **open circuit**;
- an **inductor** has no voltage across it ($v = L\,di/dt = 0$) and behaves as a **short circuit**.

Replace them accordingly and solve the resistive circuit that remains. The stored energies are

$$W_C = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2, \qquad W_L = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2$$

Capacitors combine in the opposite way to resistors: in parallel they add, and in series they combine reciprocally. Uncoupled inductors combine like resistors. Chapter 6 picks up the story of what happens *between* one steady state and the next.

## Wye–Delta Conversion and the Bridge

Some networks contain neither series nor parallel combinations; the commonest is the bridge. A **delta** (Δ) of three resistors can be replaced by an equivalent **wye** (Y), and vice versa:

$$R_a = \frac{R_{ab}R_{ca}}{R_{ab} + R_{bc} + R_{ca}} \qquad (\Delta \to Y)$$

$$R_{ab} = \frac{R_aR_b + R_bR_c + R_cR_a}{R_c} \qquad (Y \to \Delta)$$

For the Δ-to-Y direction, each wye arm is the product of the two delta resistors *touching that node*, divided by the sum of all three. For a balanced network, $R_Y = R_\Delta/3$, a relation you will use constantly in three-phase power.

The **Wheatstone bridge** is balanced, with no current in the detector, when the ratios of the arms are equal: $R_1/R_2 = R_3/R_x$, or equivalently when the products of opposite arms are equal. At balance, the detector branch can be removed (or shorted) without changing anything, and the rest of the circuit reduces to simple series–parallel form. Spotting a balanced bridge can turn a five-minute problem into a thirty-second one.

## Dependent Sources

A dependent (controlled) source has a value set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit—for example, a current source of value $g_mV_x$. Dependent sources model transistors and amplifiers. The rules for handling them are simple:

- In nodal or mesh analysis, treat them like independent sources, then add an equation expressing the controlling variable in terms of your unknowns.
- When finding $R_{Th}$, **never switch them off**. Either use the open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current, or apply a test source at the terminals and calculate $R_{Th} = V_{test}/I_{test}$.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A 12 V battery with an internal resistance of 0.5 Ω charges through a 1.5 Ω resistor into a node. From that node, a 6 Ω resistor goes to ground and a 3 Ω resistor leads to terminal a, with terminal b at ground. What load resistance between a and b would draw maximum power, and what is that power?*

**Recognise the type.** "Maximum power" means Thévenin. You need $R_{Th}$ for the first part and $V_{Th}$ for the second.

**Simplify before you start.** The battery's internal resistance is simply in series with the 1.5 Ω resistor, so treat them as a single 2 Ω resistor. Many questions hide a series combination in the wording like this.

**Find $V_{Th}$.** With the terminals open, no current flows in the 3 Ω resistor, so $V_{Th}$ is the divider output: $12 \times 6/(2 + 6) = 9$ V.

**Find $R_{Th}$.** Kill the 12 V source. Looking back from a–b: 3 Ω in series with $(2 \parallel 6) = 1.5$ Ω, so $R_{Th} = 4.5$ Ω.

**Answer.** The load should be 4.5 Ω, and it receives $P_{max} = 9^2/(4\times4.5) = 4.5$ W.

**Sanity-check.** The battery delivers $9/9 = 1$ A through the 3 Ω and load branch at maximum power transfer. A total of 9 W is dissipated in the Thévenin model, half in the load, as expected. Everything is consistent.

Three minutes or less, and not a single simultaneous equation. That is the reward for recognising the structure before reaching for a general method.

# Understanding the Exam and Building Your Plan

::: {.objectives}
- How the computer-based FE Electrical and Computer exam is structured, timed and scored
- The eighteen knowledge areas and how many questions each is likely to contribute
- How to build a twelve-week study plan that survives contact with a full-time job
- How to keep an error log that turns every mistake into a mark
- A twenty-question diagnostic test to show you where to start
:::

Every year, thousands of capable engineers fail the FE exam, and very few of them fail because the questions were beyond them. They fail because they ran out of time on question seventy-three, because they spent nine minutes hunting for a formula they could have found in forty seconds, because they studied power systems for three weeks and never opened a page on computer networks, or because they turned up on the day exhausted and rattled. The good news hidden in that list is that none of those problems is about intelligence. Every one of them is about preparation, and preparation is something you control.

This chapter is about understanding the exam as a system. Engineers are good at systems. Once you see how the exam is assembled—how many questions, from which areas, under what time pressure, with what tools—you can design your preparation the way you would design anything else: to a specification.

## What the FE Exam Is, and Why It Matters

The Fundamentals of Engineering exam is the first of two examinations that lead to licensure as a Professional Engineer (PE) in the United States. It is written and administered by the National Council of Examiners for Engineering and Surveying (NCEES), but the licence itself is granted by the licensing board of an individual state or territory. Passing the FE, together with the appropriate degree, normally allows you to be designated an Engineer Intern (EI) or Engineer-in-Training (EIT). After a period of qualifying experience—typically four years—you sit the PE exam in your discipline.

Why does this matter if you have no immediate plans to stamp drawings? Because the designation opens doors. Many utilities, consulting firms and government agencies expect it. Some pay more for it. And, frankly, the FE is never easier than it is now: every year away from your degree makes the mathematics and the breadth harder to recover. If you are reading this as a student or recent graduate, the best time to sit the exam is soon.

The FE exam comes in seven disciplines. You are preparing for **FE Electrical and Computer**, which covers the broad sweep of an electrical, electronic or computer engineering degree, from Kirchhoff's laws to subnet masks.

## The Format at a Glance

The exam is computer-based and is taken year-round at Pearson VUE test centres. At the time of writing its main features are as follows.

| Feature | Detail |
|:--|:--|
| Number of questions | 110 |
| Total appointment time | About 6 hours |
| Time spent answering questions | 5 hours 20 minutes |
| Other time | Non-disclosure agreement, tutorial and a scheduled break of about 25 minutes |
| Structure | Two sections, with the optional break between them |
| Reference material | The *FE Reference Handbook*, supplied as a searchable PDF on screen |
| Calculator | An NCEES-approved model only |
| Result | Pass or fail, usually within 7–10 days |

Table: The computer-based FE Electrical and Computer exam at a glance. Confirm current details with NCEES before you book.

A little arithmetic is instructive. Five hours and twenty minutes is 320 minutes. Divided among 110 questions, that is **2.9 minutes per question**. Some questions will take you forty seconds—a definition, a quick conversion, a reading from a table. Others will take five minutes. Your job is to make the average work, and the strategy in Chapter 16 is built around exactly that.

The exam is split into two sections, with roughly half the questions in each. Once you submit the first section you cannot return to it. You may take the scheduled break between them; take it. Ten minutes away from the screen, a drink and something to eat are worth several marks in the afternoon.

### Question formats

Most questions are conventional multiple choice with four options. NCEES also uses what it calls alternative item types, which you should expect to meet a handful of times:

- **Multiple correct:** choose all the options that apply. There is no partial credit, so read every option carefully.
- **Fill in the blank:** type a numerical answer. Pay attention to the requested units and to any instructions on rounding.
- **Point and click:** click on the relevant part of a figure, such as the node with the highest voltage.
- **Drag and drop:** arrange items in order or match them to categories, such as placing protocols against the correct network layers.

None of these is harder in substance than a multiple-choice question; they simply remove the safety net of eliminating options. The practice questions in this book are multiple choice so that they can be self-marked, but you should practise working every numerical problem to a final value *before* looking at the options. That habit makes the fill-in-the-blank items painless.

### Scoring

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. **Never leave a question blank.** Your raw score is converted to a scaled score, and NCEES sets the pass mark using a standard-setting process rather than a fixed percentage. You will see occasional internet folklore about the "magic number" needed to pass; ignore it. A candidate who can answer around two-thirds or more of a full-length practice exam correctly, under timed conditions, is in a strong position. Aim higher than that in practice, because exam-day nerves cost everyone a few marks.

Candidates who pass are told only that they passed. Candidates who do not pass receive a diagnostic report showing their relative performance in each knowledge area, which is invaluable for planning a retake. NCEES limits how often you can sit the exam—at the time of writing, no more than once in any two-month period and three times in any twelve-month period—so check the current rules before you plan around them.

## The Knowledge Areas

The NCEES specification divides the exam into knowledge areas, each with an expected range of questions. Figure 1.1 shows the approximate distribution for the current specification. Study it carefully, because it should drive how you spend your time.

![Approximate number of questions per knowledge area on the FE Electrical and Computer exam (midpoints of the NCEES ranges). Circuit analysis is by far the largest single area, but the computing areas together are worth more than most candidates realise.](figures/fig01_blueprint.png){width=100%}

Three things leap out of that chart.

First, **circuit analysis dominates**. DC and AC circuit analysis together may account for around one question in six. Many other areas—electronics, power, linear systems, control—also lean heavily on circuit skills. Time spent making your circuit analysis fast and reliable pays off across the whole paper.

Second, **mathematics is the second-largest area**. These questions are rarely hard, but they are numerous, and they are among the quickest marks available if your algebra, calculus and complex-number skills are fluent.

Third, **the computing areas add up**. Digital systems, computer systems, computer networks and software engineering together contribute something like twenty questions. Electrical engineers who never took a networking module often skip these areas entirely and quietly hand away a fifth of the exam. Do not be that candidate. Many of these questions are pure recall, and an evening or two of focused reading converts them into near-certain marks.

::: {.keyidea}
The FE exam rewards **breadth over depth**. A candidate who is competent across all eighteen areas will comfortably outscore one who is brilliant in five and lost in the rest. When you are deciding what to study next, the right question is not "what am I worst at?" but "where can I gain the most marks per hour?"
:::

## The Reference Handbook: Your Only Book on Exam Day

You cannot take notes, books or formula sheets into the exam. Instead, you will have an electronic copy of the *FE Reference Handbook* on screen, in a window beside the questions. It contains hundreds of pages of formulae, tables and definitions across every engineering discipline, and it has a search function.

This changes how you should study in two ways.

The first is that **you do not need to memorise most formulae**. If the equation for the skin depth of a conductor is in the Handbook, your job is to recognise when you need it, find it quickly and use it correctly. The second is that **finding things in the Handbook is a skill that takes practice**. The search function is literal: search for "power factor" and you may find a dozen hits in irrelevant chapters. Search for a term the Handbook does not use and you will find nothing at all. Candidates who meet the Handbook for the first time on exam day lose many minutes to this.

Download the current Handbook from the NCEES website today. It is free. Use it—and only it—as your formula source while you work through this book. Throughout the chapters you will find boxes like the one below, pointing out where the Handbook helps and where it does not.

::: {.handbook}
Some relationships you need are *not* in the Handbook, or appear only in a form that is awkward under time pressure: the voltage-divider rule, the Thévenin procedure, the meaning of a phase angle. These you must simply know. The chapters flag them as "know cold".
:::

## Your Calculator

Only calculators on the NCEES approved list are permitted, and the list is enforced at the door. At the time of writing it includes certain models from the Casio fx-115 and fx-991 families, the HP 33s and 35s, and the TI-30X and TI-36X families. Check the current list before you buy.

Choose one model, buy it now, and use it for every problem in this book. On exam day it should feel like an extension of your hand. In particular, learn to do the following on *your* calculator without thinking:

- Complex arithmetic in both rectangular and polar form, and conversion between them
- Solving a system of two or three linear equations
- Evaluating a definite integral numerically
- Using the memory registers to store an intermediate result instead of retyping it
- Switching between degree and radian modes—and checking which one you are in

That last point deserves a moment. A surprising number of wrong answers on the FE come from a calculator left in radian mode during a phasor question, or in degree mode during a calculation of $\sin(\omega t)$. Make checking the mode a ritual.

## Building a Study Plan That Survives Real Life

Most study plans fail for the same reason most diets fail: they are designed for an imaginary person with unlimited willpower and no other commitments. The plan in this section is designed for someone with a job, a family or both, who can find around ten to twelve hours a week.

For most candidates, **twelve weeks** of steady work is about right. Recent graduates whose coursework is fresh can often compress it to eight. Engineers who have been out of education for a decade or more should allow fourteen to sixteen. Figure 1.2 shows the shape of a twelve-week plan.

![A twelve-week study plan. Each part of the book receives a block of time proportional to its weight on the exam, with mixed review woven in from week four and the final fortnight reserved for full mock exams and targeted repair.](figures/fig01_studyplan.png){width=100%}

### The weekly rhythm

A sustainable week might look like this:

- **Three weekday evenings, 90 minutes each.** Read a section of a chapter, work the examples alongside it, and attempt a batch of six to ten practice questions.
- **One weekend session, three to four hours.** Finish the chapter's question set under light time pressure, mark it, update your error log and revisit any weak spots.
- **Two short sessions, 20 minutes each.** Flash review: formulae you must know cold, your error log, and a handful of mixed questions from earlier chapters.

The short sessions matter more than they look. Memory research has shown for decades that spaced, repeated retrieval beats long, infrequent cramming. Twenty minutes on a train working three mixed questions does more for retention than an extra hour re-reading a chapter you finished last week.

### Order of study

Work through the book in order unless your diagnostic results suggest otherwise. The early chapters build skills—complex numbers, circuit analysis, Laplace transforms—that the later chapters assume. If you are short of time, do not skip the computing chapters in Part V; they are among the highest-return hours you will spend.

::: {.story}
Imagine two candidates with the same degree and the same twelve weeks. The first spends ten weeks perfecting power systems and control, because that is the work she does every day and it feels satisfying, then crams everything else in a fortnight. The second divides his time according to Figure 1.1, spends one dull evening learning the OSI model and subnetting, and sits two full mock exams before the real thing. The first candidate knows more engineering. The second candidate is far more likely to pass. Plan like the second candidate.
:::

## The Error Log: Your Most Powerful Tool

If you take one habit from this chapter, take this one. Every time you get a practice question wrong—or right for the wrong reason, or right after an unreasonable amount of time—write a single line in a notebook or spreadsheet:

| Date | Question | Area | What went wrong | The fix |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 12 Mar | 5.14 | AC power | Used peak voltage in $P = V^2/R$ | Always ask: peak or RMS? |
| 12 Mar | 5.22 | AC power | Forgot $\sqrt3$ in three-phase power | $P = \sqrt3 V_L I_L \cos\theta$ uses *line* values |
| 14 Mar | 7.03 | Signals | Nyquist rate for a product of cosines | Products create sum frequencies |

Table: A sample error log. The last column is the one that matters.

After a few weeks, patterns appear. You will discover that you do not make random mistakes; you make the same five or six mistakes repeatedly. Once you can name them, you can stop making them. Read your error log for ten minutes before every study session and again on the morning of the exam.

## Preparing for Exam Day

Book your exam early. Test centre slots fill up, especially at the end of semesters, and having a fixed date concentrates the mind wonderfully. Book a date roughly twelve weeks out, and if necessary move it later; it is much harder to bring an open-ended plan to a conclusion.

In the final week, do not try to learn new topics. Sit your second mock exam, repair what it reveals, review your error log and the formula summary at the back of this book, and sleep properly. On the day, arrive early with acceptable identification, your approved calculator and nothing else you will need. The test centre will provide a reusable writing booklet and marker. Chapter 16 covers the exam day itself in detail, including what to do in the first five minutes, how to triage questions and how to recover if a section goes badly.

::: {.checklist}
- Created an NCEES account and checked the current exam specification
- Downloaded the current *FE Reference Handbook*
- Bought an approved calculator and learned its complex-number mode
- Booked an exam date about twelve weeks away
- Started an error log
- Taken the diagnostic test below
:::

## The Diagnostic Test

The twenty questions that follow sample every major knowledge area. They are not designed to make you feel good, and you are not expected to get them all right. Their purpose is to tell you where you stand *today*, so that you can adjust your plan.

Set a timer for **sixty minutes**—a little more generous than exam pace—and work the questions with your calculator and the *FE Reference Handbook* open. Do not look anything up in this book. When you have finished, mark your answers using the worked solutions that follow, and record your score by area.

How to read your result:

- **16 or more correct:** you have a strong base. Follow the plan at a brisk pace, and spend your extra time on full mock exams.
- **10 to 15 correct:** you are where most candidates start. Follow the twelve-week plan as written.
- **Fewer than 10 correct:** do not be discouraged; you are simply rusty. Allow fourteen to sixteen weeks, and spend extra time on Chapters 2, 4 and 5, which underpin everything else.

Write down your score and the areas you missed in your error log. You will sit the full mock exams in Chapters 17 and 18 near the end of your preparation, and it is deeply satisfying to look back at this page and see how far you have come.

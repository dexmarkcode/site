This chapter gathers the four general knowledge areas that are not, strictly speaking, electrical engineering at all: probability and statistics, engineering economics, and ethics and professional practice. Together they are worth somewhere between ten and sixteen questions—roughly the same as power systems. Yet many candidates treat them as an afterthought, squeezing them into the last weekend before the exam.

That is a mistake, and an expensive one, because these are some of the most learnable marks on the paper. The economics questions are formulaic: identify the cash flows, pick the right interest factor from the Handbook, and calculate. The ethics questions reward common sense plus a small amount of specific knowledge about how licensure works. The statistics questions draw on a handful of distributions that appear again and again. A week of focused work here can comfortably add half a dozen marks to your score.

## Descriptive Statistics

For a set of $n$ measurements $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, the **arithmetic mean** is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

The spread of the data is measured by the variance and its square root, the standard deviation. Here lies the most frequently tested subtlety in the whole area. There are two versions:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum (x_i - \mu)^2} \quad\text{(population)}, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2} \quad\text{(sample)}$$

Use the population formula when you have measured *every* member of the group you care about. Use the sample formula—with $n - 1$ in the denominator—when your data are a sample drawn from a larger population and you are estimating its spread. In engineering practice, the sample formula is almost always the right one: six resistors measured from a batch of ten thousand are a sample.

::: {.tip}
Most approved calculators have a statistics mode that returns both standard deviations, often labelled $\sigma_x$ (or $\sigma_n$) and $s_x$ (or $\sigma_{n-1}$). Learn which label is which on *your* calculator before exam day. Entering six numbers and reading the right answer takes about thirty seconds.
:::

Other descriptive measures occasionally appear: the **median** (the middle value of the sorted data), the **mode** (the most frequent value), the **range** (largest minus smallest) and the **coefficient of variation** ($s/\bar{x}$), which expresses spread as a fraction of the mean.

## Probability Fundamentals

A probability lies between 0 and 1. Two rules carry most of the weight:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A)$$

If $A$ and $B$ are **independent**, $P(B\mid A) = P(B)$ and so $P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. If they are **mutually exclusive**, they cannot both occur, so $P(A\cap B) = 0$. Students often confuse these two ideas. Independent events can perfectly well happen together—the failure of two separate relays, say—whereas mutually exclusive events never do.

### Counting

The number of ways of choosing $k$ items from $n$ when order does not matter is the **combination**

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$

and when order does matter it is the **permutation** $n!/(n-k)!$. A committee is a combination; a ranked shortlist is a permutation.

### Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem reverses a conditional probability:

$$P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A) + P(B\mid \bar{A})P(\bar{A})$$

The classic exam scenario is a test for a rare fault. Suppose 2% of circuit boards are faulty, an automated tester flags 95% of faulty boards, and it also flags 3% of good ones. What fraction of flagged boards are really faulty? The total probability of a flag is $0.95\times0.02 + 0.03\times0.98 = 0.0484$, so the answer is $0.019/0.0484 = 0.39$. Fewer than two in five flagged boards are actually bad. Candidates who answer "95%" have fallen into the base-rate trap, and it is one of the most reliable traps NCEES sets.

## Probability Distributions

Four distributions cover nearly every exam question. The skill is recognising which one a problem describes.

### Binomial: a fixed number of yes-or-no trials

If each of $n$ independent trials succeeds with probability $p$, the probability of exactly $k$ successes is

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$

with mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. Typical wording: "a sample of ten components, each defective with probability 0.05…"

### Poisson: events in an interval

If events occur randomly at an average rate $\lambda$ per interval, the probability of exactly $k$ events in an interval is

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^ke^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$

with both mean and variance equal to $\lambda$. Typical wording: "packets arrive at an average of three per millisecond…" or "faults occur at an average of two per year…"

### Normal: continuous measurements

The normal distribution, Figure 3.1, describes measured quantities that cluster around a mean: resistor values, voltages, manufacturing dimensions. Probabilities are found by standardising,

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

and reading the unit normal table in the Handbook.

![The normal distribution. Standardising with $z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$ lets one table serve every normal problem. The shaded upper tail is $R(z) = 1 - F(z)$.](figures/fig03_normal.png){width=85%}

::: {.handbook}
The Handbook's unit normal table gives several columns side by side: $F(x)$ (the area to the *left* of $x$), $R(x)$ (the area to the *right*), and two-tailed values. Before you read a number, sketch the curve, shade the area the question asks for, and match it to the right column. This one habit prevents most normal-distribution errors.
:::

Keep the 68–95–99.7 rule in your head as a sanity check: about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three.

### Exponential: time to failure

If a component fails at a constant rate $\lambda$ (failures per hour), its probability of surviving to time $t$ is

$$R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$$

and the mean time between failures is $\text{MTBF} = 1/\lambda$. Note a counter-intuitive consequence: a device with a 10 000-hour MTBF has only a $e^{-1} = 37\%$ chance of lasting 10 000 hours.

### Reliability of systems

Components in **series** must all work, so their reliabilities multiply: $R = R_1R_2\cdots R_n$. Components in **parallel** (redundant) fail only if all fail, so $R = 1 - (1-R_1)(1-R_2)\cdots(1-R_n)$. Complex systems are reduced block by block, just like resistor networks, as Figure 3.2 shows.

![Reliability block diagrams. Series blocks multiply reliabilities; parallel blocks multiply *unreliabilities*. Reduce mixed systems one block at a time.](figures/fig03_reliability.png){width=90%}

## Confidence Intervals

When the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known, a two-sided confidence interval for the mean is

$$\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

with $z_{\alpha/2} = 1.645$, $1.96$ and $2.576$ for 90%, 95% and 99% confidence respectively. The $\sqrt{n}$ in the denominator is why larger samples give tighter intervals—and forgetting it is the most common error. When $\sigma$ is unknown and estimated from a small sample, the Handbook's Student's $t$ table replaces $z$; the exam will make clear which situation applies.

## Engineering Economics: The Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in ten years, because today's dollar can be invested to earn interest. Engineering economics is the discipline of comparing cash flows that happen at different times by converting them to equivalent values at a common time.

### The cash-flow diagram

Always draw one. A horizontal timeline marked in years, with arrows up for money received and down for money spent, turns a wordy problem into a picture you can analyse. Figure 3.3 shows a typical example.

![A cash-flow diagram for an equipment purchase: a first cost at year 0, uniform annual operating costs, and a salvage value received at the end of year $n$. Upward arrows are receipts; downward arrows are expenditures.](figures/fig03_cashflow.png){width=92%}

### The interest factors

With interest rate $i$ per period and $n$ periods, six factors convert between a present sum $P$, a future sum $F$ and a uniform annual series $A$. The Handbook tabulates them and gives their formulae. You will use these four most often:

| Find | Given | Factor | Formula |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| $P$ | $F$ | $(P/F, i, n)$ | $(1+i)^{-n}$ |
| $F$ | $A$ | $(F/A, i, n)$ | $\dfrac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i}$ |
| $P$ | $A$ | $(P/A, i, n)$ | $\dfrac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i(1+i)^n}$ |
| $A$ | $P$ | $(A/P, i, n)$ | $\dfrac{i(1+i)^n}{(1+i)^n - 1}$ |

Table: The most frequently used interest factors.

The notation reads as a fraction: $(P/A, 8\%, 10)$ means "find $P$, given $A$, at 8% for 10 years". Multiply the known quantity by the factor and the units cancel: $P = A(P/A, i, n)$.

::: {.example}
A motor upgrade saves \$5,000 a year for 10 years. At 8% interest, what is the present worth of the savings?

$P = 5000\,(P/A, 8\%, 10) = 5000 \times 6.7101 = \$33{,}550.$

Note how much less this is than the naive \$50,000. The difference is the time value of money.
:::

The annual series in these factors is assumed to occur at the *end* of each period, starting at the end of period 1. If a problem has payments at the start of each year, you must adjust—most simply by treating the first payment separately.

### Nominal and effective rates

A loan quoted at "12% compounded monthly" charges 1% per month. Its **effective** annual rate is

$$i_e = \left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^m - 1 = 1.01^{12} - 1 = 12.68\%$$

The effective rate is always at least as large as the nominal rate. For continuous compounding, $i_e = e^r - 1$.

## Comparing Alternatives

### Present worth and annual worth

To compare mutually exclusive alternatives, convert each one's cash flows to a single equivalent value—either a present worth or an equivalent uniform annual cost (EUAC)—and choose the best. For an asset with first cost $P$, annual operating cost $O$ and salvage $S$ after $n$ years:

$$\text{EUAC} = P(A/P, i, n) + O - S(A/F, i, n)$$

The annual-worth method has a convenient property: alternatives with different lives can be compared directly, provided each could be repeated. With present worth you must use a common study period.

### Depreciation

Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over its life for accounting and tax purposes. The **straight-line** method gives a constant annual amount

$$D = \frac{C - S}{n}, \qquad BV_k = C - kD$$

where $C$ is the first cost, $S$ the salvage value and $BV_k$ the book value after $k$ years. The Handbook also gives the MACRS percentages used in US tax depreciation. Remember that depreciation is not a cash flow in itself; it affects cash flow only through taxes.

### Break-even and payback

The **break-even** quantity is where revenue equals total cost:

$$Q_{BE} = \frac{F}{p - v}$$

with fixed cost $F$, unit price $p$ and unit variable cost $v$. The **simple payback** period is the first cost divided by the net annual saving. It ignores interest and everything after the payback point, so it is a quick screening tool rather than a decision rule; the exam occasionally tests exactly that weakness.

::: {.keyidea}
**Sunk costs are irrelevant.** Money already spent cannot be recovered by any future decision, so it should not influence the choice between future alternatives. Only cash flows that *differ* between alternatives matter. This principle appears regularly in conceptual economics questions.
:::

## Ethics and Professional Practice

Ethics questions are mostly common sense, sharpened by a few specific rules. NCEES bases them on its *Model Law* and *Model Rules*, which state boards adopt in various forms. You do not need to memorise the documents, but you should read the summary of the Model Rules in the Handbook at least twice.

### The hierarchy of obligations

The Model Rules organise a licensee's duties into three groups: obligations to society, to employers and clients, and to other licensees. When they conflict, they rank in that order. The most important single principle in engineering ethics is that **the safety, health and welfare of the public are paramount**. Duties to an employer—loyalty, confidentiality, faithful service—are real, but they never override public safety.

Most ethics questions can be answered by asking: *which option protects the public, is honest, and stays within my competence?*

### Rules you should know specifically

- **Competence.** A licensee may practise only in areas where they are qualified by education or experience, and may seal only work prepared under their responsible charge. A licence is not a blanket qualification.
- **Seals.** A seal is personal. Using another engineer's seal, or allowing someone else to use yours, is a serious violation.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Disclose them promptly to all affected parties. Do not accept compensation from more than one party for the same work without everyone's consent, and do not accept gifts that could influence your judgement.
- **Honesty.** Never falsify or misrepresent qualifications, data or the extent of your responsibility on past projects.
- **Confidentiality.** Do not reveal facts about a client's or employer's business without consent, except where the law requires it or public safety demands it.
- **Reporting.** If you know of a violation of the rules by another licensee, you are obliged to report it to the board and to cooperate with its inquiries.
- **Public safety escalation.** If your professional judgement is overruled in a way that endangers life or property, notify the employer or client and, if necessary, the appropriate authority.

### Licensure

Licences are issued by **state boards**, not by NCEES. NCEES develops the examinations and maintains records that help engineers obtain licences in additional states by **comity** (also called endorsement). Each board sets its own requirements for experience and for continuing professional competency. Passing the FE exam with an approved degree typically earns the Engineer Intern (EI) or Engineer-in-Training (EIT) designation, the first step towards the PE.

### Intellectual property

The current specification includes intellectual property, and the questions are usually about choosing the right form of protection:

| Type | Protects | Duration (US, typical) |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Patent | A novel, useful, non-obvious invention, in exchange for public disclosure | 20 years from filing (utility patent) |
| Copyright | Original expression fixed in a tangible medium: documents, drawings, software code | Author's life plus 70 years (individuals) |
| Trademark | Names, logos and symbols identifying the source of goods | Indefinite while in use and renewed |
| Trade secret | Valuable information kept confidential, e.g. a proprietary algorithm | As long as secrecy is maintained |

Table: Forms of intellectual property.

::: {.story}
A candidate once told me he had answered every ethics question on his practice exam by asking himself what his most cautious, most honest colleague would do. He scored full marks in that area. It is not a bad method. The exam is not trying to trick you into cynicism; it is checking that you understand where the lines are drawn.
:::

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A utility can buy transformer A for \$80,000, with annual maintenance of \$4,000, a 20-year life and no salvage value; or transformer B for \$60,000, with annual maintenance of \$6,500, a 20-year life and a salvage value of \$5,000. With interest at 8%, which should it choose, and by how much does it save each year?*

**Recognise the type.** Two mutually exclusive alternatives with the same life, each with a first cost, annual costs and possibly a salvage value. That is an equivalent uniform annual cost comparison. (Present worth would work equally well, since the lives are equal.)

**Find the factors.** From the Handbook's 8% table at $n = 20$: $(A/P) = 0.1019$ and $(A/F) = 0.0219$.

**Calculate each EUAC.**
$$\text{EUAC}_A = 80\,000(0.1019) + 4000 = \$12{,}152$$
$$\text{EUAC}_B = 60\,000(0.1019) + 6500 - 5000(0.0219) = \$12{,}505$$

**Decide.** Transformer A is cheaper by about \$350 a year, despite costing \$20,000 more at the outset. The lower maintenance cost more than repays the higher purchase price over twenty years.

**Sanity-check.** The salvage term is small, as it should be: \$5,000 twenty years from now is worth very little per year today. If you had *added* it instead of subtracting it, B would have looked worse by about another \$220—a sign error worth watching for.

Notice how little of this problem is arithmetic. The work is in recognising the method and reading the right factors from the table. That is true of nearly every economics question on the exam, and it is why the Handbook's interest tables deserve an hour of your time before the day.

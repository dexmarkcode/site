Signal processing is the art of extracting, shaping and transmitting information carried by signals. On the FE exam it is worth between five and eight questions, and those questions are pleasingly predictable. A handful of ideas—the Nyquist rate, aliasing, the resolution of an analogue-to-digital converter, the output of a simple digital filter, the frequency spacing of a DFT—account for most of them. Each can be answered in two minutes or less if you know the relevant relationship and have seen it applied.

This chapter also connects strongly to its neighbours. The Laplace transform of Chapter 6 has a discrete-time twin, the z-transform. The spectra we draw here reappear in communications in Chapter 12, where modulation shifts them around the frequency axis. And the decibel, introduced at the end of this chapter, is the everyday unit of filters, amplifiers, control systems and radio links alike.

## Signals and Systems

A **continuous-time** signal $x(t)$ is defined for every instant; a **discrete-time** signal $x[n]$ is defined only at integer sample indices. A **system** transforms an input signal into an output signal. Several properties describe how a system behaves, and conceptual exam questions test whether you can recognise them.

- **Linear:** the response to $ax_1 + bx_2$ is $ay_1 + by_2$. Superposition holds. A system such as $y = x^2$ or $y = x + 3$ is not linear (the latter fails because zero input does not give zero output).
- **Time-invariant:** delaying the input simply delays the output by the same amount. A system whose coefficients change with time, such as $y(t) = t\,x(t)$, is time-varying.
- **Causal:** the output at any time depends only on present and past inputs. Any system operating in real time must be causal; a filter processing a stored recording need not be.
- **Stable** (bounded-input, bounded-output): every bounded input produces a bounded output.
- **Memoryless:** the output depends only on the present input, as in a resistor.

A system that is both linear and time-invariant—an **LTI system**—is completely characterised by its impulse response $h(t)$ or $h[n]$, and its output is the convolution of the input with that impulse response, as we saw in Chapter 6. Almost all of the exam's signal processing is about LTI systems.

## Fourier Series

Any reasonable periodic signal with period $T$ can be written as a sum of sinusoids at the fundamental frequency $f_0 = 1/T$ and its integer multiples, the harmonics:

$$x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos(n\omega_0t) + b_n\sin(n\omega_0t)\right], \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$$

The coefficients come from integrals over one period:

$$a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,dt, \qquad a_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\cos(n\omega_0t)\,dt, \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\sin(n\omega_0t)\,dt$$

The constant $a_0$ is simply the average (DC) value of the waveform.

You will rarely need to evaluate these integrals on the exam, because the Handbook gives the series for the common waveforms. What you do need are three habits of recognition.

**Symmetry removes terms.** An even function ($x(-t) = x(t)$) has only cosine terms. An odd function ($x(-t) = -x(t)$) has only sine terms. A waveform with half-wave symmetry ($x(t + T/2) = -x(t)$) contains only odd harmonics.

**The square wave.** A square wave alternating between $+A$ and $-A$ with zero mean contains only odd harmonics, with amplitudes $4A/(n\pi)$:

$$x(t) = \frac{4A}{\pi}\left[\sin\omega_0t + \frac{1}{3}\sin3\omega_0t + \frac{1}{5}\sin5\omega_0t + \cdots\right]$$

Figure 7.1 shows how a few harmonics already build a recognisable square wave. Notice that the fundamental's amplitude, $4A/\pi \approx 1.27A$, is larger than the square wave itself.

![Building a square wave from its odd harmonics. The fundamental alone is a sinusoid; adding the third, fifth and higher harmonics sharpens the edges. The overshoot near each edge (the Gibbs phenomenon) never disappears.](figures/fig07_square_fourier.png){width=90%}

**Harmonics fall off at a rate that reflects smoothness.** Waveforms with jumps (square, sawtooth) have harmonics falling as $1/n$. Continuous waveforms with corners (triangle) fall as $1/n^2$. Smooth waveforms fall faster still. This explains why sharp-edged digital signals radiate interference at high frequencies.

## The Fourier Transform and Spectra

For non-periodic signals, the Fourier series becomes the Fourier transform:

$$X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt$$

The transform describes how a signal's energy is distributed across frequency: its **spectrum**. You need its properties more than its integral. The most useful are:

- **Time scaling:** compressing a signal in time stretches its spectrum. Short pulses have wide bandwidths.
- **Time shift:** delaying a signal multiplies its transform by $e^{-j2\pi ft_0}$, changing the phase but not the magnitude.
- **Modulation:** multiplying by $\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ shifts the spectrum up and down by $f_c$. This is the foundation of radio, covered in Chapter 12.
- **Convolution:** convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, $Y(f) = H(f)X(f)$. A filter simply multiplies the input spectrum by its frequency response.
- **Duality:** a rectangular pulse in time has a sinc-shaped spectrum, and a rectangular spectrum corresponds to a sinc pulse in time.

## Sampling and Aliasing

To process an analogue signal digitally, we sample it at regular intervals $T_s$, the sampling rate being $f_s = 1/T_s$. The **sampling theorem** states that a signal containing no frequencies above $f_{max}$ can be reconstructed perfectly from its samples provided

$$f_s > 2f_{max}$$

The minimum rate, $2f_{max}$, is the **Nyquist rate**. Half the sampling rate, $f_s/2$, is the **Nyquist frequency**: the highest frequency that can be represented without ambiguity.

::: {.trap}
When a signal is a *product* of sinusoids, its highest frequency is the *sum* of their frequencies, because $\cos A\cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]$. The Nyquist rate for $\cos(2\pi\cdot3000t)\cos(2\pi\cdot5000t)$ is $2 \times 8 = 16$ kHz, not 10 kHz. Squared signals behave the same way: $\cos^2$ doubles the frequency.
:::

### Aliasing

If a signal contains components above $f_s/2$, those components do not vanish when sampled. They masquerade as lower frequencies—**aliases**—and once that has happened, no amount of processing can separate them from genuine low-frequency content. Figure 7.2 shows how it happens: the samples of a 7 kHz tone taken at 8 kHz are indistinguishable from those of a 1 kHz tone.

![Aliasing. A 7 kHz sinusoid (light) sampled at 8 kHz produces exactly the same samples (dots) as a 1 kHz sinusoid (dark). After sampling, the two cannot be told apart.](figures/fig07_sampling.png){width=92%}

The apparent frequency of a sampled tone is its distance from the nearest integer multiple of the sampling rate:

$$f_a = |f - kf_s|, \qquad k \text{ chosen to make } f_a \le f_s/2$$

Picture the frequency axis folded like a concertina at $f_s/2$, $f_s$, $3f_s/2$, and so on: every frequency lands somewhere between zero and $f_s/2$. That is why aliasing is sometimes called **frequency folding**.

The engineering defence is an **anti-aliasing filter**: an analogue low-pass filter placed before the sampler to remove content above $f_s/2$. Because real filters roll off gradually, practical systems sample well above the Nyquist rate. CD audio, for example, samples at 44.1 kHz to capture a 20 kHz audio band, leaving room for the filter's transition.

## Quantisation and Analogue-to-Digital Conversion

Sampling discretises time; **quantisation** discretises amplitude. An $N$-bit converter divides its full-scale range $V_{FS}$ into $2^N$ levels. The size of one step—the resolution, or least significant bit (LSB)—is

$$\varepsilon = \frac{V_{FS}}{2^N}$$

which is the form used in the Handbook. (Some texts use $2^N - 1$; the difference is negligible for large $N$, but on the exam use the Handbook's form unless told otherwise.) Each sample is rounded to the nearest level, introducing a **quantisation error** of at most $\pm\varepsilon/2$.

Quantisation error behaves like noise. For a full-scale sine wave, the ideal **signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio** is

$$\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02N + 1.76\ \text{dB}$$

The headline is worth remembering: **each extra bit adds about 6 dB**. A 16-bit audio converter achieves about 98 dB; a 12-bit converter about 74 dB.

To choose a converter, work backwards. If a sensor produces 0–5 V and you need to resolve 2 mV, you need $5/2^N \le 0.002$, so $2^N \ge 2500$ and $N \ge 12$ bits.

## Discrete-Time Systems

### Difference equations

A discrete-time LTI system is described by a difference equation relating present and past outputs to present and past inputs:

$$y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M}b_kx[n - k] - \sum_{k=1}^{N}a_ky[n - k]$$

If only the $b$ terms are present, the output depends only on inputs and the impulse response has finite length: a **finite impulse response (FIR)** filter. If any $a$ terms are present, the output feeds back, and the impulse response can go on forever: an **infinite impulse response (IIR)** filter.

FIR filters are always stable and can be designed with exactly linear phase, which preserves wave shapes. IIR filters achieve a sharp response with far fewer coefficients, but can become unstable if their feedback is poorly designed. Figure 7.3 shows both structures.

![Block diagrams of a three-tap FIR filter (top), which combines delayed inputs, and a first-order IIR filter (bottom), which feeds the delayed output back. The $z^{-1}$ blocks are one-sample delays.](figures/fig07_filters.png){width=92%}

### Computing outputs by hand

Exam questions often give a difference equation and an input sequence and ask for a particular output sample. The reliable method is to build a small table, one row per sample, filling in each delayed term explicitly. For $y[n] = 0.5y[n-1] + x[n]$ with a unit impulse input:

| $n$ | $x[n]$ | $y[n-1]$ | $y[n]$ |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | 0.5 |
| 2 | 0 | 0.5 | 0.25 |
| 3 | 0 | 0.25 | 0.125 |

The pattern $h[n] = 0.5^n$ is the impulse response, a geometric sequence that decays because $|0.5| < 1$.

## The z-Transform

The z-transform is to discrete-time systems what the Laplace transform is to continuous ones:

$$X(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x[n]z^{-n}$$

Its most useful property is that a one-sample delay corresponds to multiplication by $z^{-1}$. Taking the z-transform of a difference equation therefore produces a transfer function directly. For the filter above, $Y(z) = 0.5z^{-1}Y(z) + X(z)$, so

$$H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5z^{-1}} = \frac{z}{z - 0.5}$$

with a pole at $z = 0.5$.

### Stability in the z-plane

In continuous time, stable poles lie in the left half of the s-plane. In discrete time, **a causal system is stable if and only if all its poles lie strictly inside the unit circle**, $|z| < 1$. A pole at $z = 0.9$ gives a slowly decaying response; at $z = -0.5$, a response that alternates in sign as it decays; at $z = 1.2$, a response that grows without limit. Poles exactly on the unit circle give sustained oscillation.

The mapping between the two planes is $z = e^{sT_s}$. The imaginary axis of the s-plane maps onto the unit circle, and the left half-plane maps inside it. Knowing this makes the stability rule easy to remember.

## The DFT and the FFT

The **discrete Fourier transform** takes $N$ samples and produces $N$ frequency-domain values:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1$$

Bin $k$ corresponds to frequency $f_k = kf_s/N$, so the spacing between bins—the **frequency resolution**—is

$$\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{NT_s} = \frac{1}{T_{record}}$$

That last form carries an important insight: resolution depends on the *length of time* you observe the signal, not on the sampling rate as such. To distinguish two tones 1 Hz apart you must record for at least one second, however fast you sample.

The **fast Fourier transform** (FFT) is not a different transform but an efficient algorithm for computing the DFT. It reduces the work from about $N^2$ to about $N\log_2N$ operations—for $N = 1024$, from roughly a million to about ten thousand. Conceptual questions on this point are common.

### Leakage and windowing

The DFT implicitly assumes that the $N$ samples repeat forever. If the record does not contain a whole number of cycles, the join creates an artificial discontinuity, and energy "leaks" from the true frequency into neighbouring bins. Multiplying the record by a smooth **window** (Hann, Hamming, Blackman) tapers the ends and reduces leakage, at the cost of a slightly broader main peak.

## Decibels

The decibel expresses a ratio on a logarithmic scale. For power ratios,

$$G_{dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1}$$

and for voltage or current ratios (with equal impedances),

$$G_{dB} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1}$$

The factor of 20 arises because power is proportional to voltage squared. A few anchor values are worth knowing by heart: 3 dB is a factor of 2 in power (or $\sqrt2$ in voltage); 6 dB is a factor of 2 in voltage; 10 dB is a factor of 10 in power; 20 dB is a factor of 10 in voltage. Gains in decibels add when stages are cascaded, which is the whole point of using them.

Absolute levels use a reference: dBm is relative to 1 mW and dBW to 1 W. So 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W, and −30 dBm = 1 μW.

## Simple Analogue Filters

The simplest filter is a resistor and capacitor. With the output taken across the capacitor, it is a first-order **low-pass** filter:

$$H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$

At the cut-off frequency $f_c$, the output is $1/\sqrt2$ of the input (−3 dB, or half power) and lags by 45°. Well above cut-off the magnitude falls at 20 dB per decade (6 dB per octave). Taking the output across the resistor instead gives a **high-pass** filter with the same cut-off. Higher-order filters roll off faster: 40 dB per decade for second order, 60 for third, and so on.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *An analogue sensor produces a signal containing components at 200 Hz and 1.8 kHz. It is sampled at 2 kHz without an anti-aliasing filter. At what frequencies do the components appear in the sampled data?*

**Recognise the type.** "Sampled at… without an anti-aliasing filter" is shorthand for "check for aliasing". The Nyquist frequency is $f_s/2 = 1$ kHz.

**Test each component.** The 200 Hz component is below 1 kHz, so it is represented faithfully at 200 Hz. The 1.8 kHz component is above the Nyquist frequency, so it aliases. Its distance from the nearest multiple of $f_s$ is $|1800 - 2000| = 200$ Hz.

**Conclude.** Both components appear at **200 Hz**. The 1.8 kHz component has disguised itself perfectly as the genuine low-frequency signal, and the two now add together. This is exactly why aliasing is so dangerous: the data look entirely normal, and the error is undetectable after the fact.

**Reflect.** The fix is not clever processing but a low-pass filter *before* the sampler, with its cut-off comfortably below 1 kHz—or a higher sampling rate.

## Common Traps in Signal Processing

**Nyquist rate versus Nyquist frequency.** The Nyquist *rate* is the minimum sampling rate, $2f_{max}$. The Nyquist *frequency* is half the actual sampling rate, $f_s/2$, the highest frequency that can be represented. Read carefully which one a question asks for.

**Voltage and power decibels.** Use $20\log$ for voltage, current and field ratios, and $10\log$ for power ratios. A filter that halves the voltage attenuates by 6 dB, not 3 dB.

**FIR versus IIR.** If the output appears on the right-hand side of the difference equation, the filter is recursive (IIR) and could be unstable. If only inputs appear, it is FIR and is always stable.

**Resolution of an FFT.** Increasing the sampling rate with a fixed number of points makes the resolution *worse*, not better, because $\Delta f = f_s/N$. To resolve closer frequencies you must observe the signal for longer.

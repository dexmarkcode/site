# Digital Systems

::: {.objectives}

- Combinational building blocks: multiplexers, decoders, adders and comparators
- Latches and flip-flops: SR, D, JK and T; setup and hold times
- Counters, shift registers and finite state machines (Moore and Mealy)
- Timing: propagation delay and maximum clock frequency
- Memory organisation, programmable logic and data converters
:::

Digital systems is worth eight to twelve questions—as much as power systems—and it is one of the most reliable areas on the paper. The questions are crisp: convert this number, simplify this expression, find the next state, work out the maximum clock rate. There is little ambiguity and very little arithmetic that a careful candidate can get wrong. For many electrical engineers this area is a little rusty, but rarely forgotten, and a few hours of practice brings it back to full speed.

The secret to speed here is method. Every topic in this chapter has a standard procedure—repeated division for base conversion, invert-and-add-one for two's complement, Gray-code order on a K-map, a table for state sequences—and the procedure is always faster and safer than doing it in your head.

## Number Systems

### Positional notation

A number in base $b$ has digits weighted by powers of $b$. The binary number $1011_2$ is $1\times8 + 0\times4 + 1\times2 + 1\times1 = 11_{10}$. The bases you need are:

| Base | Name | Digits | Bits per digit |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 2 | Binary | 0, 1 | 1 |
| 8 | Octal | 0–7 | 3 |
| 10 | Decimal | 0–9 | — |
| 16 | Hexadecimal | 0–9, A–F | 4 |

Table: Number bases.

### Converting

**To decimal:** multiply each digit by its weight and add. $\text{3F}_{16} = 3\times16 + 15 = 63$.

**From decimal:** divide repeatedly by the base, recording the remainders; read them from last to first. For 45 to binary: $45 \to 22$ r 1, $22 \to 11$ r 0, $11 \to 5$ r 1, $5 \to 2$ r 1, $2 \to 1$ r 0, $1 \to 0$ r 1, giving $101101_2$. For small numbers it is faster to subtract the largest powers of two: $45 = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1$.

**Between binary, octal and hex:** group the bits. Each hexadecimal digit is exactly four bits and each octal digit three, so group from the right and translate each group. $1101\,0110_2 = \text{D6}_{16}$, and $11\,010\,110_2 = 326_8$.

**Binary-coded decimal** (BCD) encodes each decimal digit separately in four bits: 59 in BCD is `0101 1001`, not the binary for 59 (`0011 1011`). Codes `1010` to `1111` are unused.

::: {.tip}
Your approved calculator may convert between bases directly. Check whether yours does, and practise with it, but also learn the grouping method for hex and binary: it is often faster than typing.
:::

## Signed Numbers

Computers must represent negative numbers. Three schemes appear on the exam, all for $n$-bit words:

- **Sign-magnitude:** the leading bit is the sign (1 for negative), the rest the magnitude. Simple, but with two zeros and awkward arithmetic.
- **One's complement:** a negative number is formed by inverting every bit of the positive number. Still two zeros.
- **Two's complement:** invert every bit and add 1. One zero, and ordinary binary addition works for signed numbers. Every modern computer uses it.

An $n$-bit two's-complement number ranges from $-2^{n-1}$ to $+2^{n-1} - 1$: for 8 bits, −128 to +127. The leading bit has weight $-2^{n-1}$ and the rest have their usual positive weights. So `1110 0110` is $-128 + 64 + 32 + 4 + 2 = -26$.

To find the two's complement of a number—that is, to negate it—invert all the bits and add 1. A quick alternative: copy bits from the right up to and including the first 1, then invert the rest. For $+26 = 0001\,1010$, copying the trailing `10` and inverting the remaining `000110` gives `1110 0110`, which is −26.

### Overflow

Adding two $n$-bit signed numbers can produce a result outside the representable range. **Overflow** has occurred if and only if the two operands have the same sign and the result's sign differs from theirs. Adding numbers of opposite sign can never overflow. A carry out of the most significant bit is *not* the same thing as signed overflow, a distinction the exam likes to test. In 8 bits, $100 + 50 = 150$ overflows (the stored result reads as −106), while $-1 + 1$ produces a carry out but no overflow.

## Boolean Algebra and Logic Gates

Boolean algebra operates on values that are 0 or 1, with three basic operations: AND ($AB$), OR ($A + B$) and NOT ($\overline{A}$). Figure 13.1 shows the standard gate symbols.

![Standard logic gate symbols and their functions. NAND and NOR are universal: any logic function can be built from either alone.](figures/fig13_gates.png){width=95%}

The laws you need most often are:

| Law | AND form | OR form |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Identity | $A\cdot1 = A$ | $A + 0 = A$ |
| Null | $A\cdot0 = 0$ | $A + 1 = 1$ |
| Idempotent | $AA = A$ | $A + A = A$ |
| Complement | $A\overline{A} = 0$ | $A + \overline{A} = 1$ |
| Absorption | $A(A + B) = A$ | $A + AB = A$ |
| De Morgan | $\overline{AB} = \overline{A} + \overline{B}$ | $\overline{A + B} = \overline{A}\,\overline{B}$ |

Table: Boolean identities.

Two further results save time: $A + \overline{A}B = A + B$, and the **consensus theorem**, $AB + \overline{A}C + BC = AB + \overline{A}C$ (the $BC$ term is redundant).

**De Morgan's theorems** are the most heavily tested: to complement an expression, complement each variable and swap AND with OR. "Break the bar, change the sign." They also explain why NAND and NOR gates are **universal**: a NAND with its inputs tied together is a NOT gate, and NAND gates arranged appropriately make AND and OR.

The **exclusive-OR** (XOR), $A\oplus B = A\overline{B} + \overline{A}B$, is 1 when its inputs differ. It is the heart of adders, parity generators and comparators. A chain of XORs gives 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1.

## Karnaugh Maps

A Karnaugh map arranges the truth table so that logically adjacent cells—differing in one variable—are physically adjacent. Grouping adjacent 1s eliminates variables and yields a minimal sum-of-products expression. Figure 13.2 shows a three-variable example.

![A three-variable Karnaugh map. Columns follow Gray-code order (00, 01, 11, 10) so that neighbouring cells differ by one bit. Here the groups give $F = \overline{B}C + AB$.](figures/fig13_kmap.png){width=60%}

The rules:

1. Label columns and rows in **Gray-code order**—00, 01, 11, 10—never 00, 01, 10, 11.
2. Group 1s in rectangles of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 cells. Groups may wrap around the edges.
3. Make each group as **large** as possible and use as **few** groups as possible. Overlapping is allowed.
4. Each group becomes one product term containing only the variables that stay constant across it.
5. "Don't care" cells (X) may be included in a group if they help, and ignored if they do not.

A group of two cells eliminates one variable; a group of four eliminates two; a group of eight eliminates three. To find a minimal **product of sums**, group the 0s instead and complement.

On the exam, a fast check of a proposed expression is to test it against one or two minterms. If $F = \sum m(1, 3, 6, 7)$ and an option gives 1 for $ABC = 010$ (minterm 2), that option is wrong, whatever it looks like.

## Combinational Building Blocks

- A **multiplexer** (MUX) selects one of $2^n$ inputs using $n$ select lines. An 8-to-1 MUX needs 3 select lines. Multiplexers can implement any function of their select variables, which makes them handy universal logic elements.
- A **decoder** activates one of $2^n$ outputs for each $n$-bit input code. A 3-to-8 decoder generates all eight minterms, so with an OR gate it too can implement any three-variable function. Decoders are used for memory address selection.
- An **encoder** does the reverse; a **priority encoder** reports the highest-priority active input.
- A **half adder** produces $S = A\oplus B$ and $C = AB$. A **full adder** adds a carry-in: $S = A\oplus B\oplus C_{in}$, $C_{out} = AB + C_{in}(A\oplus B)$. Chaining $n$ full adders gives a **ripple-carry adder**, whose delay grows with $n$; carry-lookahead adders compute carries in parallel to go faster.
- A **comparator** outputs whether $A > B$, $A = B$ or $A < B$.

## Sequential Logic

Combinational circuits have no memory: their outputs depend only on present inputs. **Sequential** circuits have memory, so their outputs also depend on past inputs, held in **flip-flops**.

### Latches and flip-flops

A **latch** is level-sensitive: it follows its input while enabled. A **flip-flop** is edge-triggered: it samples its input only at the active clock edge. The four types appear in the Handbook with their characteristic tables:

| Type | Inputs | Next state $Q^+$ |
|:--|:--|:--|
| D | $D$ | $Q^+ = D$ |
| T | $T$ | $Q^+ = T\oplus Q$ (toggles when $T = 1$) |
| JK | $J$, $K$ | 00 hold; 01 reset; 10 set; 11 toggle |
| SR | $S$, $R$ | 00 hold; 01 reset; 10 set; 11 not allowed |

Table: Flip-flop characteristic tables.

The JK flip-flop's equation is $Q^+ = J\overline{Q} + \overline{K}Q$. The D flip-flop is by far the most common in modern design.

### Timing parameters

For a flip-flop to capture its input reliably, the input must be stable for the **setup time** $t_{su}$ before the clock edge and the **hold time** $t_h$ after it. The output changes after the **clock-to-Q delay** $t_{CQ}$. Violating setup or hold can leave the flip-flop **metastable**, hovering between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time.

In a synchronous circuit, data launched by one flip-flop must pass through the combinational logic and arrive at the next flip-flop at least one setup time before the next clock edge. The minimum clock period is therefore

$$T_{min} = t_{CQ} + t_{logic,max} + t_{su}, \qquad f_{max} = \frac{1}{T_{min}}$$

using the longest (critical) combinational path. This is a favourite exam calculation.

## Counters and Shift Registers

A counter cycles through a sequence of states. An $n$-bit binary counter has $2^n$ states; to count modulo $N$ you need $\lceil\log_2N\rceil$ flip-flops (four for a decade counter, six for a mod-60 counter).

- In an **asynchronous (ripple)** counter, each flip-flop is clocked by the output of the previous one. It is simple, but the delays accumulate, so its outputs briefly pass through wrong states (glitches), and its maximum frequency falls as stages are added.
- In a **synchronous** counter, every flip-flop shares the same clock, and logic decides which ones toggle. All outputs change together.

Figure 13.3 shows the waveforms of a 3-bit synchronous up-counter. Each output runs at half the frequency of the one before it, which is why counters double as **frequency dividers**.

![Timing diagram of a 3-bit binary up-counter. $Q_0$ toggles on every clock edge, $Q_1$ on every second, $Q_2$ on every fourth: each stage divides the frequency by two.](figures/fig13_counter.png){width=90%}

A **shift register** moves its contents one place along on each clock edge. Variants are named by how data enter and leave: serial-in serial-out (SISO), serial-in parallel-out (SIPO), and so on. Shift registers convert between serial and parallel data, create delays, and with feedback form ring and Johnson counters. To answer a shift-register question, write out the register's contents after each clock edge in a small table.

## Finite State Machines

Any sequential circuit can be described as a **finite state machine**: a set of states, transitions between them triggered by inputs, and outputs. There are two styles:

- In a **Moore** machine, outputs depend only on the current state. Outputs are stable between clock edges.
- In a **Mealy** machine, outputs depend on the current state *and* the current inputs. They can respond within the same clock cycle, often with fewer states.

Figure 13.4 shows a Moore machine that detects the input sequence 1-1, a typical exam example.

![State diagram of a Moore machine that outputs 1 after detecting two consecutive 1s on its input. States are circles (with the output shown inside); arrows are labelled with the input that causes each transition.](figures/fig13_fsm.png){width=82%}

To analyse a state machine, build a **state table**: for each present state and input, list the next state and output. To design one, go the other way: draw the diagram, build the table, choose a binary encoding for the states, and derive the next-state and output logic with K-maps. The number of flip-flops needed is $\lceil\log_2(\text{number of states})\rceil$.

## Memory and Programmable Logic

A memory of $2^n$ words of $m$ bits needs $n$ address lines and $m$ data lines. A 64K × 8 memory has 16 address lines and 8 data lines; its capacity is 512 kbit or 64 KB. To build a larger memory from smaller chips, put chips side by side to widen the word and stack them, with a decoder selecting among them, to deepen it.

- **SRAM** stores each bit in a latch of about six transistors. It is fast and needs no refresh, but is expensive per bit; it is used for caches.
- **DRAM** stores each bit as charge on a tiny capacitor, which leaks and must be refreshed every few milliseconds. It is dense and cheap; it is used for main memory.
- **ROM**, **EPROM**, **EEPROM** and **Flash** are non-volatile. Flash is erased in blocks and has a limited number of write cycles.

Programmable logic implements custom digital circuits without fabricating a chip. **PLAs** and **PALs** use programmable AND–OR arrays; **CPLDs** combine several such blocks; **FPGAs** use thousands of small look-up tables (LUTs) and flip-flops with programmable interconnect, configured by a hardware description language such as VHDL or Verilog.

## Data Converters

A **digital-to-analogue converter** (DAC) with $N$ bits and reference voltage $V_{ref}$ produces

$$V_{out} = V_{ref}\frac{D}{2^N}$$

for input code $D$, with an LSB step of $V_{ref}/2^N$. The common architectures are the binary-weighted resistor network and the R–2R ladder, which needs only two resistor values.

An **analogue-to-digital converter** (ADC) does the reverse (see Chapter 7 for resolution and quantisation noise). The main types trade speed for resolution: the **flash** converter, with $2^N - 1$ comparators, is fastest; the **successive-approximation** converter, which performs a binary search in $N$ steps, is the workhorse of microcontrollers; the **dual-slope** integrating converter is slow but very accurate; and the **sigma–delta** converter achieves high resolution by oversampling.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A synchronous circuit uses flip-flops with $t_{CQ} = 2$ ns, $t_{su} = 1.5$ ns and $t_h = 0.5$ ns. The critical combinational path passes through three gates of 3 ns each and a 2-to-1 multiplexer of 2.5 ns. What is the maximum clock frequency?*

**Identify the path.** Logic delay $= 3(3) + 2.5 = 11.5$ ns.

**Add the flip-flop overheads.** $T_{min} = 2 + 11.5 + 1.5 = 15$ ns.

**Convert.** $f_{max} = 1/15\text{ ns} = 66.7$ MHz.

**Reflect.** The hold time does not appear: it constrains the *shortest* path (to stop new data arriving too soon), not the clock period. Distractors would add it, or omit the setup time, or ignore the clock-to-Q delay.

## Common Traps in Digital Systems

**Gray-code order on K-maps.** Columns must run 00, 01, 11, 10. Labelling them in binary order (00, 01, 10, 11) breaks the adjacency on which the method depends.

**Carry versus overflow.** A carry out of the most significant bit signals unsigned overflow. Signed (two's-complement) overflow is detected separately, by the sign rule. They are different flags.

**Address lines and capacity.** Address lines are set by the number of *words*, not the number of bits. A 64K × 16 memory needs 16 address lines, not 20.

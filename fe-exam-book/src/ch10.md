Power systems questions—eight to twelve on a typical paper—have a reassuring character. They involve big machines and high voltages, but the calculations are short and the traps are few and well known. Nearly every one turns on three ideas: the $\sqrt3$ relationships of three-phase circuits, the turns ratio of a transformer, and the synchronous speed of a rotating machine. Add the per-unit system, which turns fault calculations into simple arithmetic, and you have most of the knowledge area.

If you work in power, this chapter will be revision. If you have never touched a three-phase circuit since your degree, it is one of the best-value chapters in the book: a few evenings' work can make eight or more questions routine.

## Three-Phase Systems

Almost all bulk electrical power is generated, transmitted and used as three-phase alternating current. A three-phase source produces three sinusoidal voltages of equal magnitude, displaced by 120° from one another:

$$\mathbf{V}_{an} = V_p\angle0°, \qquad \mathbf{V}_{bn} = V_p\angle-120°, \qquad \mathbf{V}_{cn} = V_p\angle+120°$$

This is **positive** (abc) phase sequence, the usual assumption. Figure 10.1 shows the waveforms. Their great virtue is that, with a balanced load, the total instantaneous power is constant—not pulsating as in a single-phase circuit—which means smoother torque in motors and generators. A three-phase system also delivers more power for a given quantity of conductor material than a single-phase one.

![The three phase voltages of a balanced three-phase supply, displaced by 120°. At every instant they sum to zero, and with a balanced load the total power is constant.](figures/fig10_three_phase.png){width=88%}

### Wye and delta connections

The three phases of a source or load can be connected in two ways.

In a **wye** (Y, or star) connection, one end of each phase is joined at a common neutral point. The **phase voltage** $V_p$ (line-to-neutral) is smaller than the **line voltage** $V_L$ (line-to-line) by a factor of $\sqrt3$, and the line voltage leads by 30°:

$$V_L = \sqrt3\,V_p, \qquad I_L = I_p \qquad \text{(wye)}$$

In a **delta** (Δ) connection, the phases are connected end to end in a closed loop. Each phase sits directly between two lines, so the phase voltage equals the line voltage, but each line current is the difference of two phase currents:

$$V_L = V_p, \qquad I_L = \sqrt3\,I_p \qquad \text{(delta)}$$

Figure 10.2 shows the phasor relationships.

![Wye and delta connections. In wye, line voltages are $\sqrt3$ times the phase voltages and lead them by 30°. In delta, line currents are $\sqrt3$ times the phase currents.](figures/fig10_wye_delta.png){width=95%}

::: {.keyidea}
A simple way to keep the factors straight: **in wye the voltages differ; in delta the currents differ.** And remember that a quoted system voltage—"480 V three-phase", "13.8 kV"—is always the *line-to-line* voltage unless stated otherwise.
:::

### The per-phase equivalent

The elegant way to solve balanced three-phase problems is to solve **one phase** of an equivalent wye circuit and multiply powers by three. Convert any delta load to its equivalent wye ($Z_Y = Z_\Delta/3$), use the phase voltage $V_L/\sqrt3$, and solve a single-phase circuit:

$$I_L = \frac{V_L/\sqrt3}{|Z_Y|}$$

This works for any balanced system, however complicated, and it is how power engineers actually analyse networks.

## Three-Phase Power

The total real, reactive and apparent power of a balanced three-phase load are

$$P = \sqrt3\,V_LI_L\cos\theta, \qquad Q = \sqrt3\,V_LI_L\sin\theta, \qquad |S| = \sqrt3\,V_LI_L$$

where $V_L$ and $I_L$ are line quantities and $\theta$ is the angle of the *phase* impedance (the power-factor angle). The same totals can be written $P = 3V_pI_p\cos\theta$ using phase quantities. Both forms hold for wye and delta alike.

::: {.trap}
The two classic three-phase errors are (1) using $3V_LI_L$ instead of $\sqrt3V_LI_L$, which overstates power by a factor of $\sqrt3$, and (2) using the line voltage across a wye-connected phase impedance, which overstates current by $\sqrt3$ and power by 3. If two options differ by exactly $\sqrt3$ or 3, check which voltage and which current you have used.
:::

::: {.example}
A balanced wye load of $(8 + j6)$ Ω per phase is supplied at 400 V line-to-line.

Phase voltage: $400/\sqrt3 = 231$ V. Phase impedance: $|Z| = 10$ Ω. Current: $I = 23.1$ A in each line.

$P = 3I^2R = 3(23.1)^2(8) = 12.8$ kW. Check: $\sqrt3(400)(23.1)(0.8) = 12.8$ kW, with power factor $8/10 = 0.8$.
:::

Three-phase power-factor correction works exactly as in Chapter 5, with the total reactive power to be cancelled divided equally among three capacitors. For delta-connected capacitors, each sees the line voltage: $C = Q_{C,total}/(3\omega V_L^2)$.

## Transformers

### The ideal transformer

A transformer consists of two or more windings coupled by a common magnetic core. For an ideal transformer with turns ratio $a = N_1/N_2$:

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{N_1}{N_2} = a, \qquad \frac{I_1}{I_2} = \frac{1}{a}, \qquad V_1I_1 = V_2I_2$$

Voltage scales with the turns ratio, current inversely, and power is conserved. An impedance $Z_2$ connected to the secondary appears at the primary as

$$Z_1' = a^2Z_2$$

The squared turns ratio is the basis of impedance matching: an 8 Ω loudspeaker on a 10:1 transformer looks like 800 Ω to the amplifier.

### The practical transformer

A real transformer has winding resistances, leakage reactances (flux that links only one winding), core losses and a finite magnetising current. Figure 10.3 shows the usual equivalent circuit, with the secondary quantities referred to the primary. For most calculations the magnetising branch can be moved to the input terminals, leaving a simple series impedance $R_{eq} + jX_{eq}$.

![The approximate equivalent circuit of a transformer referred to the primary. $R_{eq}$ and $X_{eq}$ represent the combined winding resistance and leakage reactance; the shunt branch represents core loss ($R_c$) and magnetising reactance ($X_m$).](figures/fig10_transformer.png){width=86%}

The parameters are found from two standard tests:

- The **open-circuit test** (rated voltage, secondary open) draws only the small magnetising current, so copper loss is negligible and the wattmeter reads the **core loss**. It yields $R_c$ and $X_m$.
- The **short-circuit test** (secondary shorted, reduced voltage driving rated current) leaves the core almost unmagnetised, so the wattmeter reads the **full-load copper loss**. It yields $R_{eq}$ and $X_{eq}$.

### Efficiency and regulation

Core loss is essentially constant whenever the transformer is energised; copper loss varies with the square of the load. At a fraction $x$ of full load,

$$\eta = \frac{xS\cos\theta}{xS\cos\theta + P_{core} + x^2P_{cu,FL}}$$

Efficiency is greatest at the load where copper loss equals core loss: $x = \sqrt{P_{core}/P_{cu,FL}}$. Distribution transformers, lightly loaded much of the day, are therefore designed with low core losses.

**Voltage regulation** measures how much the secondary voltage sags under load:

$$\text{VR} = \frac{V_{NL} - V_{FL}}{V_{FL}}\times100\%$$

with the full-load voltage in the denominator. A lagging load gives worse regulation than a unity-power-factor load, and a leading load can even give negative regulation.

### Three-phase transformers

Three-phase transformers (or banks of three single-phase units) can be connected Y–Y, Y–Δ, Δ–Y or Δ–Δ. The line-to-line voltage ratio equals the turns ratio for Y–Y and Δ–Δ, but differs by a factor of $\sqrt3$ for the mixed connections, which also introduce a 30° phase shift. The Δ–Y connection is common for step-up transformers at generating stations and for distribution transformers supplying four-wire loads.

## The Per-Unit System

Power systems contain equipment at many voltage levels, linked by transformers. The per-unit system expresses every quantity as a fraction of a chosen base value, so that the transformers' turns ratios disappear from the calculation. Choose a base power $S_b$ (common to the whole system) and a base voltage $V_b$ in each zone (related by the transformer ratios). Then

$$I_b = \frac{S_b}{\sqrt3V_b}, \qquad Z_b = \frac{V_b^2}{S_b}, \qquad Z_{pu} = \frac{Z_{actual}}{Z_b}$$

using three-phase power and line-to-line voltage. Equipment impedances are usually quoted in per unit on the equipment's own rating. To move them to the system base:

$$Z_{pu,new} = Z_{pu,old}\left(\frac{S_{b,new}}{S_{b,old}}\right)\left(\frac{V_{b,old}}{V_{b,new}}\right)^2$$

A further advantage is that per-unit impedances for similar equipment fall in narrow ranges—transformers typically 0.05 to 0.15 pu—so an unusual value is a warning sign.

## Faults

A **bolted three-phase fault** is a direct short circuit of all three phases. With the system represented by its Thévenin equivalent (prefault voltage, typically 1.0 pu, behind a reactance $X_{Th}$), the fault current is

$$I_{f,pu} = \frac{1.0}{X_{Th,pu}}, \qquad I_f = I_{f,pu}\times I_b$$

A bus with a Thévenin reactance of 0.1 pu therefore experiences a fault current ten times the base current. These numbers determine the required interrupting rating of circuit breakers. Unbalanced faults (single line-to-ground, line-to-line) require symmetrical components, which the Handbook summarises; on the FE exam, the three-phase fault is by far the most common.

## Rotating Machines

### Synchronous speed

The stator windings of an AC machine create a magnetic field that rotates at the **synchronous speed**

$$n_s = \frac{120f}{P}\ \text{rpm}$$

where $P$ is the number of **poles** (not pole pairs). A 4-pole machine on 60 Hz runs at 1800 rpm; a 2-pole machine at 3600 rpm; a 6-pole machine at 1200 rpm. On 50 Hz systems these become 1500, 3000 and 1000 rpm.

### Induction motors

An induction motor's rotor must turn slightly slower than the field, so that the field sweeps past the rotor conductors and induces the currents that produce torque. The fractional shortfall is the **slip**:

$$s = \frac{n_s - n_r}{n_s}$$

Typical full-load slip is 2–5%. The frequency of the induced rotor currents is $f_r = sf$, only a few hertz at full load. At standstill, $s = 1$ and the rotor frequency equals the supply frequency; this is why an induction motor draws a starting current five to seven times its rated current, and why soft starters and variable-frequency drives are so widely used. Figure 10.4 shows the typical torque–speed characteristic.

![The torque–speed characteristic of an induction motor. Normal operation lies on the steep, nearly linear section between breakdown torque and synchronous speed, where slip is small.](figures/fig10_torque_speed.png){width=84%}

The power flow through the machine follows from the slip. Of the power crossing the air gap, $P_{ag}$, a fraction $s$ is lost as rotor copper loss and the remainder $(1 - s)$ is converted to mechanical power:

$$P_{rotor\,cu} = sP_{ag}, \qquad P_{mech} = (1 - s)P_{ag}$$

### Torque, power and speed

For any rotating machine,

$$P = T\omega, \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi n}{60}$$

with $P$ in watts, $T$ in N·m and $n$ in rpm. A 15 kW motor at 1460 rpm delivers $15\,000/152.9 = 98$ N·m. Forgetting to convert rpm to rad/s is a common slip, and so is using horsepower without conversion (1 hp ≈ 746 W).

### Synchronous machines

A synchronous machine's rotor carries a DC field winding and turns at exactly synchronous speed. As a generator it is the workhorse of every power station. As a motor its speed is fixed by the supply frequency, regardless of load, until it pulls out of step. Its most useful property is that its power factor can be controlled by the field current: an **over-excited** machine operates at a leading power factor and supplies reactive power to the system; an **under-excited** machine absorbs it. A synchronous motor running unloaded and over-excited is a **synchronous condenser**, used for power-factor correction and voltage support.

### DC machines

In a DC machine, the induced armature voltage (back EMF) and the developed torque are

$$E_a = K\phi\omega, \qquad T = K\phi I_a$$

For a motor, $V = E_a + I_aR_a$, so $E_a = V - I_aR_a$; for a generator, $E_a = V + I_aR_a$. With constant field flux, speed is proportional to back EMF:

$$\frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{E_{a2}}{E_{a1}}$$

This gives quick answers to "the load increases and the armature current rises; what is the new speed?" questions. Weakening the field of a shunt motor *raises* its speed, which surprises many candidates.

## Transmission and Distribution

Transmitting power at high voltage reduces current and therefore $I^2R$ loss. For a fixed power and power factor, doubling the voltage halves the current and quarters the loss. That is the entire economic case for high-voltage transmission. The current in a line carrying power $P$ is

$$I = \frac{P}{V\cos\theta}\ \text{(single-phase)}, \qquad I = \frac{P}{\sqrt3V_L\cos\theta}\ \text{(three-phase)}$$

and the loss is $I^2R$ per conductor. Poor power factor raises the current and therefore the losses, which is why utilities penalise it.

Short transmission lines are modelled by a series impedance $R + jX$; medium lines add shunt capacitance (the π model); long lines require distributed-parameter treatment. On the FE exam, short-line calculations of voltage drop and loss are the norm. The power transferred across a mainly inductive line between two buses is approximately

$$P = \frac{V_1V_2}{X}\sin\delta$$

where $\delta$ is the angle between the bus voltages—a relationship that underlies power-system stability.

## Protection and Power Quality

Protective systems detect faults and isolate them quickly. **Current transformers** and **voltage transformers** scale system quantities down to levels that **relays** can measure. A relay decides when to trip; a **circuit breaker** interrupts the fault current; a **fuse** combines detection and interruption in one sacrificial element. **Disconnect switches** isolate equipment for maintenance but cannot interrupt load current. **Surge arresters** limit overvoltages from lightning and switching.

Power quality concerns deviations from an ideal sinusoid: voltage sags and swells, interruptions, flicker, and harmonics generated by non-linear loads such as rectifiers and variable-speed drives. Harmonic distortion is quantified by the **total harmonic distortion**,

$$\text{THD} = \frac{\sqrt{V_2^2 + V_3^2 + V_4^2 + \cdots}}{V_1}$$

the RMS of the harmonics relative to the fundamental.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A 6-pole, 60 Hz, three-phase induction motor runs at 1164 rpm and delivers 20 hp to its load. Find the slip, the rotor current frequency and the shaft torque.*

**Synchronous speed.** $n_s = 120(60)/6 = 1200$ rpm.

**Slip.** $s = (1200 - 1164)/1200 = 0.03$, or 3%.

**Rotor frequency.** $f_r = sf = 0.03\times60 = 1.8$ Hz.

**Torque.** Convert power to watts: $20\times746 = 14\,920$ W. Convert speed: $\omega = 2\pi(1164)/60 = 121.9$ rad/s. So $T = 14\,920/121.9 = 122$ N·m.

**Sanity-check.** A 3% slip is typical for a motor of this size; 1.8 Hz is a sensible rotor frequency; and around 120 N·m is right for a 15 kW motor at roughly 1200 rpm. The distractors would include 60 Hz for the rotor frequency (the supply, not the rotor) and a torque calculated with rpm instead of rad/s.

The leap from DC to AC frightens more students than it should. The physics is richer—energy sloshes back and forth between electric and magnetic fields, and voltages and currents no longer rise and fall together—but the *method* barely changes at all. Once a circuit is in sinusoidal steady state, every voltage and current is a sinusoid at the same frequency, differing only in amplitude and phase. Represent each one as a complex number, replace resistance with complex impedance, and Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws, dividers, nodal analysis and Thévenin's theorem all work exactly as they did in Chapter 4.

This chapter is where your complex-number fluency from Chapter 2 pays off. It is also where some of the most dependable traps on the exam live: peak values used in place of RMS values, $\omega$ confused with $f$, power-factor angles taken with the wrong sign. We will meet each of them head-on.

## Sinusoids and Phasors

A sinusoidal voltage is written

$$v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t + \theta)$$

where $V_m$ is the amplitude (peak value), $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency in rad/s, $f$ the frequency in hertz, and $\theta$ the phase angle. The FE convention, like most textbooks, uses the cosine as the reference; a sine can always be converted with $\sin x = \cos(x - 90°)$.

### RMS values

The root-mean-square value of a sinusoid is

$$V_{rms} = \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707V_m$$

It is the DC voltage that would deliver the same average power to a resistor. The 120 V or 230 V of a domestic supply is an RMS value; the corresponding peaks are about 170 V and 325 V. In power engineering, voltages and currents are always quoted in RMS unless stated otherwise.

::: {.trap}
The single most common AC error on the exam is mixing peak and RMS values. A question that gives $v(t) = 170\cos(377t)$ is giving you a *peak* value; the RMS value is 120 V. Power calculated with the peak value comes out exactly twice too large, and that answer will be waiting among the distractors. Before you calculate any power, ask: *peak or RMS?*
:::

### The phasor transformation

Because every quantity in a linear circuit at steady state oscillates at the same frequency, the $\omega t$ part carries no information. We can drop it and represent each sinusoid by a complex number—a **phasor**—holding just its magnitude and phase:

$$v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t + \theta) \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{V} = V_m\angle\theta$$

(In power work the phasor magnitude is usually the RMS value instead; just be consistent within a problem.) Figure 5.1 shows a voltage and current phasor for an inductive load, in which the current lags the voltage.

![A phasor diagram for an inductive load. The current phasor lags the voltage phasor by the impedance angle $\theta$. Projecting the rotating phasors onto the real axis recovers the time-domain sinusoids.](figures/fig05_phasor.png){width=80%}

Adding sinusoids of the same frequency is now just complex addition. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$, and integration becomes division by $j\omega$. Differential equations become algebraic equations. That is the whole reason phasors exist.

## Impedance

The ratio of the voltage phasor to the current phasor for an element is its **impedance** $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{V}/\mathbf{I}$, measured in ohms:

| Element | Impedance | Phase of $\mathbf{V}$ relative to $\mathbf{I}$ |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Resistor | $R$ | In phase |
| Inductor | $j\omega L = jX_L$ | Voltage leads current by 90° |
| Capacitor | $\dfrac{1}{j\omega C} = -jX_C$ | Voltage lags current by 90° |

Table: Impedances of the passive elements.

with inductive reactance $X_L = \omega L$ and capacitive reactance $X_C = 1/(\omega C)$. The old mnemonic **ELI the ICE man** still earns its keep: in an inductor (L), voltage (E) comes before current (I); in a capacitor (C), current comes before voltage.

A general impedance is $\mathbf{Z} = R + jX$. Its real part is resistance and its imaginary part is reactance: positive for inductive, negative for capacitive. The reciprocal of impedance is **admittance**, $\mathbf{Y} = 1/\mathbf{Z} = G + jB$, in siemens.

::: {.trap}
Reactance depends on $\omega$, not $f$. $X_L = 2\pi fL$, not $fL$. Forgetting the $2\pi$ produces an answer about six times too small for an inductor and six times too large for a capacitor, and both appear routinely as distractors.
:::

### Combining impedances

Impedances in series add; impedances in parallel combine reciprocally, exactly like resistors. The difference is that the arithmetic is complex. A resistor of 30 Ω in series with an inductive reactance of 40 Ω has $|\mathbf{Z}| = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = 50$ Ω, not 70 Ω. A resistor in parallel with a reactance has magnitude $RX/\sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$, not $RX/(R + X)$. If you add magnitudes arithmetically, you have treated the circuit as DC.

### Working with admittance

For parallel circuits, admittance is often the more natural quantity, because admittances in parallel simply add. A resistor contributes conductance $G = 1/R$, a capacitor contributes susceptance $+j\omega C$, and an inductor contributes $-j/(\omega L)$. For a resistor of 50 Ω in parallel with a 10 μF capacitor at 1 kHz:

$$\mathbf{Y} = \frac{1}{50} + j(2\pi\times1000\times10\times10^{-6}) = 0.020 + j0.0628\ \text{S}$$

so $|\mathbf{Y}| = 0.0659$ S and $|\mathbf{Z}| = 1/|\mathbf{Y}| = 15.2$ Ω. That is three lines of arithmetic, with no product-over-sum fraction to wrestle with.

One warning: the conductance $G$ is the reciprocal of $R$ *only* when the element is a pure resistance. For a series combination $R + jX$, the conductance of its admittance is $R/(R^2 + X^2)$, not $1/R$. Candidates who invert the real and imaginary parts separately get a wrong answer that looks entirely reasonable, which is exactly why it makes such a good distractor.

### Impedance and frequency at a glance

It helps to have a feel for how each element behaves at the extremes of frequency, because many conceptual questions turn on it. At DC ($\omega = 0$), an inductor has zero impedance and a capacitor has infinite impedance—the short and open circuits of Chapter 4. At very high frequency the roles reverse: the inductor blocks and the capacitor passes. A series inductor and a shunt capacitor therefore form a low-pass filter; swap them and you have a high-pass filter. When an exam question asks what a circuit does to high or low frequencies, mentally set $\omega$ to zero and to infinity and redraw the circuit with shorts and opens. The answer is usually obvious.

## AC Circuit Analysis

Every DC technique now applies directly:

- **Voltage and current dividers** with impedances in place of resistances.
- **Nodal and mesh analysis**, producing complex simultaneous equations—a job for your calculator's complex equation solver.
- **Thévenin and Norton equivalents**, with $\mathbf{V}_{Th}$ and $\mathbf{Z}_{Th}$ complex.
- **Superposition**, which becomes essential when sources operate at *different* frequencies: you must analyse each frequency separately (each with its own impedances) and add the results in the time domain, never as phasors.

::: {.example}
A 120 V RMS, 60 Hz source drives a 30 Ω resistor in series with an 80 mH inductor.

$X_L = 2\pi(60)(0.08) = 30.2$ Ω, so $\mathbf{Z} = 30 + j30.2 = 42.5\angle45.2°$ Ω.

$\mathbf{I} = 120\angle0°/42.5\angle45.2° = 2.82\angle-45.2°$ A RMS. The current lags the voltage by 45.2°, as expected for an inductive circuit.
:::

## Resonance

In a series RLC circuit, the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel at the **resonant frequency**

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

At resonance the impedance is purely resistive and at its minimum, so the current is at its maximum and in phase with the source voltage. Figure 5.2 shows how sharply the current peaks for circuits of different quality factor.

![Current magnitude against frequency for a series RLC circuit at three values of $Q$. Higher $Q$ gives a taller, narrower peak and a smaller bandwidth.](figures/fig05_resonance.png){width=85%}

The sharpness of the peak is measured by the **quality factor**

$$Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0CR} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \quad\text{(series RLC)}$$

and the half-power **bandwidth** is

$$B = \frac{\omega_0}{Q} = \frac{R}{L}\ \text{rad/s}, \qquad\text{or}\qquad B = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{R}{2\pi L}\ \text{Hz}$$

A striking consequence of resonance is voltage magnification. At resonance the current is $V/R$, so the voltage across the inductor is $IX_L = QV$. A series circuit with $Q = 20$ driven by 10 V develops 200 V across the inductor and the capacitor—a fact of real practical importance when choosing component ratings.

For a **parallel** RLC circuit, resonance occurs at the same frequency, but the impedance is at its *maximum* and $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$—the role of the resistor is inverted. If an exam question gives a parallel circuit, take care not to use the series formula.

## Power in AC Circuits

### Instantaneous and average power

With $v(t)$ and $i(t)$ both sinusoidal, the instantaneous power $p(t) = v(t)i(t)$ pulses at twice the supply frequency. What matters for energy is its average, the **real power**

$$P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta$$

measured in watts, where $\theta$ is the angle by which the voltage leads the current. The factor $\cos\theta$ is the **power factor**. A resistor has $\theta = 0$ and a power factor of 1; an ideal inductor or capacitor has $\theta = \pm90°$ and absorbs no average power at all.

### Complex power and the power triangle

It is convenient to combine real and reactive power into the **complex power**

$$\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{I}^* = P + jQ$$

where $\mathbf{V}$ and $\mathbf{I}$ are RMS phasors and the asterisk denotes the complex conjugate. The conjugate is what makes the angles *subtract*, so that $\mathbf{S}$ has angle $\theta_v - \theta_i$. Its components are:

- **Real power** $P = |\mathbf{S}|\cos\theta$, in watts (W): energy actually converted to heat, light or mechanical work.
- **Reactive power** $Q = |\mathbf{S}|\sin\theta$, in volt-amperes reactive (var): energy shuttling back and forth between source and fields. Positive for inductive loads, negative for capacitive.
- **Apparent power** $|\mathbf{S}| = V_{rms}I_{rms}$, in volt-amperes (VA): the product that sets the current, and therefore the size of cables, transformers and generators.

The three form a right-angled triangle, Figure 5.3: $|\mathbf{S}|^2 = P^2 + Q^2$.

![The power triangle for a lagging (inductive) load. Real power lies along the horizontal axis, reactive power along the vertical, and apparent power is the hypotenuse at the power-factor angle $\theta$.](figures/fig05_powertriangle.png){width=72%}

::: {.tip}
For a load described by its impedance, you can calculate power directly from the resistive part: $P = I_{rms}^2R$ and $Q = I_{rms}^2X$. A wattmeter "sees" only the resistance. This is often quicker than finding the phase angle.
:::

### Lagging and leading

A **lagging** power factor means the current lags the voltage, as in inductive loads such as motors. A **leading** power factor means the current leads, as in capacitive loads. Industrial loads are overwhelmingly lagging, which is why power-factor correction almost always means adding capacitors.

## Power-Factor Correction

A load with a poor power factor draws more current than its real power requires. Since conductor losses are $I^2R$, and since every cable and transformer is sized by current, utilities penalise low power factors. The remedy is to supply the load's reactive power locally, with a capacitor connected in parallel. Real power is unchanged; reactive power, apparent power and current all fall.

The procedure is mechanical:

1. Find the original reactive power: $Q_1 = P\tan\theta_1$, where $\theta_1 = \cos^{-1}(\text{pf}_1)$.
2. Find the target reactive power: $Q_2 = P\tan\theta_2$.
3. The capacitor must supply $Q_C = Q_1 - Q_2$.
4. For a single-phase capacitor across voltage $V$: $C = \dfrac{Q_C}{\omega V^2}$.

::: {.example}
A 50 kW load runs at 0.8 lagging from 480 V, 60 Hz. To correct to 0.95 lagging:

$Q_1 = 50\tan(36.87°) = 37.5$ kvar; $Q_2 = 50\tan(18.19°) = 16.4$ kvar; so $Q_C = 21.1$ kvar.

$C = 21\,100/(377 \times 480^2) = 243\ \mu$F.

The line current falls from $50\,000/(480\times0.8) = 130$ A to $50\,000/(480\times0.95) = 110$ A.
:::

Correcting all the way to unity is rarely economic: the last few hundredths of power factor cost a great deal of capacitance for little reduction in current. Notice, too, that the exam may describe the target simply as "unity", in which case $Q_2 = 0$.

## Maximum Power Transfer in AC Circuits

For a source with Thévenin impedance $\mathbf{Z}_{Th} = R_{Th} + jX_{Th}$, a load of *any* impedance receives the most power when it is the **complex conjugate** of the source impedance:

$$\mathbf{Z}_L = \mathbf{Z}_{Th}^* = R_{Th} - jX_{Th}, \qquad P_{max} = \frac{|\mathbf{V}_{Th}|^2}{4R_{Th}}$$

with $\mathbf{V}_{Th}$ in RMS. The reactances cancel, leaving a purely resistive loop of $2R_{Th}$. If the load is constrained to be purely resistive, the best choice is instead $R_L = |\mathbf{Z}_{Th}|$, which delivers less power. Read the question carefully to see which case applies.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

It is one thing to know the formulae and another to use them calmly with the clock running. Let us walk through a typical multi-step AC question the way I would want you to think about it on the day.

> *A single-phase industrial load is supplied at 240 V RMS, 60 Hz. A wattmeter reads 3.6 kW and an ammeter reads 20 A. What capacitance, connected in parallel with the load, would raise the power factor to unity?*

**Step 1: decide what kind of problem this is.** The words *power factor* and *capacitance in parallel* tell you at once that this is a power-factor correction problem. You therefore need the load's reactive power, because the capacitor must cancel it. Everything else is a route to $Q$.

**Step 2: extract what the instruments tell you.** The ammeter and voltmeter together give the apparent power: $|S| = VI = 240 \times 20 = 4800$ VA. The wattmeter gives the real power directly: $P = 3600$ W. Notice that you were never told the power factor; you have to infer it. That is a very common way for the exam to add a step.

**Step 3: complete the power triangle.** $Q = \sqrt{|S|^2 - P^2} = \sqrt{4800^2 - 3600^2} = 3175$ var. (The power factor is $3600/4800 = 0.75$, which you may want for a sanity check, but you do not actually need it.) The load is described as industrial and we are correcting with a capacitor, so it is inductive and $Q$ is positive.

**Step 4: size the capacitor.** For unity power factor the capacitor must supply all 3175 var:

$$C = \frac{Q_C}{\omega V^2} = \frac{3175}{(2\pi \times 60)(240)^2} = 146\ \mu\text{F}$$

**Step 5: sanity-check.** Is 146 μF plausible for a few kilowatts at 240 V? Power-factor correction capacitors for small industrial loads are typically tens to a few hundred microfarads, so yes. Had you obtained 146 mF or 0.146 μF, a unit error would be the likely culprit. Had you obtained about 878 μF, you would suspect a missing $2\pi$.

The whole question takes perhaps two and a half minutes if you recognise its type immediately. The recognition—*this is a $Q$ problem*—is the step that saves the time.

## Five AC Traps to Recognise on Sight

Over many years of marking practice exams, the same handful of AC mistakes appear again and again. Learn to recognise them in the options as well as in your own working.

**1. Peak versus RMS.** We have met this one already. A given amplitude, such as $v(t) = 325\cos\omega t$, is a peak value. Powers and meter readings use RMS values. If one option is exactly twice another, suspect this trap.

**2. Angular frequency versus frequency.** Reactances need $\omega = 2\pi f$; resonant frequencies are often asked for in hertz, whereas $1/\sqrt{LC}$ is in rad/s. Two options differing by a factor of about 6.28 are a sure sign that this trap is in play.

**3. Arithmetic addition of $R$ and $X$.** The magnitude of $30 + j40$ is 50, not 70. Any option equal to the simple sum of a resistance and a reactance is almost certainly a distractor.

**4. The sign of the power-factor angle.** The angle in $\cos\theta$ is the voltage angle *minus* the current angle. With $\mathbf{V} = 240\angle10°$ and $\mathbf{I} = 5\angle-26.87°$, the angle is $36.87°$, not $-16.87°$. Adding the angles gives a power factor that is plausible and wrong.

**5. Series formulae applied to parallel circuits.** In a parallel RLC circuit, the quality factor is $R\sqrt{C/L}$, the inverse dependence on $R$ from the series case. Larger resistance means a *sharper* parallel resonance. Read the circuit before you reach for the formula.

::: {.tip}
When you check an AC answer, check its *character* as well as its number. An inductive circuit must have a lagging current and positive reactive power; a capacitive circuit must have a leading current and negative reactive power. A purely resistive circuit absorbs no reactive power at all. If the sign of your answer contradicts the physics, look for one of the five traps above.
:::

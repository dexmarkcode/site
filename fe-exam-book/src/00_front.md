# A Word Before We Begin {.unnumbered}

Most people open a book like this with a knot in their stomach. Perhaps you graduated last spring and the exam is the last box to tick before your career properly starts. Perhaps you graduated twelve years ago, you have been doing good engineering ever since, and you are now staring at Laplace transforms you have not touched since your second year. Perhaps you have already sat the exam once, and you are back. Whichever of those is you, I want to say something plainly before we go any further: you can pass this exam, and you can pass it the first time you sit it from here.

I say that with some confidence because the Fundamentals of Engineering exam is not a test of genius. It is a test of breadth, of method and of nerve. It asks whether you can recognise a familiar engineering problem quickly, find the right relationship, put the right numbers into it and avoid the handful of mistakes that catch nearly everybody. Every one of those things can be trained. That is what this book is for.

I have written it the way I would teach you if we were sitting at the same table. You will find the theory explained from the ground up, but never for its own sake: every idea is here because it turns up on the exam, and every explanation ends with the question, *what does this look like when NCEES asks it?* You will find stories of the traps candidates fall into, because recognising a trap is worth more than any number of formulae. And you will find problems—eight hundred of them—each with a full worked solution, because nobody ever learned to solve problems by reading about solving problems.

## Who This Book Is For {.unnumbered}

This guide is written for anyone preparing for the computer-based FE Electrical and Computer exam in 2026 or 2027: final-year students, recent graduates, engineers returning to the exam after years in industry, and international graduates working towards a US licence. It assumes you have met the core subjects of an electrical or computer engineering degree at some point, but it does not assume you remember them. Where a topic is likely to be rusty, we rebuild it.

It is also written for people with busy lives. The study plan in Chapter 1 is designed around a working week, not around the fantasy of eight uninterrupted hours a day.

## How the Book Is Organised {.unnumbered}

The eighteen chapters are grouped into six parts of three chapters each.

**Part I, Foundations for First-Time Success,** explains how the exam works, gives you a diagnostic test and a realistic study plan, and then covers the general subjects that open the exam: mathematics, probability and statistics, engineering economics and ethics.

**Part II, Circuits: The Heart of the Exam,** covers DC and AC circuit analysis, power in AC circuits, transients and linear systems. Circuit analysis is the single largest knowledge area on the exam, and the skills in Part II feed almost every other chapter.

**Part III, Signals, Electronics and Fields,** covers signal processing, analogue electronics, electromagnetics and the properties of electrical materials.

**Part IV, Power, Control and Communications,** covers three-phase power, machines and transformers, feedback control and communication systems.

**Part V, Digital and Computer Engineering,** covers digital logic, computer architecture, computer networks and software engineering—the areas that electrical engineers most often neglect, and therefore some of the cheapest marks on the paper.

**Part VI, Exam Mastery,** brings everything together. Chapter 16 is about strategy: time management, the Reference Handbook, the calculator and the psychology of exam day, with a timed mixed drill. Chapters 17 and 18 are two complete, full-length mock exams with answer keys, scoring sheets and worked solutions.

At the back you will find a formula summary, a table of constants and conversions, and a glossary.

## How to Use the Questions {.unnumbered}

Every teaching chapter from Chapter 2 onwards ends with thirty-six practice questions, followed immediately by worked solutions. Resist the temptation to read a question, glance at the options and then read the solution. That feels productive and teaches almost nothing. Instead:

1. Work each question on paper, with your approved calculator, as though it counted.
2. Commit to an answer before you look at the solution.
3. Mark it, and then read the solution *even if you were right*. The solutions explain why the wrong options are wrong, and that is often where the learning is.
4. Keep an error log—a simple notebook in which you write down every question you got wrong and, in one sentence, *why*. Chapter 1 shows you how.

The distractors in this book are not random numbers. Each wrong option was built from a specific, common mistake: forgetting the factor of $\sqrt{3}$, using a peak value where an RMS value was needed, dropping the $2\pi$ from an angular frequency, using the line voltage where the phase voltage belonged. If you choose a wrong answer, the chances are good that the solution names the exact slip you made. Learn the slip and you will not make it on exam day.

## A Note on Language and Units {.unnumbered}

This book is written in British English, so you will read *analyse*, *behaviour* and *programme*. The exam itself is American and uses American spelling and conventions, including 60 Hz power systems in most examples and dollars in engineering economics. None of that will trouble you. SI units are used throughout, as they are on the exam, with the occasional US customary unit where the exam would use one.

## A Note on Accuracy and Currency {.unnumbered}

NCEES revises its exam specifications and the *FE Reference Handbook* from time to time. Everything in this book reflects the computer-based FE Electrical and Computer exam as it stood at the time of writing. Before you register, and again in the weeks before your exam, check the NCEES website for the current specification, the current Handbook and the current list of approved calculators. If anything has changed, the official source always wins.

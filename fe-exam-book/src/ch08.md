Electronics is worth seven to eleven questions, which puts it among the larger knowledge areas. It also has a reputation for being treacherous, mostly because textbooks on the subject are thick and full of device physics. The good news is that the exam is far more practical than the textbooks. It wants to know whether you can find the current through a diode, the operating point of a transistor, the gain of an op-amp circuit or the ripple on a power supply. Every one of those questions reduces to the circuit analysis you have already mastered, once each device is replaced by the right simple model.

That is the theme of this chapter: **model, then analyse**. A diode becomes a 0.7 V battery. A transistor becomes a current source controlled by its base or gate. An op-amp becomes two rules. Pick the right model, check that your assumption about the device's operating state is consistent, and the rest is Kirchhoff.

## Diodes

A semiconductor diode conducts readily in one direction (forward bias) and blocks in the other (reverse bias). Its exact behaviour follows the Shockley equation,

$$i_D = I_S\left(e^{v_D/(nV_T)} - 1\right)$$

where $I_S$ is the tiny saturation current, $n$ the ideality factor (between 1 and 2) and $V_T = kT/q \approx 26$ mV at room temperature the thermal voltage. Figure 8.1 shows the characteristic.

![The current–voltage characteristic of a silicon diode. Forward current rises steeply near 0.7 V; reverse current is negligible until breakdown. A Zener diode is designed to operate in breakdown at a well-defined voltage.](figures/fig08_diode_iv.png){width=82%}

For hand analysis, the exponential is replaced by simpler models:

- **Ideal diode:** a short circuit when forward-biased, an open circuit when reverse-biased.
- **Constant-voltage-drop model:** a 0.7 V source when forward-biased (silicon), open when reverse-biased. Use 0.3 V for germanium, about 2 V for red LEDs and about 3 V for blue or white LEDs.

The constant-voltage-drop model answers most exam questions. The method is always the same: **assume** each diode is on or off, **solve** the circuit, then **check**. An "on" diode must carry positive forward current; an "off" diode must have less than 0.7 V across it. If either check fails, change the assumption and try again.

::: {.example}
A 9 V source drives a red LED ($V_F = 2.0$ V) at 15 mA. The series resistor must drop $9 - 2 = 7$ V at 15 mA, so $R = 7/0.015 = 467$ Ω. Choose the next standard value above, 470 Ω.
:::

### Rectifiers

A **half-wave rectifier** passes only the positive half-cycles of an AC input. A **full-wave bridge** uses four diodes to pass both halves, flipping the negative ones. For a sinusoidal input of peak $V_m$, after subtracting diode drops to get the output peak $V_p$:

$$V_{dc,\,half} = \frac{V_p}{\pi} \approx 0.318V_p, \qquad V_{dc,\,full} = \frac{2V_p}{\pi} \approx 0.637V_p$$

A bridge has *two* diodes conducting at any instant, so it loses about 1.4 V rather than 0.7 V. And remember that a transformer secondary is usually specified in RMS: a 12 V secondary has a peak of $12\sqrt2 = 17.0$ V.

### Filtering and ripple

A large reservoir capacitor across the output charges to the peak on each cycle and then supplies the load until the next peak, as Figure 8.2 shows. The voltage sags slightly between peaks; this sag is the **ripple**. If the load draws a roughly constant current $I$,

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{I}{fC}\ \text{(half-wave)}, \qquad \Delta V \approx \frac{I}{2fC}\ \text{(full-wave)}$$

where $f$ is the mains frequency. Full-wave rectification halves the ripple because the capacitor is recharged twice per cycle.

![A rectifier with a reservoir capacitor. The capacitor charges to the peak and discharges into the load between peaks, leaving a small ripple. Full-wave rectification (right) recharges twice as often.](figures/fig08_rectifier.png){width=92%}

### Zener regulators

A Zener diode operated in reverse breakdown holds a nearly constant voltage over a wide range of current. In the simple shunt regulator, a series resistor $R_S$ drops the difference between the supply and the Zener voltage, and the current through it divides between the Zener and the load:

$$I_S = \frac{V_s - V_Z}{R_S}, \qquad I_L = \frac{V_Z}{R_L}, \qquad I_Z = I_S - I_L$$

The regulator works only while $I_Z$ stays positive (above a small minimum). If the load draws more than $I_S$, the Zener drops out of breakdown and the output collapses. Exam questions often ask for $I_Z$, for the minimum load resistance, or for the power dissipated in the Zener, $P_Z = V_ZI_Z$, which is greatest when the load is disconnected.

## Bipolar Junction Transistors

A BJT has three terminals—collector, base and emitter—and comes in NPN and PNP forms. In the **active region**, a small base current controls a much larger collector current:

$$I_C = \beta I_B, \qquad I_E = I_C + I_B = (\beta + 1)I_B$$

with $V_{BE} \approx 0.7$ V for silicon. The current gain $\beta$ (also written $h_{FE}$) typically lies between 50 and 300 and varies widely between devices of the same type, which is why good bias circuits are designed to be insensitive to it.

### Regions of operation

| Region | Base–emitter junction | Base–collector junction | Behaviour |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Cut-off | Reverse | Reverse | Off; $I_C \approx 0$ |
| Active | Forward | Reverse | Amplifier; $I_C = \beta I_B$ |
| Saturation | Forward | Forward | Closed switch; $V_{CE} \approx 0.2$ V |

Table: BJT operating regions (NPN).

Amplifiers operate in the active region. Digital switches alternate between cut-off and saturation. After any bias calculation, check the region: if $V_{CE}$ comes out above about 0.2 V, the active-region assumption holds; if it comes out lower (or negative), the transistor is saturated and $I_C$ is set by the external circuit, not by $\beta$.

### DC biasing

The simplest arrangement, **fixed bias**, feeds the base from $V_{CC}$ through a resistor $R_B$:

$$I_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE}}{R_B}, \qquad I_C = \beta I_B, \qquad V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_CR_C$$

It is easy to analyse but poor in practice, because $I_C$ is directly proportional to the unpredictable $\beta$.

The **voltage-divider bias** circuit of Figure 8.3 is the workhorse. A divider sets the base voltage, and an emitter resistor provides negative feedback that stabilises the current.

![Voltage-divider bias. The divider $R_1$–$R_2$ fixes the base voltage; the emitter resistor $R_E$ sets the emitter current almost independently of $\beta$.](figures/fig08_bjt_bias.png){width=58%}

If the divider current is much larger than the base current (the usual design rule, and the usual exam assumption when "$\beta$ is large"):

$$V_B = V_{CC}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}, \qquad V_E = V_B - 0.7, \qquad I_C \approx I_E = \frac{V_E}{R_E}$$

$$V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_C(R_C + R_E)$$

When the question gives a specific $\beta$ and you want more accuracy, replace the divider with its Thévenin equivalent ($V_{Th} = V_B$, $R_{Th} = R_1\parallel R_2$) and write KVL around the base loop: $V_{Th} = I_BR_{Th} + 0.7 + (\beta + 1)I_BR_E$.

### Small-signal operation

Around its bias point, a BJT behaves as a voltage-controlled current source with **transconductance**

$$g_m = \frac{I_C}{V_T} \approx \frac{I_C}{26\ \text{mV}}$$

and input resistance $r_\pi = \beta/g_m$ looking into the base. For a common-emitter amplifier with the emitter resistor bypassed by a capacitor, the voltage gain is

$$A_v \approx -g_m(R_C \parallel R_L)$$

The minus sign indicates inversion. If the emitter resistor is not bypassed, local feedback reduces the gain to roughly $-R_C/R_E$, trading gain for stability and linearity. The three basic configurations have characteristic personalities worth knowing for conceptual questions:

| Configuration | Voltage gain | Input impedance | Output impedance | Typical use |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Common emitter | High, inverting | Moderate | High | General amplification |
| Common collector (emitter follower) | ≈ 1 | High | Low | Buffering |
| Common base | High, non-inverting | Low | High | High-frequency amplifiers |

Table: The three BJT amplifier configurations.

## MOSFETs

The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor is controlled by gate *voltage* rather than base current, and its gate draws essentially no DC current. An n-channel enhancement MOSFET is off until the gate–source voltage exceeds the threshold $V_t$. Above threshold, it operates in one of two regions:

- **Triode (ohmic):** $v_{DS} < v_{GS} - V_t$. The device behaves like a voltage-controlled resistor. This is the "on" state of a MOSFET switch.
- **Saturation:** $v_{DS} \ge v_{GS} - V_t$. The drain current is almost independent of $v_{DS}$:
$$i_D = K(v_{GS} - V_t)^2$$

Be careful with the definition of $K$. Some texts write $i_D = \frac{1}{2}k'\frac{W}{L}(v_{GS} - V_t)^2$, absorbing the half into the constant differently. Exam questions will state which form they use; read it and use it exactly. Note too that "saturation" means opposite things for BJTs and MOSFETs: a saturated BJT is a closed switch, but a saturated MOSFET is the region used for amplification.

The small-signal transconductance in saturation is $g_m = 2K(V_{GS} - V_t) = 2\sqrt{KI_D}$.

### The CMOS inverter

Pairing an n-channel and a p-channel MOSFET gives the CMOS inverter, the building block of nearly all digital logic. With the input high, the NMOS conducts and the PMOS is off, pulling the output low; with the input low, the roles reverse. In either steady state one transistor is off, so almost no static current flows. Power is consumed mainly when the output switches and charges or discharges the load capacitance:

$$P_{dyn} \approx \alpha CV_{DD}^2f$$

where $\alpha$ is the fraction of cycles in which the node switches. This is why lowering the supply voltage is the most effective way to reduce power in digital chips.

## Operational Amplifiers

The op-amp is the most useful analogue building block ever invented, and the easiest to analyse. An **ideal op-amp** has infinite open-loop gain, infinite input impedance and zero output impedance. With negative feedback connected, those properties lead to two golden rules:

1. **No current flows into either input terminal.**
2. **The output does whatever is needed to make the two input voltages equal.**

Every op-amp circuit on the exam yields to these two rules plus KCL at the inverting input.

### The standard circuits

**Inverting amplifier** (Figure 8.4, left). The non-inverting input is grounded, so the inverting input sits at 0 V—a **virtual earth**. The input current $V_{in}/R_{in}$ cannot enter the op-amp, so it all flows through $R_f$:

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = -\frac{R_f}{R_{in}}$$

![The inverting (left) and non-inverting (right) amplifiers. Both rely on negative feedback through $R_f$; the golden rules give their gains in one line.](figures/fig08_opamps.png){width=95%}

**Non-inverting amplifier** (Figure 8.4, right). The input drives the non-inverting terminal directly; the feedback divider returns a fraction of the output:

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}$$

Its gain is never less than 1, and its input impedance is very high. With $R_f = 0$ and $R_1$ removed, it becomes a **voltage follower** with unity gain: a perfect buffer.

**Summing amplifier.** Several inputs, each through its own resistor, feed the virtual earth:

$$V_{out} = -R_f\left(\frac{V_1}{R_1} + \frac{V_2}{R_2} + \cdots\right)$$

**Difference amplifier.** With matched resistor ratios, $V_{out} = \frac{R_2}{R_1}(V_2 - V_1)$, amplifying the difference between two signals and rejecting what they have in common.

**Integrator.** Replace $R_f$ with a capacitor $C$:

$$v_{out}(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int v_{in}\,dt$$

A constant input produces a linear ramp. **Differentiator:** swap the positions of $R$ and $C$ to get $v_{out} = -RC\,dv_{in}/dt$. Differentiators amplify high-frequency noise and are rarely used without modification, a point that occasionally surfaces in conceptual questions.

::: {.trap}
In the inverting configuration, it is tempting to write the gain as $-R_{in}/R_f$, or to forget the minus sign. In the non-inverting configuration, it is tempting to forget the "1 +". A quick check: if $R_f = 0$, the inverting amplifier must give zero output and the non-inverting amplifier must give unity gain.
:::

### Real op-amp limitations

**Saturation.** The output cannot exceed the supply rails (often a volt or two inside them). An inverting amplifier with a gain of $-100$ and an input of 0.2 V, powered from $\pm15$ V, does not produce $-20$ V; it saturates near $-13$ V.

**Gain–bandwidth product.** A real op-amp's open-loop gain falls with frequency. For the common single-pole devices, gain multiplied by bandwidth is constant. A device with a 1 MHz gain–bandwidth product, used as a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of 20, has a closed-loop bandwidth of about 50 kHz.

**Slew rate.** The output voltage can change only so fast, typically a fraction of a volt to tens of volts per microsecond. A sinusoid of peak $V_p$ at frequency $f$ requires a slew rate of at least $2\pi fV_p$; above that, the output becomes distorted towards a triangle.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *An NPN transistor with $\beta = 100$ is biased with $V_{CC} = 12$ V, $R_1 = 47$ kΩ, $R_2 = 10$ kΩ, $R_C = 2.2$ kΩ and $R_E = 1$ kΩ. The emitter resistor is bypassed. Estimate the small-signal voltage gain with no external load.*

**Recognise the type.** Two stages: DC bias first, then small-signal gain. The gain depends on $g_m$, which depends on $I_C$, so the bias must come first.

**Bias.** $V_B = 12 \times 10/57 = 2.11$ V; $V_E = 1.41$ V; $I_C \approx I_E = 1.41$ mA. Check the region: $V_{CE} = 12 - 1.41(3.2) = 7.5$ V, comfortably active. (The divider current, about 0.21 mA, is fifteen times the base current of 14 μA, so the "large $\beta$" approximation is reasonable.)

**Small signal.** $g_m = 1.41/26 = 54.2$ mS. With $R_E$ bypassed, $A_v = -g_mR_C = -54.2\times10^{-3}\times2200 = -119$.

**Sanity-check.** A bypassed common-emitter stage typically has a gain of order 100, so this is plausible. If $R_E$ were *not* bypassed, the gain would collapse to about $-R_C/R_E = -2.2$, a figure that might appear among the distractors.

Electromagnetics has a fearsome reputation among electrical engineers, built in large part on long nights with vector calculus. The FE exam is far kinder. Its five to eight electromagnetics questions, and four to six on electrical materials, are almost always applications of a handful of formulae to simple geometries: a point charge, a parallel-plate capacitor, a long straight wire, a solenoid, a uniform plane wave, a transmission line with a mismatched load. If you can identify the geometry, find the formula in the Handbook and keep your units in order, you can collect these marks in two or three minutes each.

Units deserve special mention. Electromagnetics problems are full of tiny constants ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m, $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m) and awkward unit prefixes (centimetres, millimetres, microcoulombs, nanofarads). Most wrong answers in this area are not conceptual errors at all. They are a square centimetre treated as a square metre, or a microcoulomb treated as a coulomb. Convert everything to base SI units before you substitute, every time.

## Electrostatics

### Coulomb's law

Two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by a distance $r$ exert a force on each other of magnitude

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon}\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}$$

where $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of the surrounding medium and $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) \approx 8.99\times10^9$ N·m²/C². Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. The force acts along the line joining the charges. When several charges are present, forces add as vectors—a question about three charges at the corners of a triangle is really a question about vector addition.

### Electric field and potential

The electric field is force per unit charge, $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{F}/q$, measured in V/m. Around a point charge,

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon r^2}, \qquad V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon r}$$

The potential $V$ falls off as $1/r$ while the field falls off as $1/r^2$. Potential is a scalar, so potentials from several charges simply add—often a much quicker calculation than adding field vectors. The field is the negative gradient of the potential, $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$; it points from high potential to low. Figure 9.1 shows the field pattern of a pair of opposite charges.

![Electric field lines of two equal and opposite point charges. Lines leave positive charge and end on negative charge; their density indicates field strength. Equipotential surfaces (not shown) cross the field lines at right angles.](figures/fig09_field_lines.png){width=72%}

### Gauss's law

The total electric flux leaving a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by the permittivity:

$$\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\varepsilon}$$

For symmetric situations it gives fields almost effortlessly. Around an infinite line charge of density $\rho_L$ (C/m), $E = \rho_L/(2\pi\varepsilon r)$. Near an infinite sheet of surface density $\rho_S$ (C/m²), $E = \rho_S/(2\varepsilon)$, independent of distance. Inside a conductor in equilibrium, $E = 0$: charges rearrange themselves on the surface until the interior field vanishes, which is the principle of electrostatic screening and the Faraday cage.

## Capacitance and Dielectrics

A capacitor stores charge in proportion to voltage, $Q = CV$. For the parallel-plate geometry of Figure 9.2, neglecting fringing at the edges,

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A}{d}$$

![A parallel-plate capacitor. The field between the plates is uniform, $E = V/d$. Inserting a dielectric of relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ multiplies the capacitance by $\varepsilon_r$.](figures/fig09_capacitor.png){width=70%}

Inside, the field is uniform at $E = V/d$, and the stored energy is $W = \frac12CV^2$, or equivalently $\frac12\varepsilon E^2$ per unit volume.

A **dielectric** is an insulator whose molecules polarise in an applied field, partly cancelling it and allowing more charge to be stored at a given voltage. Its **relative permittivity** $\varepsilon_r$ (also called the dielectric constant) multiplies the capacitance: about 2.1 for PTFE, 4.4 for FR-4 circuit-board laminate, 6 for mica, 9–10 for alumina, and thousands for some ceramics. Every dielectric also has a **dielectric strength**, the maximum field it can withstand before breaking down and conducting—about 3 kV/mm for dry air and tens of kV/mm for good solid insulators. It sets the minimum thickness of insulation for a given voltage.

Two other capacitance formulae appear occasionally. For a coaxial cable of inner radius $a$ and outer radius $b$, $C' = 2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(b/a)$ per metre. For an isolated sphere, $C = 4\pi\varepsilon R$.

## Magnetostatics

### Magnetic fields from currents

Currents create magnetic fields. Around a long straight wire carrying current $I$, the field circles the wire with flux density

$$B = \frac{\mu I}{2\pi r}$$

and its direction follows the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl in the direction of $\mathbf{B}$. At the centre of a circular loop of radius $R$, $B = \mu I/(2R)$. Inside a long solenoid of $N$ turns and length $\ell$, the field is nearly uniform at

$$B = \frac{\mu NI}{\ell}$$

These results follow from **Ampère's law**, $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{enc}$, where the magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$ relates to the flux density by $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$. Remember which is which: $\mathbf{H}$ (A/m) is set by the currents, while $\mathbf{B}$ (teslas) depends also on the material through its permeability $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$.

### Flux and inductance

Magnetic flux through an area is $\Phi = \int\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, or simply $\Phi = BA$ for a uniform field perpendicular to a flat area, measured in webers. **Inductance** is flux linkage per unit current, $L = N\Phi/I$. For a long solenoid,

$$L = \frac{\mu N^2A}{\ell}$$

Inductance grows with the *square* of the number of turns, because each turn both contributes to the flux and is linked by it. A distractor that scales linearly with $N$ is a common feature of inductance questions.

### Magnetic circuits

In a ferromagnetic core, flux behaves much like current in a resistive circuit. The coil's **magnetomotive force** $\mathcal{F} = NI$ (ampere-turns) drives flux $\Phi$ through the core's **reluctance** $\mathcal{R} = \ell/(\mu A)$:

$$\Phi = \frac{NI}{\mathcal{R}}$$

Reluctances in series add, just like resistances. A small air gap, with $\mu_r = 1$ rather than thousands, often dominates the total reluctance of a magnetic circuit—which is why it controls the inductance of gapped inductors and the magnetising current of motors.

## Magnetic Forces

A charge moving through a magnetic field experiences the **Lorentz force**, perpendicular to both its velocity and the field:

$$\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$$

For a straight conductor of length $L$ carrying current $I$ in a uniform field, the force is

$$F = BIL\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the conductor and the field. The force is greatest when they are perpendicular and zero when they are parallel. This is the principle of every electric motor. Two parallel conductors carrying currents in the same direction attract; in opposite directions they repel, with force per unit length $\mu I_1I_2/(2\pi d)$. This force becomes dramatic during short-circuit faults, when busbars can be physically bent.

## Electromagnetic Induction

**Faraday's law** states that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force:

$$\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

The flux can change because the field changes, the area changes, or the orientation changes—a rotating coil in a steady field produces a sinusoidal EMF, which is how every generator works. For a uniform change, $|\mathcal{E}| = N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$. A conductor of length $L$ moving with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a field develops a motional EMF $\mathcal{E} = BLv$.

The minus sign expresses **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change producing it. Push a magnet towards a coil and the induced current makes the coil repel the magnet. Lenz's law is conservation of energy in disguise—if the induced current *aided* the change, you would have a perpetual-motion machine.

## Maxwell's Equations in Words

The four Maxwell equations summarise all of classical electromagnetics. The exam tests their meaning more than their mathematics.

| Equation | Name | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_v$ | Gauss's law | Electric flux originates on charges |
| $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ | Gauss's law for magnetism | There are no magnetic monopoles; field lines close on themselves |
| $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ | Faraday's law | A changing magnetic field induces a circulating electric field |
| $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ | Ampère–Maxwell law | Currents, and changing electric fields, produce circulating magnetic fields |

Table: Maxwell's equations.

Maxwell's addition of the displacement current $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ to Ampère's law is what makes electromagnetic waves possible: a changing electric field creates a magnetic field, whose change creates an electric field, and the disturbance propagates.

## Electromagnetic Waves

In a lossless medium, electromagnetic waves travel at

$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}}$$

with $c \approx 3\times10^8$ m/s in free space. Wavelength is $\lambda = v/f$. In a non-magnetic dielectric, both speed and wavelength are reduced by the factor $\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$—not $\varepsilon_r$ itself, a common distractor.

The ratio of the electric to the magnetic field in a plane wave is the medium's **intrinsic impedance**:

$$\eta = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}}, \qquad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} \approx 377\ \Omega$$

In a non-magnetic dielectric, $\eta = 377/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ Ω. The power density carried by the wave is given by the Poynting vector, $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$, with average magnitude $E_0^2/(2\eta)$ for a sinusoidal wave of peak field $E_0$.

### Skin depth

In a good conductor, an alternating field decays exponentially with depth, so current crowds towards the surface. The **skin depth**—the depth at which the field falls to $1/e$ of its surface value—is

$$\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}$$

For copper at 60 Hz it is about 8.5 mm; at 1 MHz about 66 μm; at 1 GHz about 2 μm. The consequences are everywhere: hollow conductors for large busbars, stranded (Litz) wire for high-frequency inductors, silver plating on microwave components, and the shielding effectiveness of thin metal enclosures.

## Transmission Lines

When a conductor's length becomes a significant fraction of a wavelength, it must be treated as a **transmission line** with a characteristic impedance $Z_0$, typically 50 Ω for coaxial RF systems, 75 Ω for television and 300 Ω for twin-lead. A line terminated in a load $Z_L$ that differs from $Z_0$ reflects part of the incident wave. The **reflection coefficient** at the load is

$$\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$

It ranges from $-1$ (short circuit) through 0 (matched load) to $+1$ (open circuit). The incident and reflected waves combine into a standing wave, shown in Figure 9.3, whose ratio of maximum to minimum voltage is the **voltage standing-wave ratio**:

$$\text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|}$$

![Standing waves on a transmission line for three loads. A matched load gives a flat voltage profile (VSWR = 1); mismatches create maxima and minima a quarter-wavelength apart.](figures/fig09_standing_wave.png){width=88%}

For a purely resistive load, there is a handy shortcut: VSWR equals $Z_L/Z_0$ or $Z_0/Z_L$, whichever is greater than 1. A 100 Ω load on a 50 Ω line has VSWR = 2 and $|\Gamma| = 1/3$. The fraction of incident power reflected is $|\Gamma|^2$, so about 11% of the power is reflected in that case.

The input impedance of a lossless line of length $\ell$ is given in the Handbook. One special case is worth knowing by heart. A **quarter-wavelength** line inverts the load impedance:

$$Z_{in} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}$$

This makes the **quarter-wave transformer**: to match a load $R_L$ to a line $Z_1$, insert a quarter-wave section of characteristic impedance $\sqrt{Z_1R_L}$. A half-wavelength line, by contrast, simply repeats the load impedance.

## Properties of Electrical Materials

This knowledge area overlaps with Chapter 4 (resistivity) and Chapter 8 (semiconductors). Here we gather the remaining ideas.

### Conductors

Resistivity rises with temperature in metals, because lattice vibrations scatter electrons. The relation $R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$ from Chapter 4 applies. Annealed copper sets the standard for conductivity (about $5.8\times10^7$ S/m); aluminium has about 61% of copper's conductivity but less than a third of its density, which is why overhead power lines are aluminium.

### Semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor has equal numbers of electrons and holes, generated thermally; their concentration rises steeply with temperature, so an intrinsic semiconductor's resistance *falls* as it warms. **Doping** controls conductivity. Group V atoms (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony) donate electrons to make **n-type** material; group III atoms (boron, gallium, indium) accept electrons, creating holes, to make **p-type** material. Conductivity is

$$\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$$

and in doped material the majority carriers dominate, so for n-type silicon $\sigma \approx qN_D\mu_n$. In silicon, electron mobility (about 1350 cm²/V·s) is nearly three times hole mobility (about 480), which is why n-channel devices are faster than p-channel ones of the same size.

The **band gap** $E_g$ determines optical behaviour: a photon can create an electron–hole pair only if its energy exceeds $E_g$. With $E = hc/\lambda$ and $hc \approx 1240$ eV·nm, the longest absorbed (or emitted) wavelength is $\lambda = 1240/E_g$ nm. Silicon ($E_g = 1.12$ eV) responds to wavelengths below about 1100 nm; gallium arsenide (1.42 eV) emits near 870 nm in the infrared.

### Magnetic materials

Materials respond to magnetic fields in three main ways. **Diamagnetic** materials ($\mu_r$ slightly below 1, such as copper) weakly oppose a field. **Paramagnetic** materials ($\mu_r$ slightly above 1, such as aluminium) weakly reinforce it. **Ferromagnetic** materials (iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys) have relative permeabilities in the hundreds or thousands, due to the alignment of magnetic domains. Above the **Curie temperature**, thermal agitation destroys the alignment and a ferromagnet becomes paramagnetic.

Ferromagnets exhibit **hysteresis**: their flux density depends on their history, tracing the loop of Figure 9.4. The area of the loop is the energy lost per cycle per unit volume.

![The B–H hysteresis loop of a ferromagnetic material. Remanence $B_r$ remains when $H$ returns to zero; coercive force $H_c$ is needed to drive $B$ back to zero. Soft magnetic materials (narrow loop) suit transformers; hard materials (wide loop) suit permanent magnets.](figures/fig09_bh_curve.png){width=72%}

Soft materials with narrow loops, such as silicon steel and ferrites, are used in transformer and motor cores to minimise hysteresis loss; laminating the core reduces the other main loss, eddy currents. Hard materials with wide loops, such as neodymium–iron–boron, make permanent magnets.

### Other effects worth recognising

- **Piezoelectric** materials, such as quartz, generate a voltage when stressed and deform when a voltage is applied—the basis of crystal oscillators.
- **Thermocouples** generate a small voltage from the temperature difference between junctions of two dissimilar metals (the Seebeck effect).
- **Superconductors** have zero DC resistance below a critical temperature and expel magnetic flux (the Meissner effect).
- The **Hall effect** produces a transverse voltage when a current-carrying material sits in a magnetic field; Hall sensors measure current and position without contact.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A 50 Ω coaxial line feeds a 200 Ω resistive antenna at 100 MHz. The line's dielectric has $\varepsilon_r = 2.25$. What is the VSWR, and how long must a quarter-wave matching section be if it uses the same dielectric?*

**Part 1: VSWR.** For a resistive load, VSWR $= 200/50 = 4$. Check with $\Gamma = (200 - 50)/(200 + 50) = 0.6$: $(1 + 0.6)/(1 - 0.6) = 4$. About 36% of the incident power is reflected ($\Gamma^2$), which is why matching matters.

**Part 2: section impedance.** Although only the length was asked for, note that the section needs $Z_q = \sqrt{50\times200} = 100$ Ω.

**Part 3: length.** In the dielectric, $v = c/\sqrt{2.25} = 2\times10^8$ m/s, so $\lambda = v/f = 2$ m, and a quarter-wavelength is **0.5 m**. Using the free-space wavelength (3 m) would give 0.75 m; dividing by $\varepsilon_r$ instead of its square root would give 0.33 m. Both are classic distractors.

## Common Traps in Electromagnetics and Materials

**Square roots of permittivity.** Wave speed, wavelength and intrinsic impedance all scale with $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, but capacitance scales with $\varepsilon_r$ itself. Keep the two straight and many distractors disappear.

**Radius or diameter, centimetres or metres.** Geometry questions are designed to catch unit slips. Write every dimension in metres, with its exponent, before substituting.

**$B$ and $H$.** Ampère's law gives $H$ from the currents; multiply by $\mu$ to get $B$. In a ferromagnetic core, forgetting $\mu_r$ underestimates the flux density by a factor of hundreds or thousands.

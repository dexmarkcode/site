# Mock Exam One

::: {.objectives}
- A complete 110-question mock exam in two 55-question sections
- Knowledge areas weighted to match the NCEES specification
- Instructions for sitting it under realistic conditions
- An answer key, a scoring sheet by knowledge area and full worked solutions
- A method for turning your results into a targeted repair plan
:::

This is the moment the whole book has been building towards. Everything up to now has been practice in short bursts, one topic at a time, with the chapter heading telling you which tools to reach for. The real exam gives you no such clues. Question 31 might be a Bode plot, question 32 a subnet mask and question 33 an ethics dilemma. Switching between topics at speed, without warning, is a skill of its own, and this mock exam is where you practise it.

The questions that follow are distributed across the eighteen knowledge areas in close proportion to the NCEES specification. They are written at the level of the real exam: some quick, most standard, a few long. They are presented in two sections of 55 questions, as on the day.

## How to Sit This Exam

Treat this as a dress rehearsal. The more realistic the conditions, the more useful the result.

**Choose your time.** Set aside a full morning or afternoon—about six hours—when you will not be interrupted. If possible, start at the same time of day as your real exam appointment, so that your body gets used to concentrating at that hour.

**Set up your desk.** Have your approved calculator, the *FE Reference Handbook* open on a screen (not printed, since the real one is electronic), some blank paper to stand in for the writing booklet, and a clock or timer. Nothing else: no notes, no textbook, no phone, and certainly not the solutions at the back of this chapter.

**Keep strict time.**

| Part | Time allowed |
|:--|:--|
| Section 1 (questions 1–55) | 2 hours 40 minutes |
| Break | Up to 25 minutes |
| Section 2 (questions 56–110) | 2 hours 40 minutes |

When Section 1's time is up, stop, even in mid-question. During the break, do not look back at Section 1—on the real exam, it would be submitted and locked. Use the three-pass method from Chapter 16 in each section, and record a best guess for every question before time expires.

**Record your answers.** Write each answer on a separate sheet, numbered 1 to 110, rather than marking the book. You will want to sit this exam again later without seeing your previous choices.

**Note your confidence.** Next to each answer, put a mark: ✓ for "sure", ? for "probably", × for "guess". This takes no time at all and makes the post-mortem far more informative, because a question you got right by guessing is still a weakness.

::: {.tip}
Resist the urge to check an answer during the exam by turning to the solutions "just this once". It destroys the value of the whole exercise. The point is not to get a high score today; it is to find out exactly where you would lose marks on the real day while there is still time to fix it.
:::

## Scoring Your Exam

When both sections are finished, mark your answers against the answer key that follows the questions. Then complete the scoring sheet, which groups the questions by knowledge area. Your total tells you where you stand overall; the breakdown tells you where to work.

There is no official conversion from a practice score to a pass or fail, and anyone who claims to know the exact pass mark is guessing. As a working guide:

- **75% or above** (around 83 questions or more): you are in a strong position. Keep your skills fresh, review your error log and sit Mock Exam Two under the same conditions.
- **65–75%** (around 72–82 correct): you are close. The gap can be closed with a fortnight of targeted repair on your weakest areas.
- **Below 65%**: you need more preparation before the real exam. Look carefully at the area breakdown; the problem is usually concentrated in a few areas, not spread evenly, and those areas can be fixed.

Remember that the real exam brings nerves and unfamiliar surroundings, which cost most candidates a few percentage points. Aim to score comfortably above the level you think you need.

## The Post-Mortem

The mock exam earns most of its value *after* you have marked it. Set aside a further two or three hours, and work through the questions in this order:

1. **Every wrong answer.** Read the worked solution carefully. Then decide which of four categories the error falls into, and write it in your error log:
   - **Knowledge gap:** you did not know the concept or formula. *Fix:* reread the relevant chapter section and work its practice questions again.
   - **Method error:** you knew the topic but set the problem up wrongly. *Fix:* rework the problem from scratch without looking, then do two similar problems.
   - **Careless slip:** arithmetic, units, a misread question, a calculator in the wrong mode. *Fix:* identify the specific check that would have caught it, and add it to your exam-day ritual.
   - **Time pressure:** you could have done it, but rushed or guessed. *Fix:* practise that question type until it becomes a quick win.
2. **Every correct guess** (marked × or ?). Treat these exactly like wrong answers; next time, luck may not be on your side.
3. **Every question that took more than five minutes.** Ask whether there was a faster route. Often there is: a shortcut formula, a better choice of method, a quicker way to find something in the Handbook.

When you have finished, count your errors by category and by knowledge area. The pattern will tell you what to do with the time you have left. Most candidates find that careless slips and a small number of weak areas account for the majority of their lost marks—which is good news, because both are fixable in days rather than weeks.

## A Repair Plan

Allocate your remaining study time in proportion to *marks available*, not to how uncomfortable a topic makes you feel. A knowledge area worth ten questions in which you scored 40% offers six marks of improvement; one worth four questions in which you scored 50% offers two. Start with the larger opportunity.

For each weak area:

- reread the chapter's summary box and the sections relevant to your errors;
- rework the chapter's practice questions that you got wrong the first time;
- write the key formulae for that area from memory, then check them against the Handbook;
- finish with a short timed set of mixed questions, including the weak area, to confirm the repair holds up when topics are interleaved.

Then, about a week later, sit Mock Exam Two under the same conditions and compare.

## What Each Knowledge Area Will Test

Before you begin, it helps to know what to expect from each part of the paper. The notes below summarise the kinds of question you will meet in this mock exam—and on the real one—and point you to the chapter that covers each area. Read them now, then set them aside: during the exam itself you should rely on the Handbook and your own preparation.

**Mathematics (Chapter 2).** Expect quick algebra, logarithms, complex-number conversions, determinants and simultaneous equations, derivatives and integrals, eigenvalues, vectors, first- and second-order differential equations and simple numerical methods. These questions are mostly short; aim to average two minutes or less.

**Probability and statistics (Chapter 3).** Descriptive statistics, the binomial, Poisson, normal and exponential distributions, Bayes' theorem, reliability block diagrams and confidence intervals. Identifying the right distribution is usually the key step.

**Ethics and professional practice (Chapter 3).** Scenarios about public safety, competence, conflicts of interest, seals, confidentiality, licensure and intellectual property. Read every option; the best answer is usually the one that protects the public honestly and within the engineer's competence.

**Engineering economics (Chapter 3).** Present and future worth, annuities, capital recovery, effective interest, depreciation, break-even and comparison of alternatives. Draw a cash-flow diagram and pick the right factor.

**Properties of electrical materials (Chapters 4 and 9).** Resistivity and temperature coefficients, semiconductor doping and conductivity, dielectrics, magnetic materials and band gaps. Watch your units.

**Circuit analysis (Chapters 4 and 5).** The largest area: series–parallel reduction, dividers, nodal and mesh analysis, superposition, Thévenin and Norton equivalents, maximum power, impedance, resonance, AC power and power-factor correction. Speed here buys time everywhere else.

**Linear systems (Chapter 6).** RC and RL transients, second-order damping, Laplace transforms, transfer functions and poles, initial and final values, and convolution.

**Signal processing (Chapter 7).** Sampling and aliasing, ADC resolution, Fourier series, digital filters, z-transform stability, the DFT and decibels.

**Electronics (Chapter 8).** Diode circuits, rectifiers and ripple, Zener regulators, BJT and MOSFET biasing, small-signal gain and op-amp circuits.

**Power systems (Chapter 10).** Three-phase relationships and power, transformers, per-unit calculations, fault currents and rotating machines.

**Electromagnetics (Chapter 9).** Point charges, capacitance, magnetic fields around conductors, induction, wave propagation, skin depth and transmission lines.

**Control systems (Chapter 11).** Block diagrams, second-order response specifications, steady-state error, Routh stability and Bode margins.

**Communications (Chapter 12).** AM and FM, channel capacity, digital modulation, noise and link budgets.

**Computer networks (Chapter 14).** The OSI model, protocols, IPv4 subnetting, devices and delays.

**Digital systems (Chapter 13).** Number systems and two's complement, Boolean algebra and K-maps, flip-flops, counters, timing and memory.

**Computer systems (Chapter 14).** Performance equations, Amdahl's law, pipelining, caches, virtual memory and I/O.

**Software engineering (Chapter 15).** Code tracing, complexity, data structures, object-oriented concepts, development life cycles and testing.

The questions in this mock exam appear grouped broadly in the order above, as they often are on the computer-based exam. Do not let that lull you into treating each group as a separate test; keep your pace steady across the whole section, and remember that a question you find hard is often followed by several you find easy.

## A Word on Mindset

A mock exam can be an emotional experience. Somewhere around question forty you may feel that it is going badly. Nearly every candidate has that feeling at some point, and it is almost always wrong: the questions you struggle with stick in the mind, while the ones you answered smoothly fade. Keep going. Finish both sections, mark them honestly, and let the numbers—not your feelings during the exam—tell you where you stand.

And whatever the result, remember what it is for. A low mock score now, followed by targeted repair, is far better than a comfortable feeling followed by a disappointing real exam. Every wrong answer in this chapter is a mark you can still win on the day.

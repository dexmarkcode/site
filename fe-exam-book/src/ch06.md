Chapters 4 and 5 dealt with circuits that had settled down: DC circuits in which nothing changes, and AC circuits in a steady rhythm. This chapter is about the interesting moments in between—the instant a switch closes, a fault occurs or a step of voltage is applied. For a brief period, circuits containing capacitors and inductors do not jump straight to their new steady state. They glide, swing or ring towards it. That glide is the **transient response**, and describing it is the subject of the *Linear Systems* knowledge area.

There are two ways to analyse transients, and the exam uses both. The first is direct and physical: for first-order circuits, a simple three-step recipe gives the full answer in under a minute. The second is the Laplace transform, which turns differential equations into algebra and gives us the language of transfer functions, poles and zeros that runs through signals and control as well. We will learn the recipe first, because it answers most exam questions, and then build the more general tool.

## The Two Rules of Continuity

Everything about switching transients follows from two facts:

- The **voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously**, because $i = C\,dv/dt$ would require infinite current.
- The **current through an inductor cannot change instantaneously**, because $v = L\,di/dt$ would require infinite voltage.

So if a capacitor holds 5 V just before a switch moves ($t = 0^-$), it still holds 5 V just after ($t = 0^+$). If an inductor carries 2 A at $t = 0^-$, it carries 2 A at $t = 0^+$. Every other voltage and current in the circuit—including the capacitor's current and the inductor's voltage—*can* jump, and usually does.

These rules are the key to the initial conditions of every transient problem. Before the switch moves, the circuit is usually in DC steady state, so you find capacitor voltages and inductor currents by treating capacitors as open circuits and inductors as short circuits, exactly as in Chapter 4. Those values then carry across the switching instant unchanged.

::: {.keyidea}
On a transient question, your first job is always to find the capacitor voltages and inductor currents at $t = 0^-$. They are the only quantities you can carry across the switching instant. Everything else must be recalculated at $t = 0^+$ from those values.
:::

## First-Order Circuits

A circuit with one energy-storage element (or several that combine into one) is first-order. Its response to a sudden change always has the same exponential form:

$$x(t) = x(\infty) + \left[x(0^+) - x(\infty)\right]e^{-t/\tau}$$

where $x$ is any voltage or current in the circuit. Read it aloud and it is almost obvious: the quantity starts at its initial value, ends at its final value, and moves between them exponentially with time constant $\tau$.

### The three-step method

1. **Initial value** $x(0^+)$: find the capacitor voltage or inductor current at $t = 0^-$, carry it across, then find the quantity you want at $t = 0^+$.
2. **Final value** $x(\infty)$: redraw the circuit for the new steady state (capacitor open, inductor short) and solve it.
3. **Time constant** $\tau$: find the Thévenin resistance $R_{Th}$ seen by the energy-storage element with all independent sources killed. Then
$$\tau = R_{Th}C \quad\text{(RC)}, \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R_{Th}} \quad\text{(RL)}$$

Substitute the three numbers into the general form and you are done. No differential equation needs to be solved.

![Charging and discharging of an RC circuit. After one time constant the response has covered 63.2% of the distance to its final value; after five time constants it is within 1%.](figures/fig06_rc_charge.png){width=88%}

Figure 6.1 shows the familiar shapes. Some landmarks are worth memorising, because questions often ask "how long until…":

| Time | $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$ (rising) | $e^{-t/\tau}$ (falling) |
|:--|:--|:--|
| $0.69\tau$ | 50% | 50% |
| $1\tau$ | 63.2% | 36.8% |
| $2.3\tau$ | 90% | 10% |
| $3\tau$ | 95.0% | 5.0% |
| $4.6\tau$ | 99% | 1% |
| $5\tau$ | 99.3% | 0.7% |

Table: Landmark points of the exponential response.

For any other fraction, solve $t = \tau\ln\frac{x(0^+) - x(\infty)}{x(t) - x(\infty)}$, which follows directly from the general form.

::: {.trap}
For an RL circuit, the time constant is $L/R$, not $RL$. It is easy to see why if you check units: henries divided by ohms gives seconds. Henries multiplied by ohms gives nonsense, but it gives a number, and the distractor will be built from it.
:::

### The inductive kick

Consider Figure 6.2. An inductor has been connected to a DC source through $R_1$ for a long time, so it carries a steady current $I_0 = V_s/R_1$. A resistor $R_2$ sits across the inductor, but in steady state the inductor shorts it out. Now the source is disconnected.

![An inductor carrying a steady current has its source disconnected. The inductor current continues, momentarily unchanged, through $R_2$—producing a voltage that may be many times the original supply.](figures/fig06_rl_switch.png){width=80%}

The inductor current cannot change instantly, so at $t = 0^+$ the same current $I_0$ is forced through $R_2$, producing a voltage $I_0R_2$ across the inductor. If $R_2$ is large, that voltage can be enormous: 12 V through 4 Ω gives 3 A, and 3 A through 1 kΩ gives 3000 V. This is the physics behind the spark at a switch contact, the flyback diode across every relay coil and the ignition coil in a petrol engine. The exam loves it, because the candidate who forgets the continuity rule answers "12 V".

## Second-Order Circuits

A circuit with two independent energy-storage elements—typically one inductor and one capacitor—is second-order. For a series RLC circuit the governing equation, written for the capacitor voltage, is

$$LC\frac{d^2v}{dt^2} + RC\frac{dv}{dt} + v = v_s$$

Dividing through by $LC$ puts it in the standard form you met in Chapter 2:

$$\ddot{v} + 2\zeta\omega_0\dot{v} + \omega_0^2 v = \omega_0^2 v_s$$

with

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \alpha = \zeta\omega_0 = \frac{R}{2L}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} \quad\text{(series RLC)}$$

The quantity $\alpha$ is the **neper frequency** or damping coefficient. For a parallel RLC circuit, $\alpha = 1/(2RC)$ and $\zeta = \frac{1}{2R}\sqrt{L/C}$; once again the resistor's role is inverted.

The response type follows from $\zeta$ exactly as in Chapter 2. Figure 6.3 shows step responses for several values.

![Step responses of a second-order system for several damping ratios. Lightly damped systems respond quickly but overshoot and ring; heavily damped systems are sluggish.](figures/fig06_step_zeta.png){width=88%}

- **Overdamped** ($\zeta > 1$, $\alpha > \omega_0$): two real roots, $s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$. The response is a sum of two decaying exponentials.
- **Critically damped** ($\zeta = 1$): a repeated root $s = -\alpha$. The response has the form $(A + Bt)e^{-\alpha t}$, the fastest approach without overshoot.
- **Underdamped** ($\zeta < 1$): complex roots $s = -\alpha \pm j\omega_d$ with $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2}$. The response is a decaying sinusoid, $e^{-\alpha t}(A\cos\omega_dt + B\sin\omega_dt)$.

On the exam, second-order questions usually ask you to classify the response, find $\zeta$ or $\omega_0$, or find the frequency of the ringing ($\omega_d$). Full solutions with initial conditions for both constants are rare, because they take too long for a three-minute question.

## The Laplace Transform

The Laplace transform converts a function of time into a function of the complex frequency $s$:

$$F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt$$

You will never need to evaluate this integral on the exam. What you need is the table of transform pairs in the Handbook, a few properties, and the skill of partial fractions. The essential pairs are:

| $f(t)$ | $F(s)$ |
|:--|:--|
| $\delta(t)$ (unit impulse) | $1$ |
| $u(t)$ (unit step) | $1/s$ |
| $t\,u(t)$ (ramp) | $1/s^2$ |
| $e^{-at}u(t)$ | $1/(s+a)$ |
| $\sin(\omega t)u(t)$ | $\omega/(s^2 + \omega^2)$ |
| $\cos(\omega t)u(t)$ | $s/(s^2 + \omega^2)$ |
| $e^{-at}\sin(\omega t)u(t)$ | $\omega/((s+a)^2 + \omega^2)$ |

Table: Laplace transform pairs used constantly on the exam.

The two properties that make the transform useful are linearity and the derivative rule,

$$\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{df}{dt}\right\} = sF(s) - f(0^-)$$

which turns a differential equation into an algebraic one, with the initial conditions built in.

### Circuits in the s-domain

You can skip the differential equation altogether by transforming the circuit directly. Each element gets an s-domain impedance:

$$Z_R = R, \qquad Z_L = sL, \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{sC}$$

(compare $j\omega L$ and $1/j\omega C$ from Chapter 5—the Laplace impedance is the phasor impedance with $j\omega$ replaced by $s$). Initial conditions are modelled by extra sources: an inductor with initial current $I_0$ gains a series voltage source $LI_0$, and a capacitor with initial voltage $V_0$ gains a series source $V_0/s$. Then every technique from Chapter 4 applies, with $s$ carried through the algebra.

### Partial fractions

To return to the time domain, break $F(s)$ into simple terms that appear in the table. For distinct real poles, the **cover-up method** is fastest:

$$\frac{10}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{A}{s+1} + \frac{B}{s+3}, \qquad A = \left.\frac{10}{s+3}\right|_{s=-1} = 5, \qquad B = \left.\frac{10}{s+1}\right|_{s=-3} = -5$$

so $f(t) = 5(e^{-t} - e^{-3t})u(t)$. To find each coefficient, cover up its factor in the denominator and evaluate what remains at the pole.

## Transfer Functions, Poles and Zeros

For a linear system with zero initial conditions, the ratio of the output transform to the input transform is the **transfer function**:

$$H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}$$

It is a ratio of polynomials in $s$. The roots of the numerator are **zeros**; the roots of the denominator are **poles**. The poles are the most important single thing to know about a system, because they determine the form of its natural response. Figure 6.4 maps pole positions to responses.

![Pole positions in the s-plane and the natural responses they produce. Poles in the left half-plane give decaying responses (stable); poles on the imaginary axis give sustained oscillation; poles in the right half-plane give growing responses (unstable).](figures/fig06_pole_zero.png){width=92%}

The rules are simple and frequently tested:

- A pole at $s = -a$ (real, negative) contributes a decaying exponential $e^{-at}$.
- A complex pair at $s = -\alpha \pm j\omega_d$ contributes a decaying oscillation $e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$.
- **A system is stable if and only if all its poles lie in the left half of the s-plane.** A pole in the right half-plane, however small, produces a response that grows without limit.
- Poles on the imaginary axis (and not repeated) produce sustained oscillation: marginal stability.

Zeros do not affect stability. They shape the response—how much of each pole's mode appears in the output—but never decide whether it decays.

## The Initial and Final Value Theorems

These two theorems let you find the start and end of a time response directly from its transform, without inverting it:

$$f(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} sF(s), \qquad f(\infty) = \lim_{s\to0} sF(s)$$

The final value theorem comes with a condition that the exam likes to test: it is valid only if $sF(s)$ has all its poles in the left half-plane. A function such as $\sin\omega t$ has no final value, and blindly applying the theorem would give zero.

::: {.example}
For $F(s) = \dfrac{20(s+2)}{s(s+4)(s+5)}$, the poles of $sF(s)$ are at $-4$ and $-5$, both stable. The final value is $\lim_{s\to0}\dfrac{20(s+2)}{(s+4)(s+5)} = \dfrac{40}{20} = 2$.
:::

## Convolution and the Impulse Response

The output of a linear time-invariant (LTI) system is the convolution of its input with its **impulse response** $h(t)$:

$$y(t) = \int_0^t x(\tau)h(t - \tau)\,d\tau = x(t) * h(t)$$

In the s-domain this becomes simple multiplication, $Y(s) = H(s)X(s)$, which is precisely why transfer functions are so useful. The impulse response is the inverse transform of $H(s)$, and the step response is its integral.

In discrete time the same idea applies with a sum:

$$y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n - k]$$

Discrete convolution questions are common and quick if you organise them. Write the input sequence along the top of a grid, the impulse response down the side, fill in the products, and add along the diagonals. The length of the output is always one less than the sum of the two input lengths. Chapter 7 develops discrete-time systems further.

## Thinking Through an Exam Problem

> *A 100 μF capacitor charged to 50 V is connected at $t = 0$, through a 2 kΩ resistor, to a 10 V source whose positive terminal faces the capacitor's positive plate. What is the capacitor voltage 300 ms later?*

**Recognise the type.** One capacitor, one resistor, a source: first-order. Apply the three-step method.

**Initial value.** The capacitor holds 50 V, and its voltage cannot jump, so $v(0^+) = 50$ V.

**Final value.** In the new steady state no current flows, so the capacitor voltage equals the source: $v(\infty) = 10$ V. Notice that the capacitor *discharges* towards 10 V, not towards zero—a detail designed to catch candidates who reach for $V_0e^{-t/\tau}$ by reflex.

**Time constant.** $\tau = RC = 2000 \times 100\times10^{-6} = 0.2$ s.

**Combine.**
$$v(0.3) = 10 + (50 - 10)e^{-0.3/0.2} = 10 + 40(0.2231) = 18.9\ \text{V}$$

**Sanity-check.** After 1.5 time constants, the voltage should have covered about 78% of the 40 V journey, finishing near 19 V. It has.

## Common Traps in Transients and Linear Systems

Transient questions punish carelessness more than ignorance. Almost every wrong answer I see in this area comes from one of the following habits, and each has a simple antidote.

**Assuming the capacitor starts at zero.** Many candidates reach for $V(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ by reflex, because it is the first formula they learned. But the capacitor may already be charged, perhaps to a voltage *higher* than the new source, in which case it discharges towards the new final value. The antidote is to write the general form, $x(\infty) + [x(0^+) - x(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau}$, every time, and fill in the two values deliberately.

**Using the wrong resistance in the time constant.** The time constant uses the Thévenin resistance seen by the capacitor or inductor *in the circuit as it is after the switching event*. If a switch has removed a resistor, or the energy-storage element now discharges through a different path, the time constant changes. Draw the post-switching circuit, kill the sources, and look back from the terminals of the storage element.

**Forgetting continuity.** Only capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous. The current through a capacitor can jump from zero to its maximum in an instant, and the voltage across an inductor can leap to many times the supply voltage. If a question asks for the capacitor *current* at $t = 0^+$, find the capacitor *voltage* first, then use the circuit to find the current.

**Confusing $\omega_0$, $\omega_d$ and $\alpha$.** In second-order circuits, $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is the undamped natural frequency, $\alpha$ is the decay rate, and $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2}$ is the frequency at which the response actually rings. A question asking for the frequency of oscillation wants $\omega_d$; one asking whether the circuit oscillates wants a comparison of $\alpha$ with $\omega_0$.

**Applying the final value theorem blindly.** Before using $\lim_{s\to0}sF(s)$, check that the poles of $sF(s)$ lie in the left half-plane. If $F(s)$ contains $\omega/(s^2 + \omega^2)$, the signal oscillates forever and has no final value, whatever the theorem appears to give.

**Mixing up poles and zeros.** Poles come from the denominator and decide stability; zeros come from the numerator and do not. A transfer function with a zero in the right half-plane is still stable if its poles are in the left half-plane—it simply has an unusual, initially reversed step response.

**Discrete convolution indices.** When convolving short sequences by hand, misaligning the indices by one place is the commonest error. Use the grid method, and check that the output length is $N + M - 1$.

None of these traps involves difficult mathematics. They involve reading the circuit carefully before writing anything down. A useful discipline is to say aloud, or at least in your head, three things before you calculate: *what is continuous here, what is the final state, and what resistance does the storage element see?* With those three answered, the rest is arithmetic.

### A note on units and magnitudes

Transient questions often involve microfarads, millihenries, kilohms and milliseconds in the same problem. The time constant $RC$ with $R$ in kilohms and $C$ in microfarads comes out directly in milliseconds, a handy shortcut: 4.7 kΩ × 10 μF = 47 ms. Similarly, $L/R$ with $L$ in millihenries and $R$ in ohms gives milliseconds. Use these pairings to check the magnitude of every time constant you calculate. A time constant of 47 seconds for a small electronic circuit, or 47 microseconds for a large motor winding, should make you stop and look again.

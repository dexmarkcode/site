# The Grading Floor and the Long Road: Examiner's Notes, Common Faults and a Lifetime of Practice

::: chapter-intro
This final chapter brings everything together. It describes how gradings are structured across the kyū and dan ranks, explains what examiners are really looking for and how they weigh it, sets out a three-month preparation plan, deals honestly with nerves and with failure, and then looks beyond the last grading you will ever take to the decades of practice that follow.
:::

I have sat on both sides of the grading table many times. As a candidate I have been elated, relieved and, once or twice, quietly devastated. As an examiner I have watched hundreds of people perform kata, some brilliantly, many competently, a few disastrously. What I have learned from the examiner's chair is that gradings are much more predictable than candidates fear, and that most of the outcome is decided long before the candidate walks onto the floor.

This chapter tries to give you the examiner's view, so that you can prepare for it.

## The grading ladder

Every association has its own syllabus, and you must check yours. But the broad progression in Okinawan-line Gōjū-ryū is reasonably consistent, and Figure 12.1 shows a typical example.

![**Figure 12.1** A typical grading ladder in an Okinawan-line association. The kata shown at each grade are the main examination kata; previous kata may also be required. Syllabuses vary, so always check your own association's requirements.](figures/fig_ladder.png)

At the kyū grades, the emphasis is on learning the shape of each kata correctly and building a sound foundation of stances, techniques and basic bunkai. At the early dan grades, the emphasis shifts to quality: the depth of the stances, the rhythm, the breath, the understanding shown in bunkai. At the senior dan grades, examiners look for something harder to define: maturity, authority, an individual expression of the kata within the tradition, and often the ability to teach it.

## How examiners think

Examiners are human. They are usually volunteers who give up their weekends to sit at a table for hours. Most of them want you to pass. They know how nervous you are, because they have been nervous themselves. They are not looking for perfection; they are looking for evidence that you have reached the standard for the grade.

They are also, inevitably, influenced by first impressions. The way you walk onto the floor, the way you bow, the way you stand in yōi: all these tell an experienced examiner a great deal before you have performed a single technique. A candidate who walks on calmly, bows properly and stands still in a strong ready position has already made a good impression.

### What carries the most weight

Figure 12.2 shows an illustrative breakdown of where an examiner's attention goes during a kata. The exact proportions vary between examiners and associations, and no examiner works with literal percentages, but the general shape is reliable.

![**Figure 12.2** An illustrative breakdown of where examiners' attention typically goes in a kata assessment. Stances and kime together usually account for more than a third.](figures/fig_scoring.png)

**Stances** carry the most weight because they are the most visible and the most revealing. A weak stance cannot be disguised.

**Kime and focus** come next. Techniques that finish with a clear moment of focus look like karate; techniques that drift look like dance.

**Bunkai** carries significant weight at most grades, because it shows whether you understand what you are doing.

**Rhythm and speed** show whether you have absorbed the character of the kata.

**Breathing**, **embusen accuracy** and **chakugan** (the eyes) make up the rest.

### The silent criteria

Some things are rarely written on the marking sheet but always influence the result:

- **Composure**: how you handle nerves, mistakes and fatigue.
- **Attitude**: how you behave between performances, how you treat your partner in bunkai, how you accept instructions.
- **Consistency**: whether your second kata is as good as your first, and your last technique as good as your first.
- **Effort**: whether you appear to be giving everything, especially at the end of a long grading.

## Common faults, across all kata

Each kata chapter lists its own particular faults. Here are the faults I see most often across all kata, at all grades, roughly in order of frequency.

1. **Rushing.** Nerves speed people up. A kata performed too fast loses its rhythm, its stances and its kime. Practise at your grading pace, and in the grading, deliberately slow down.
2. **Weak stances.** Usually too high, too short, with knees collapsing or heels lifting.
3. **No hikite.** The pulling hand drifts instead of snapping back to the hip.
4. **Eyes on the floor.** Looking down at the feet, especially on turns.
5. **Held breath.** Leading to a rigid body and exhaustion.
6. **Techniques without hips.** Arms flailing without the body behind them.
7. **No contrast.** Every technique at the same speed and tension.
8. **Weak kiai.** A shout from the throat rather than a contraction from the abdomen.
9. **Wrong embusen.** Angles drifting, steps too long or short, finishing in the wrong place.
10. **No zanshin.** Relaxing the moment the last technique is done.

Each of these is fixable. Take the list, ask a training partner or your instructor to watch your kata, and have them tick off every fault they see. Then work on the top two for a month.

## Three months to a grading: a preparation plan

This plan assumes you know your grading kata and have about three months to prepare. Adapt it to your schedule.

### Month one: foundations

- **Stances**: the daily routine from Chapter 2, every day.
- **Sanchin**: three times every session.
- **Grading kata**: slow practice, section by section, using the problems and solutions section of its chapter.
- **Previous kata**: once each per week.
- **Fitness**: two sessions a week of general conditioning.

### Month two: quality

- **Grading kata**: rhythm and breath. Use the rhythm notes in the chapter. Perform the whole kata at least five times per session.
- **Bunkai**: twice a week with a partner, building from slow and cooperative to faster and more realistic.
- **Video**: record yourself once a week and compare with the grading notes. Be honest.
- **Kihon**: the basic techniques of your syllabus, three times a week.
- **Fitness**: continue.

### Month three: performance

- **Mock gradings**: at least three, in front of your instructor or senior students, wearing your gi, in the dojo, with a formal start.
- **Perform under fatigue**: practise your kata at the end of a hard session.
- **Taper**: reduce volume in the final week. Do not learn anything new. Rest the day before.
- **Logistics**: check the time, place, fees, gi, belt, licence and anything else your association requires.

## Nerves

Nearly everyone is nervous at a grading. A little nervousness is helpful; it sharpens attention. Too much makes you rush, forget and tense up. Here is what helps.

**Preparation.** Most nerves come from uncertainty. The more thoroughly you have prepared, especially with mock gradings, the less uncertain you will be.

**Breathing.** Before you walk onto the floor, take five slow breaths: in through the nose for four counts, out through the mouth for six. In yōi, take one more. Long out-breaths calm the nervous system.

**Routine.** Have a fixed routine for the moments before your kata: how you stand, where you look, what you think. Practise it at every mock grading.

**Focus on process.** Do not think about passing. Think about your first stance, your first breath, your first turn.

**Accept the nerves.** Trying not to be nervous usually makes things worse. Notice the nerves, accept them, and get on with the kata.

## When things go wrong

### Forgetting the kata

Stop. Keep your composure. Do not invent. Return to yōi, look at the panel, and ask quietly if you may begin again. Most panels will let you. Then perform the kata calmly from the beginning.

### Performing badly

Sometimes you get to the end and know it was not your best. Hold zanshin anyway. Bow properly. Walk off calmly. The next part of the grading is a new opportunity, and examiners notice candidates who recover.

### Failing

Not every grading goes the way you hope. If you fail or are deferred, it hurts. Allow yourself to be disappointed. Then ask your instructor, or the panel if your association allows it, for specific feedback. Write it down. Most deferred candidates pass at the next attempt, and many say afterwards that the extra time made them much better karateka.

Some of the best karateka I know failed a grading at some point. Very often the story is the same: rushed preparation, a little overconfidence and a moment of lost concentration in the middle of the kata. Almost all of them describe that failure as the most useful grading they ever took. It taught them that the kata does not care how much you want the belt.

## Teaching the kata

At the senior grades, you will be asked to teach. Many people find that teaching a kata deepens their understanding of it more than years of performing it. Some principles:

- **Teach in sections.** Use the sections in this book, or your own.
- **Teach the embusen first.** Students who know the floor pattern learn the techniques far faster.
- **Correct one thing at a time.** A student given ten corrections remembers none.
- **Use the problems and solutions.** Look for the common faults and correct them early.
- **Show the bunkai early.** Students who know what a movement is for learn it faster and perform it better.
- **Be patient.** Remember how long it took you.

## A grading day, hour by hour

Many candidates prepare their kata thoroughly and then lose marks to things that have nothing to do with karate: arriving late, warming up badly, eating at the wrong time, standing in a cold hall for an hour before their turn. Here is a practical guide to the day itself.

**The night before.** Pack your bag: clean gi, belt, licence or membership card, grading fee, water, a snack, a towel, any medication you need and a warm layer to wear while you wait. Do not train hard. A short, gentle run-through of your kata is fine. Go to bed at your usual time.

**The morning.** Eat a normal breakfast at your normal time. Do not try anything new. If the grading is in the afternoon, eat a light lunch at least two hours before you are due to perform.

**Arrival.** Aim to arrive at least forty-five minutes before the start. Find the changing rooms, the toilets and the place where candidates wait. Report to the organiser.

**Warming up.** Warm up thoroughly but not exhaustingly: joint mobility, light movement to raise the heart rate, a few stances, one slow run-through of each grading kata. If there is a long wait before your turn, keep warm with your extra layer and repeat a short warm-up ten minutes before you are called.

**Waiting.** Waiting is the hardest part for many people. Do not spend it watching every other candidate and comparing yourself. Breathe slowly. Walk through your kata in your head. Have a sip of water.

**Your turn.** Walk on calmly. Bow properly. Announce your kata clearly if your association does so. Take a breath in yōi. Begin.

**Between sections.** Stand still in a relaxed but attentive posture. Do not fidget, adjust your gi constantly or talk to the other candidates.

**After.** Thank your bunkai partner. Bow out properly. Change, eat and drink. Whatever the result, write down what went well and what did not while it is fresh.

## Questions examiners ask

Many associations include a few questions in their gradings, especially at the dan grades. These are the ones I hear most often. Prepare your own answers; examiners can always tell a memorised answer from an understood one.

- What does the name of your kata mean?
- Who created or brought this kata to Gōjū-ryū, as far as we know?
- What does *Gōjū* mean, and where does the name come from?
- Show me an application of this movement. What else could it be?
- Why does this kata begin with a step to the diagonal (or backwards, or forwards)?
- Where is the kiai, and why there?
- What is the difference between ibuki and nogare?
- What is zanshin?
- What is the most important thing you have learned from this kata?
- How would you teach this kata to a beginner?

The last two questions have no right answer. Examiners ask them to find out whether you have thought about your karate. A short, honest, thoughtful answer is always better than a long recitation.

## Problems and solutions: the grading as a whole

**Problem: "I always perform worse in gradings than in class."** This is nearly universal, and it is mostly a matter of experience. The solution is mock gradings: formal performances in front of an audience, in your gi, with a bow and an announcement, as often as possible in the month before. Each one makes the real thing more familiar.

**Problem: "I get tired halfway through the grading and my last kata is my worst."** Gradings are long and often involve a great deal of standing around followed by short bursts of intense effort. Build your fitness with interval training, and practise performing your kata at the end of hard sessions. Keep warm while you wait.

**Problem: "My bunkai partner was someone I had never trained with."** This is common at large gradings. Practise your bunkai with as many different partners as you can beforehand, including people of different sizes. At the grading, take a moment to agree the distance and the attack before you start.

**Problem: "I was deferred and I do not understand why."** Ask, politely and specifically. "Which parts of my performance did not meet the standard?" is a better question than "Why did I fail?". Write the answer down and build your next three months of training around it.

**Problem: "I passed, but I know my kata is not really at the standard."** That is an honest and admirable thing to recognise. The belt is a record of the standard you showed on the day, not a certificate of perfection. Keep working on the weaknesses you know about. Most senior karateka will tell you that every grading they passed was the beginning of the work on that grade, not the end.

**Problem: "I am older and worried about keeping up with younger candidates."** Examiners know the difference between a twenty-year-old and a sixty-year-old body, and good examiners assess each candidate against the standard appropriate to them. What matters is correct structure, sound understanding and full effort within your capacity. Many of the finest kata I have seen were performed by karateka over sixty.

## The examiner's chair

Eventually, many readers of this book will sit on a grading panel themselves. A few words for them.

**Mark what you see, not what you expect.** It is easy to be influenced by a candidate's reputation, their club, or how they performed last time. Watch the kata in front of you.

**Know the version.** Before you examine a kata, make sure you know the version taught by the candidate's association. Marking a candidate down for a legitimate variation is unfair.

**Look for the standard, not perfection.** The question is not "Is this perfect?" but "Has this candidate reached the standard for this grade?".

**Give useful feedback.** If your association allows feedback, make it specific and actionable. "Your shiko dachi was too high in the second section" is useful; "Needs more spirit" is not.

**Remember what it was like.** Every examiner was once a nervous candidate. A kind word at the end of a hard grading costs nothing and may be remembered for years.

## Self-test for the whole book

These questions cover the whole book. If you can answer them all, you have a solid grasp of its contents.

1. Who taught Miyagi Chōjun, and roughly when did Miyagi name his style?
2. From which text is the name Gōjū-ryū taken, and what does the line mean?
3. What are the heishugata, the fukyūgata and the kaishugata?
4. Name the four commonest faults in sanchin dachi.
5. What is the difference between ibuki and nogare?
6. In the diagrams in this book, which side of the performer is drawn in white?
7. What are the three levels of bunkai?
8. Which kata contains no kicks?
9. Which kata opens with three slow nukite in sanchin dachi?
10. Which kata opens with a chain of punches after three steps in sanchin dachi?
11. Which kata contains a full spin?
12. Which kata is performed mainly in neko ashi dachi and is known for its quietness?
13. Which kata is the longest, and what does its name mean?
14. What are the ten commonest faults across all kata?
15. What should you do if you forget your kata in a grading?

*Answers*: (1) Higaonna Kanryō; around 1930; (2) the *Bubishi*; "the way of inhaling and exhaling is hardness and softness"; (3) the closed-hand kata (Sanchin and Tenshō), the promotional kata (the Gekisai kata) and the open-hand kata (Saifa to Suparinpei); (4) front foot turned too far, rear heel lifting, stance too long, hips pushing back; (5) ibuki is the slow, deep, audible power breath; nogare the quieter breath of fast movement; (6) the left side; (7) basic, applied and free; (8) Seiyunchin; (9) Shisochin (Suparinpei opens similarly with double nukite); (10) Seisan; (11) Seipai; (12) Kururunfa; (13) Suparinpei, one hundred and eight; (14) rushing, weak stances, no hikite, eyes on the floor, held breath, techniques without hips, no contrast, weak kiai, wrong embusen, no zanshin; (15) stop, stay composed, return to yōi and ask to begin again.

## After the last grading

There comes a point when there are no more gradings to take, either because you have reached the highest grade your association awards, or because you simply stop wanting them. This is where the real practice begins.

Without a grading to aim at, many karateka drift. Their training becomes occasional, their kata stale. Others find a deeper motivation. They practise because the kata are interesting, because the practice is good for them, because they are curious about what they still do not understand. They find that every kata, even Gekisai, contains something new each year.

I would encourage you to aim for the second path. Here are some ideas for keeping kata alive over the decades:

- **Choose a kata for the year.** Make it your main study. Read about it, practise its bunkai, perform it in every session.
- **Go back to the beginning.** Spend a month on Gekisai Dai Ichi. You will be surprised what you find.
- **Study with other associations.** See how they perform the same kata. Learn from the differences.
- **Teach.** There is no better way to understand something.
- **Write.** Keep a training diary. Record what you notice.
- **Adapt to your body.** As you age, your kata will change. Embrace it. A seventy-year-old's Sanchin should not look like a twenty-year-old's, but it can be just as deep.

## A last word

Miyagi Chōjun is reported to have said that the purpose of karate is to cultivate oneself through training. Whatever his exact words, the meaning is clear enough. The kata are tools for that cultivation. They are not ends in themselves, and the belts are not ends in themselves. The point is the person who practises them.

I hope this book helps you on the road. I hope it saves you some of the mistakes I made, and that it sends you back to the dojo with new questions. Above all, I hope it helps you to enjoy these extraordinary old forms as much as I have.

Now put the book down and go and practise.

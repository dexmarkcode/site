# Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni

::: chapter-intro
The two Gekisai kata are the doorway into Gōjū-ryū. They were written to be simple, and they are, but simple is not the same as easy. Everything you will ever do in the senior kata, the turns, the stance changes, the combination of block and counter, the kiai, is introduced here in its plainest form. Learn these two kata well and every later kata becomes easier. Learn them carelessly and you will be correcting the same faults for years.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Gekisai* is usually translated as "attack and destroy", "attack and smash" or "to pulverise". *Dai ichi* means number one and *dai ni* number two. The name has a slightly martial, wartime flavour, which is not surprising when you consider when the kata were made.

As we saw in Chapter 1, in 1940 the authorities in Okinawa set up a committee to create simple kata that could be taught to schoolchildren and to the general public, to promote karate and to improve physical fitness. Miyagi Chōjun created Gekisai Dai Ichi as his contribution; Nagamine Shōshin created a companion kata, Fukyūgata Ichi. Miyagi later created Gekisai Dai Ni as a development of the first, introducing more of the open-hand and circular techniques that characterise the kaishu kata. Both kata are therefore *fukyūgata*, promotional kata, rather than classical forms brought from China.

This modern origin is a strength rather than a weakness. Miyagi was a highly experienced teacher who knew exactly which skills a beginner needed. The Gekisai kata are carefully designed to introduce those skills in a logical order, and to do so in a form that can be learned in a few weeks and practised in a small space. They are the karate equivalent of a well-written primer.

In most Okinawan-line associations, Gekisai Dai Ichi is the first kata a beginner learns, usually for the first or second kyū grading, and Gekisai Dai Ni follows at the next grade or two. In some associations, particularly in mainland Japan, a set of simpler *taikyoku* kata comes first. In Shōrin-ryū and related styles, a version of Gekisai Dai Ichi is practised under the name Fukyūgata Ni.

## Character of the kata

Both Gekisai kata are fast, direct and symmetrical. They are performed mostly at a brisk, even tempo, with clear stops at the end of each technique, and they contain none of the long, slow, tense movements of the kaishu kata. Breathing is natural and quick, with an out-breath on each technique.

The main differences between the two are these:

- **Gekisai Dai Ichi** uses closed fists throughout and relies mainly on zenkutsu dachi, sanchin dachi and shiko dachi. Its middle section, the combination of block, kick, elbow strike, back-fist, downward block and reverse punch, is one of the most useful combinations in the whole of karate.
- **Gekisai Dai Ni** keeps the opening of Dai Ichi but replaces the sanchin-dachi blocks of the middle section with open-hand hooking blocks (*kake uke*) in neko ashi dachi, and replaces the plain ending with mawashi uke. It is the first time most students meet these two signature Gōjū techniques inside a kata.

If Dai Ichi teaches you to be hard, Dai Ni gives you your first taste of being soft.

## Before you begin

Before learning Gekisai Dai Ichi you should be reasonably comfortable with:

- musubi dachi and heiko dachi, and the formal opening and closing;
- zenkutsu dachi, stepping forward and turning;
- sanchin dachi, at least in its basic form;
- shiko dachi, with the knees over the toes;
- jōdan age uke, chūdan uke and gedan barai;
- chūdan tsuki, jōdan tsuki and gyaku tsuki;
- mae geri (front kick) with a good chamber;
- mae empi uchi (forward elbow strike) and uraken uchi (back-fist).

For Gekisai Dai Ni you will also need neko ashi dachi, kake uke and at least a basic mawashi uke. Chapter 2 describes all of these.

## The embusen of Gekisai Dai Ichi

The floor pattern of Gekisai Dai Ichi is a T. You begin at the centre of the crossbar, work first to the left (west), then to the right (east), then forward up the stem of the T (north), turn and come back down it (south), and finally turn to face the front again and finish where you began.

{{MAP:gekisai1}}

Before you learn any techniques, walk this pattern. Stand at station A facing north. Look left. Step left into a long stance facing west. Step forward again with the right foot. Return to the centre. Look right, step right into a long stance facing east, step forward with the left foot, return to the centre. Step forward up the stem in three steps, turn round, come back down two steps, turn to face the front, step back to the start. Do this ten times, calling out the directions aloud, before you add a single hand technique.

## The sequence of Gekisai Dai Ichi, count by count

I divide Gekisai Dai Ichi into five sections for practice. The diagram sequence and count sheet follow the walkthrough.

### Opening: rei and yōi

Stand in musubi dachi, heels together and toes apart, hands open at your sides. Bow. Bring the hands up in front of the groin, open, left over right, in *kaishu yōi*. Breathe in slowly through the nose. Then step the left foot out to heiko dachi, closing the hands into fists in front of the thighs (count 1). This is the ready position from which the kata begins.

### Section one: left and right (counts 2 to 7)

**Count 2.** Turn your head to look west. Step the left foot out to the west into left zenkutsu dachi, and as the foot lands, perform left jōdan age uke. The right fist pulls to the right hip. The head leads; the step follows; the block arrives with the foot.

**Count 3.** Step forward with the right foot into right zenkutsu dachi and punch right jōdan tsuki to the face, pulling the left fist to the hip. This is your first counter-attack. It should be fast and committed, with a sharp hikite.

**Count 4.** Draw the right foot back and turn to face the front in heiko dachi, fists in front of the thighs. This is a pause, a reset. Breathe.

**Counts 5 to 7.** Repeat counts 2 to 4 to the east, on the other side: look right, right zenkutsu dachi with right age uke; step forward into left zenkutsu dachi with left jōdan tsuki; return to heiko dachi facing front.

These first seven counts introduce the most basic pattern in karate: receive, counter, recover. Notice also the habit of looking before moving. Examiners watch the eyes on the first turn of the kata more closely than almost anything else.

### Section two: forward (counts 8 to 14)

**Count 8.** Step forward with the left foot into left zenkutsu dachi facing north and perform left jōdan age uke.

**Count 9.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and perform right chūdan uke. The change from the long zenkutsu dachi to the short, rooted sanchin dachi is deliberate: you are closing the distance and settling the body.

**Count 10.** Keeping the guard, kick right mae geri. Chamber the knee high, kick, and recover the knee before stepping down. Do not let the arms drop as you kick.

**Count 11.** Step down forward into right zenkutsu dachi and drive right mae empi uchi, a forward elbow strike, as you land. The landing and the elbow arrive together. Students often kick and then land and then strike; aim for kick, then land-and-strike in one action.

**Count 12.** Without moving the feet forward, turn the hips to the left into shiko dachi (you now face west, with the right side towards the north) and snap right uraken uchi, a back-fist, out to the north. The fist snaps out and returns.

**Count 13.** In the same shiko dachi, perform right gedan barai to the north.

**Count 14.** Turn the hips back into right zenkutsu dachi facing north and punch left gyaku tsuki, a reverse punch, with a kiai.

These seven counts are the heart of the kata. The combination of elbow, back-fist, downward block and reverse punch, linked by the twist of the hips from zenkutsu dachi to shiko dachi and back, teaches you that the hips are the engine of every technique.

### Section three: back (counts 15 to 20)

**Count 15.** Turn round to the left, pivoting on the balls of the feet, into left sanchin dachi facing south, and perform left chūdan uke.

**Count 16.** Kick left mae geri.

**Count 17.** Land in left zenkutsu dachi with left mae empi uchi.

**Count 18.** Turn the hips to the right into shiko dachi facing east, snapping left uraken uchi to the south.

**Count 19.** Left gedan barai in shiko dachi.

**Count 20.** Turn back into left zenkutsu dachi facing south and punch right gyaku tsuki.

This section mirrors section two in the opposite direction. Many associations place a second kiai here; I have marked only one kiai in my count sheet, at count 14, but you should follow your own dojo's practice.

### Section four: turning to the front (counts 21 to 25)

**Count 21.** Turn to face north again, into left zenkutsu dachi, and perform left chūdan uke.

**Count 22.** Punch right gyaku tsuki.

**Count 23.** Step back with the left foot into right zenkutsu dachi and perform right chūdan uke.

**Count 24.** Punch left gyaku tsuki.

**Count 25.** Draw back to heiko dachi at the starting point, crossing the open hands low in front of the body, slowly.

### Closing: naore

**Count 26.** Bring the feet together into musubi dachi with the hands open in front of the groin. Hold zanshin for a moment, then lower the hands and bow.

## The sequence of Gekisai Dai Ni, count by count

### Opening and section one (counts 1 to 7)

The opening and the first seven counts are exactly the same as in Gekisai Dai Ichi: heiko dachi; age uke and jōdan tsuki to the west; return; age uke and jōdan tsuki to the east; return. Many students find it reassuring that the second kata begins with something they already know. Others find it a trap, because it is very easy to drift into Dai Ichi later in the kata out of habit. Concentrate.

### Section two: forward (counts 8 to 14)

**Count 8.** Step forward into left zenkutsu dachi facing north with left jōdan age uke, as in Dai Ichi.

**Count 9.** Here is the first difference. Step forward with the right foot into right neko ashi dachi and perform right kake uke, an open-hand hooking block. The hand circles out and forward so that the back of the wrist meets an imaginary punch and hooks around it. The left hand stays at the hip. The weight is on the back leg.

**Count 10.** From neko ashi dachi, the front leg is already light, so the right mae geri follows naturally. Keep the hooking hand where it is: in the application, it is holding the opponent's arm.

**Counts 11 to 14.** Land in right zenkutsu dachi with right mae empi uchi, turn into shiko dachi with right uraken uchi and right gedan barai, then turn back into zenkutsu dachi with left gyaku tsuki and kiai, exactly as in Dai Ichi.

### Section three: back (counts 15 to 20)

**Count 15.** Turn to face south into left neko ashi dachi with left kake uke.

**Counts 16 to 20.** Left mae geri; land in left zenkutsu dachi with left empi; shiko dachi with left uraken and left gedan barai; left zenkutsu dachi with right gyaku tsuki.

### Section four: mawashi uke (counts 21 to 23)

**Count 21.** Turn to face north into left neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke, the circular two-handed block, finishing with the hands in tora guchi, one above the other in front of the chest.

**Count 22.** Push both palms forward in a double palm-heel strike, breathing out strongly.

**Count 23.** Step back into right neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke again, on the other side, finishing with the double push.

### Closing

**Count 24.** Draw the front foot back into musubi dachi, hands open, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Both Gekisai kata should be performed at a lively, even pace, with a crisp stop at the end of each count and a short pause at each return to heiko dachi. The breathing is natural: breathe out on each technique, in on each transition. There is no ibuki in either kata, with the partial exception of the double palm push in Gekisai Dai Ni, which many teachers like to see performed with a strong, audible out-breath.

The most important rhythm points are these:

- **Counts 2 and 3** (and their mirrors) should be performed as a block and counter, with a slight pause between them, not as one blurred movement.
- **Counts 10 and 11** should be linked: kick, and land into the elbow strike, with no pause on landing.
- **Counts 11 to 14** should flow as a combination. The hips drive each technique: forward for the elbow, sideways for the back-fist and downward block, forward again for the reverse punch.
- **Count 25** in Dai Ichi, and the mawashi uke in Dai Ni, should be noticeably slower than the rest of the kata, signalling that the kata is coming to its close.

A useful test: have someone time your Gekisai Dai Ichi. A good performance, with proper pauses, usually takes between fifty and seventy seconds. If yours takes thirty, you are rushing. If it takes a minute and a half, you are hesitating.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

Here are two applications from each kata, chosen because they illustrate principles you will use throughout the rest of the system.

### Gekisai Dai Ichi: the opening (counts 2 and 3)

Your partner steps in with a right punch to your face. You step forward and slightly outside the punch into zenkutsu dachi, meeting the punching arm with age uke. Do not simply hold the arm up; drive the block upwards and forwards into the attacker's arm so that it lifts their elbow and opens their ribs. Then step in and counter with jōdan tsuki.

At the applied level, the age uke can be seen as a forearm strike to the side of the neck or the underside of the jaw, or as a way of lifting an arm that has grabbed your collar. Try it slowly with a partner and see how much the upward drive unbalances them.

![**Figure 4.1** Gekisai Dai Ichi, counts 2 and 3: the rising block meets the punch and drives it upwards, opening the attacker for the counter.](figures/bk_gekisai1_1.png)

### Gekisai Dai Ichi: the elbow combination (counts 10 to 14)

Your partner faces you at kicking distance. Kick to the groin or thigh to close the distance, and land close enough to drive the forward elbow into the face or chest. As your partner recoils, turn the hips into shiko dachi and snap the back-fist to the face, then sweep down with gedan barai, which at the applied level is often a hammer-fist to the groin or a downward strike to the forearm of a grabbing hand. Turn back and finish with the reverse punch.

What this combination teaches above all is that close range is not a problem but an opportunity. The elbow, back-fist and hammer-fist are all short-range weapons. Most beginners stand too far away; this sequence teaches them to come in.

![**Figure 4.2** Gekisai Dai Ichi, count 11: landing from the kick directly into the forward elbow strike, at very close range.](figures/bk_gekisai1_2.png)

### Gekisai Dai Ni: the hooking block (counts 9 and 10)

Your partner punches to your body. Shift back into neko ashi dachi and meet the punch with kake uke, hooking around the forearm and drawing it slightly towards you and to the side. Because your weight is on the back leg, your front leg is free to kick immediately, while your hooking hand keeps the attacker's arm under control so that they cannot strike again.

The lesson here is the first appearance of an idea that runs through all the kaishu kata: *a block that becomes a grip*. In Gekisai Dai Ichi you block and then strike. In Gekisai Dai Ni you block, keep hold, and strike while holding.

![**Figure 4.3** Gekisai Dai Ni, count 9: shifting back into neko ashi dachi, the open hand hooks the punching arm and controls it while the front leg prepares to kick.](figures/bk_gekisai2_1.png)

### Gekisai Dai Ni: mawashi uke (counts 21 and 22)

Your partner attacks with a punch, then a second punch, or with both hands to your chest. The circular action of mawashi uke deflects both attacks, the upper hand clearing high and the lower hand clearing low. As the hands come together in tora guchi, they are in perfect position to push the attacker away with both palms, driving from the back leg of neko ashi dachi.

At the applied level, mawashi uke can be a way of trapping and turning an arm, of releasing a two-handed grab, or of striking with the palms to the face and body at the same time.

![**Figure 4.4** Gekisai Dai Ni, counts 21 and 22: mawashi uke clears both attacking hands and finishes in tora guchi, ready for the double palm push.](figures/bk_gekisai2_2.png)

## Problems and solutions

These are the faults I see most often in the Gekisai kata, with their causes and their cures.

**Problem: stepping before looking on the first turn.** *Cause*: the student is concentrating on the feet. *Solution*: practise count 2 alone, ten times, saying "look" aloud before you move. The head should turn crisply and stop before the foot leaves the floor.

**Problem: age uke too low or too close to the head.** *Cause*: the arm stops when it reaches the face rather than continuing upward and forward. *Solution*: the forearm should finish about a fist's height above and a fist's distance in front of the forehead. Have a partner place a hand on your head; your forearm should be clearly above and ahead of it.

**Problem: zenkutsu dachi too narrow.** *Cause*: carrying sanchin-dachi habits into zenkutsu dachi. *Solution*: practise zenkutsu dachi along two parallel lines of tape on the floor, hip width apart, placing one foot on each.

**Problem: arms drop during the kick.** *Cause*: the body treats the kick as a separate action. *Solution*: practise counts 9 and 10 with a soft ball or folded towel held between the blocking forearm and the body. If it falls, the guard has dropped.

**Problem: landing heavily from the kick and then striking.** *Cause*: the kick is thrown without control of the recovery. *Solution*: practise kicking and holding the chambered knee for a count of two, then stepping down with the elbow. Gradually shorten the pause until the land and the strike become one.

**Problem: the back-fist and downward block are performed with the arm only.** *Cause*: the hips are not turning into shiko dachi. *Solution*: practise the turn from zenkutsu dachi to shiko dachi and back with the hands on the hips, feeling the hips drive. Only then add the arms.

**Problem: shiko dachi too high or with collapsing knees.** *Cause*: weak legs and tight hips. *Solution*: see Chapter 2. Do not sacrifice the knees for depth.

**Problem: mixing Dai Ichi and Dai Ni.** *Cause*: the kata are deliberately similar. *Solution*: practise each kata from count 8 onwards only, without the shared opening, until the differences are firmly in place.

**Problem: mawashi uke performed as a wave rather than a circle.** *Cause*: the hands are moving from the shoulders rather than from the elbows and wrists. *Solution*: practise mawashi uke very slowly in front of a mirror, drawing two complete circles with the hands, before speeding up.

## Variations between associations

The Gekisai kata are among the most standardised in Gōjū-ryū, but there are still differences.

- In some lineages, the first movement steps forward with the right foot towards the west (or towards the north-west) rather than stepping the left foot out to the side. The effect is to turn into the first block rather than step sideways into it.
- Some associations perform the counters in section one as chūdan rather than jōdan punches.
- The return to heiko dachi between sides is performed in some versions by bringing the stepping foot back and in others by turning in place.
- Some versions perform section four with age uke rather than chūdan uke, or with a different ending, such as a double block or a sequence in sanchin dachi.
- The kiai points vary. Many associations kiai at both reverse punches in sections two and three.
- In Gōjū-kai and some Japanese lineages, Gekisai Dai San (number three) also exists. It is not part of the Okinawan syllabus and is not covered here.

As always, follow your own teacher's version.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni**

*Typical grade*: Gekisai Dai Ichi for the first one or two kyū gradings; Gekisai Dai Ni for the next one or two. Both are often asked for again at later gradings as a test of fundamentals.

*What the examiners look for*:

- The head turning crisply before the first step to each side.
- Clear, correct stances: long zenkutsu dachi, rooted sanchin dachi, low shiko dachi with knees out.
- Age uke above and in front of the head, not on it.
- A sharp hikite on every punch.
- A good mae geri chamber with the guard held.
- The landing and elbow strike arriving together.
- The hips driving the turn into shiko dachi and back.
- A strong, short kiai from the abdomen.
- In Dai Ni, a genuinely open, hooking kake uke and a smooth, circular mawashi uke.
- Finishing on the starting spot.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: rushing; weak or narrow stances; arms dropping during kicks; techniques performed with the arms only; confusing Dai Ichi and Dai Ni.
:::

## A four-week training plan

This plan assumes you have been shown the kata in class and want to consolidate it. Train four or five times a week, for twenty to thirty minutes, in addition to your normal classes.

**Week one: shape.** Walk the embusen ten times each session without techniques. Then add stances only. Finish each session with three slow performances using the diagrams.

**Week two: structure.** Practise each section five times slowly, stopping on every count to check the position against the diagram. Spend five minutes each session on the stance routine from Chapter 2.

**Week three: transitions and rhythm.** Practise the transitions in isolation: zenkutsu dachi to sanchin dachi; kick to landing; zenkutsu dachi to shiko dachi and back. Then perform the whole kata at normal speed, five times, concentrating on the rhythm points above.

**Week four: performance.** Perform the kata three times in front of someone each session. Have them use the grading notes as a checklist. Practise the bunkai with a partner at least twice during the week.

### Key points

- Gekisai Dai Ichi and Dai Ni were created by Miyagi Chōjun from 1940 onwards as promotional kata for schools. They are short, symmetrical and brilliantly designed for beginners.
- The embusen is a T: left, right, forward, back, front.
- The heart of both kata is the combination of kick, elbow, back-fist, downward block and reverse punch, driven by the hips.
- Dai Ni introduces kake uke in neko ashi dachi and mawashi uke: your first taste of the soft side of Gōjū-ryū.
- Look before you turn. Keep the guard during kicks. Land into the strike. Let the hips do the work.

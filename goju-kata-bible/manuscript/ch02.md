# The Body of Gōjū: Stances, Breathing and the Engine of Power

::: chapter-intro
Before a single kata can be learned well, the body has to learn how to stand, how to breathe and how to move from one position to the next without losing its structure. This chapter sets out the stances, hand techniques and breathing methods used in every kata in the book, explains what each one is for, and gives you a way to diagnose and correct the faults that hold most students back.
:::

If you take only one chapter of this book seriously, let it be this one. I have watched hundreds of gradings, and I can tell you that most failed or marginal performances do not fail because the candidate forgot the kata. They fail because the stances are weak, the breathing is wrong or the transitions between positions leak power. The kata is simply the place where those weaknesses become visible.

The good news is that these are all fixable problems. They respond to patient, intelligent practice. The bad news is that there is no shortcut. You cannot read your way to a strong sanchin dachi; you have to stand in it, a great deal, while someone who knows what they are looking at tells you what is wrong.

## Why stances matter so much in Gōjū-ryū

Every technique you perform is only as strong as the platform it is launched from. A punch thrown from an unstable stance is a punch that pushes you backwards as much as it pushes the target. A block made from a stance with no root is a block that can be knocked aside. In Gōjū-ryū, which fights at close range and expects to be grabbed, pushed and pulled, the stance is not just a platform for techniques. It is itself a technique: the means by which you stay upright when someone is trying to knock you down.

Gōjū stances tend to be shorter and higher than those of many other styles. This is not laziness. A shorter stance lets you turn quickly in any direction, keeps your weight under control and allows you to move into and out of grappling range. A longer, lower stance, such as zenkutsu dachi or shiko dachi, appears in Gōjū kata at specific moments where its particular strengths are needed: driving forward, dropping the body weight, generating power from the hips.

Figures 2.1 and 2.3 show the eight stances used in this book, each drawn from the side or front and from above as footprints. Study both views. The side view shows the line of the body and the bend of the knees; the footprints show the width, length and angle of the feet, which is where most stance faults begin.

## The stances, one by one

### Musubi dachi: the formal stance

*Musubi* means knot or tied. In musubi dachi the heels touch and the toes open to about forty-five degrees, so that the feet form a V. The knees are straight but not locked, the spine is tall and the weight is evenly distributed.

Every kata in this book begins and ends in musubi dachi. It is the stance of the bow, of attention and of readiness. In most Gōjū kata the hands are placed open in front of the groin, the left hand over the right, which is called *kaishu yōi* or open-hand ready.

**What it teaches.** Musubi dachi teaches you to stand still with dignity. That sounds trivial, but watch a nervous candidate at a grading and you will see shuffling feet, a drifting gaze and restless hands. Learning to stand perfectly still in musubi dachi, with a calm breath, is the first act of kata.

### Heiko dachi: the natural stance

*Heikō* means parallel. In heiko dachi the feet are about shoulder width apart, parallel, with the toes pointing straight ahead. The knees are soft. It is the stance from which many kata begin after the formal opening, and the stance you return to between sections of some kata, such as Gekisai.

**What it teaches.** Heiko dachi is the stance of the ordinary person standing in the street. It is in some ways the most realistic stance in karate, because it is where you will be standing when something happens. It also teaches the important habit of keeping the knees unlocked. A locked knee is a vulnerable knee and a slow one.

### Sanchin dachi: the heart of the style

Sanchin dachi is the stance of the kata Sanchin and the foundation of Gōjū-ryū. It deserves more attention than any other stance in this book.

The feet are roughly shoulder width apart. One foot is advanced so that the heel of the front foot is level with, or slightly ahead of, the toes of the rear foot, a distance of roughly one foot-length. The front foot turns inwards by about twenty to thirty degrees; the rear foot points forward or very slightly inwards. The knees bend and press inwards and slightly forwards, as if gripping something between them, but without collapsing. The hips tilt so that the tailbone tucks under. The buttocks are tight. The abdomen is firm. The spine is upright, the shoulders down, the chin slightly lowered.

Figure 2.2 shows the dimensions and the most common faults.

![**Figure 2.2** Sanchin dachi in detail. The front heel sits roughly a foot-length ahead of the rear toes, the feet are shoulder width apart and the front foot turns in. Four common faults are listed on the right.](figures/fig_sanchin_detail.png)

Sanchin dachi feels strange at first, and often painful. The inward rotation of the knees asks the muscles of the inner thigh, the buttocks and the hips to work in a way they rarely do in daily life. Do not force it. Over months, the stance will become more comfortable, and you will start to feel what it is for: a structure in which force applied to you from almost any direction is directed down into the ground.

**What it teaches.** Sanchin dachi teaches *root*: the ability to stay connected to the ground under pressure. It teaches you to organise the whole body as a single unit, from the soles of the feet to the crown of the head. It teaches the tucked pelvis and engaged abdomen that are the source of power in every Gōjū technique. And because it is practised with dynamic tension and deep breathing, it teaches the relationship between breath and body that gives the style its name.

**How it moves.** In sanchin dachi you step forward in a crescent: the rear foot draws in towards the front foot and then curves out and forward to become the new front foot. The knees stay bent and the height stays level throughout. The crescent step protects the groin and keeps the stance closed. When students first learn it, they tend to bob up and down; a good test is to have a partner hold a hand just above your head as you step.

### Zenkutsu dachi: the forward stance

Zenkutsu dachi is the long forward stance familiar from most styles of karate. In Gōjū-ryū it is usually somewhat shorter than in Shōtōkan. The front knee bends so that it is roughly above the toes of the front foot; the rear leg is almost straight, with the rear foot turned out by around twenty-five degrees and the heel on the ground. The feet are about shoulder width apart, so that the stance has width as well as length. Most of the weight, perhaps sixty or seventy per cent, sits over the front leg.

**What it teaches.** Zenkutsu dachi is the stance for driving forward. In the kata in this book it appears in moments of commitment: the elbow strike after the kick in Gekisai, the reverse punch that ends a section, the hammer fist after a kick in Saifa.

**A common Gōjū problem.** Students who have spent a long time in sanchin dachi sometimes carry the inward-turned front foot and squeezed knees into zenkutsu dachi. The result is a narrow, unstable stance, like walking along a tightrope. Keep the feet a hip's width or more apart.

### Shiko dachi: the sumo stance

*Shiko* is the stamping action of sumo wrestlers, and shiko dachi is a wide, low stance with the feet roughly twice shoulder width apart, the toes turned out at about forty-five degrees and the knees bent and pushed outwards so that they track over the toes. The hips sink, the spine stays vertical and the tailbone tucks under, as in sanchin dachi.

![**Figure 2.3** The second four stances: shiko dachi, neko ashi dachi, sagi ashi dachi and kōsa dachi.](figures/fig_stances_2.png)

**What it teaches.** Shiko dachi drops the body weight. It is the stance for absorbing a heavy push, for driving an opponent down, for throwing and for delivering power sideways. It appears throughout the kaishu kata and dominates Seiyunchin, which is performed almost entirely in it.

**What goes wrong.** The commonest fault is allowing the knees to fall inwards, so that the stress of the stance lands on the inside of the knee joint. Always check that the knee is above the toes, or slightly outside them, never inside. The second commonest fault is leaning forward, so that the bottom pokes out behind. Imagine your back against a wall.

### Neko ashi dachi: the cat stance

*Neko ashi* means cat foot. In neko ashi dachi almost all the weight, perhaps eighty or ninety per cent, rests on the bent rear leg. The front foot rests lightly on the ball of the foot with the heel raised, about one foot-length in front of the rear foot. The rear foot turns out at forty to forty-five degrees. The front knee turns slightly inwards to protect the groin.

**What it teaches.** Neko ashi dachi frees the front leg for a quick kick and allows you to withdraw from an attack while staying in range to counter. In Gōjū kata it is associated above all with *mawashi uke*, the circular two-handed block that closes most of the kaishu kata, and it dominates the opening of Kururunfa.

**What goes wrong.** Students often place too much weight on the front foot, so that it cannot lift for a kick, or sink so low on the rear leg that they cannot move. Test yourself: can you lift the front foot without shifting your body? If not, your weight is too far forward.

### Sagi ashi dachi: the heron stance

*Sagi* is a heron. Sagi ashi dachi is a one-legged stance in which the supporting leg is slightly bent and the other foot is drawn up, usually so that the instep or sole rests against the side or back of the supporting knee, or so that the knee is raised high in front as a guard or in preparation for a kick.

**What it teaches.** Balance, obviously, but also the ability to lift a leg out of the way of a low sweep or kick, to raise the knee as a shield and to chamber a kick from a position of stillness. You will meet it in Saifa, Kururunfa and Suparinpei.

### Kōsa dachi: the crossed stance

*Kōsa* means crossed. In kōsa dachi the legs cross, with one foot flat and the other behind it on the ball of the foot, the rear knee tucked in behind the front knee. The body is low and compact.

**What it teaches.** Kōsa dachi appears at moments where the body drops suddenly, often to strike downwards or to follow a falling opponent. It is a transitional stance more than a fighting stance. You will meet it in Seiyunchin, Seipai and Suparinpei.

## Moving between stances

Kata are not a series of stances; they are a series of movements between stances. Most of the power in a Gōjū technique is generated in the transition, not in the final position. Three principles will help you move well.

**Keep the height level.** Unless the kata specifically asks you to rise or drop, your head should travel in a straight horizontal line when you step. Bobbing up and down wastes energy and announces your movement.

**Move from the centre.** The movement begins in the hips and lower abdomen, the area called *tanden* or *hara*, and the feet and hands follow. A student who moves the feet first and drags the body after them will always look disconnected.

**Arrive together.** The stance, the technique and the breath should all finish at the same instant. This is the essence of *kime*, focus. If the hand arrives before the foot is planted, the technique has no root. If the foot is planted before the hand arrives, the technique has no drive.

## Turning

Gōjū kata turn a great deal, through forty-five, ninety, one hundred and eighty and, in Seipai, three hundred and sixty degrees. Turns are where most students lose their balance, their direction and their place in the kata.

The basic rule is that you turn on the balls of the feet, never on the heels, and that the head turns first. Look in the new direction, then turn. This is called *chakugan*, placing the eyes, and it is not mere theatre. In a real encounter you turn because you have seen something; the eyes lead the body.

For a one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turn in sanchin dachi, the usual method is to move the rear foot across behind the front foot and then pivot on both feet, so that you end up facing the other way with the opposite foot forward. For a ninety-degree turn into shiko dachi, you usually pivot on one foot and swing the other out to the side. Each kata chapter describes its specific turns, and the diagrams show the direction you should face at the end of each one.

## The levels of attack

Before we look at the hand techniques, we need three words that you will meet constantly. Karate divides the body into three levels, as shown in Figure 2.4.

![**Figure 2.4** The three target levels. Jōdan covers the head and neck, chūdan the chest and abdomen down to the belt, gedan everything below the belt.](figures/fig_levels.png)

*Jōdan* is the upper level, the head and neck. *Chūdan* is the middle level, from the collarbones to the belt. *Gedan* is the lower level, below the belt. A *jōdan age uke* is a rising block that protects the head; a *chūdan tsuki* is a punch to the body; a *gedan barai* is a sweeping block that clears the lower level.

## The hand techniques

The hand techniques in Gōjū kata fall into three broad families: blocks (*uke*), strikes with the fist or hand (*tsuki* and *uchi*), and grips, pulls and presses that do not have tidy names but are everywhere in the kaishu kata. Figures 2.5 and 2.7 show the most important of them as they appear in sanchin dachi. In each drawing, the working arm is the white arm nearest to you.

![**Figure 2.5** Six essential hand techniques: jōdan age uke, chūdan uke, gedan barai, kake uke, chūdan tsuki and uraken uchi.](figures/fig_tech_1.png)

### Uke: receiving techniques

The Japanese word *uke* is usually translated as block, but receive is closer to the meaning. A Gōjū-ryū uke rarely meets force head-on. It receives the attack, redirects it and very often takes hold of it.

**Jōdan age uke**, the rising block, lifts the forearm above and slightly in front of the forehead, turning it so that the little-finger edge faces upwards and outwards. It protects the head from a downward or straight strike.

**Chūdan uke**, the middle-level block, is the characteristic block of Sanchin. The forearm is vertical, the fist at about shoulder height, the elbow about a fist's width from the body, and the arm moves from the centre line outwards. In other styles you may know it as *uchi uke* or *yoko uke*; naming conventions differ even within Gōjū-ryū.

**Gedan barai**, the downward sweep, clears the lower level with the little-finger edge of the forearm. The fist finishes just above the front knee and a little outside it.

**Kake uke**, the hooking block, is made with an open hand. The wrist or the back of the hand meets the attacking arm and then hooks around it, so that the defence becomes a grip. It is one of the most important techniques in the kaishu kata.

**Hiki uke**, the pulling block, is similar but finishes with the hand drawing the attacker's arm in towards you.

**Mawashi uke**, the circular or roundhouse block, is so important that it has a figure of its own (Figure 2.6). Both hands circle in opposite directions, the upper hand hooking and the lower hand pressing, until they finish one above the other in front of the chest, palms forward, like the jaws of a tiger. This finishing shape is called *tora guchi*, tiger's mouth, and from it the hands usually push out together in a double palm strike. Almost every kaishu kata ends with mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

![**Figure 2.6** Mawashi uke in four phases. Learn it slowly. The circle must be a genuine circle, not a wave, and the final push should come from the hips and the breath, not from the shoulders.](figures/fig_mawashi.png)

### Tsuki and uchi: striking techniques

**Tsuki** is a thrusting strike, usually a punch. The basic Gōjū punch travels in a straight line from the side of the body to the target, turning over at the end so that the palm faces down. The opposite hand pulls back sharply to the side of the body in *hikite*, the pulling hand, which is as important as the punch itself. In bunkai, the hikite is very often pulling an opponent onto the punch.

*Gyaku tsuki* is a reverse punch, thrown with the hand opposite to the forward leg. *Jōdan tsuki* targets the head. *Ura tsuki* is a short upward punch with the palm facing up, used at very close range.

**Uchi** is a striking technique delivered in a swinging or snapping arc rather than a straight thrust. In this book you will meet:

- **Uraken uchi**, the back-fist strike, in which the back of the knuckles snaps out to the target and back.
- **Tettsui uchi**, the hammer-fist strike, using the little-finger edge of the clenched fist.
- **Empi uchi** or **hiji ate**, the elbow strike, in several directions: forward (*mae*), rising (*age*), sideways (*yoko*) and backwards.
- **Shōtei uchi**, the palm-heel strike, using the heel of the open hand.
- **Nukite**, the spear hand, thrusting with the fingertips. *Yonhon nukite* uses four fingers; *nihon nukite* uses two.

### The hands in between

Much of what happens in a Gōjū kata does not fit neatly into blocks and strikes. A hand that rises slowly, open, while the other drops to the hip. Two hands that come together at the chest and then separate. A fist placed in the opposite palm and pulled sharply across the body. These movements have names in some dojo and not in others, and they are usually best understood through bunkai, which we will look at in Chapter 3. For now, the important point is that they are not decoration. Each one does something.

## Breathing

We come now to the subject that gives the style its name. Gōjū-ryū uses breathing deliberately, audibly and in coordination with movement. There are two main methods, shown in Figure 2.8.

![**Figure 2.8** The two breathing patterns. Ibuki, the power breath, is a slow, full inhalation followed by a long, forceful exhalation. Nogare, the escape breath, is shorter, quieter and more frequent, and is used for fast sequences.](figures/fig_breathing.png)

### Ibuki: the power breath

*Ibuki* is the breathing of Sanchin. You breathe in through the nose, steadily and deeply, filling the lower abdomen first. Then you breathe out through the mouth, with the throat partly closed so that the breath is audible, while tightening the abdomen, the buttocks and the whole body. The out-breath is long and strong, and it finishes with a short, sharp contraction that empties the lungs.

Ibuki is not natural breathing. It is a training method, designed to strengthen the muscles of the trunk, to teach coordination between breath and tension and to prepare the body to absorb blows. It should be practised under supervision, because it raises blood pressure, and anyone with heart or blood-pressure problems should consult a doctor before doing it.

In the kaishu kata, ibuki appears in the slow, tense sections: the opening of Seiyunchin, the nukite of Shisochin and Suparinpei, the slow blocks of Seipai and Kururunfa. It is never used for the whole of a kaishu kata.

### Nogare: the escape breath

*Nogare* is a quieter, more natural breathing method, used for the faster sections of kata. You breathe in briefly through the nose and out through the mouth, often with a small sound, timing the out-breath to the moment of a technique. Several techniques may be performed on one breath.

The most common breathing fault I see in kata, at every level, is the held breath. The student breathes in before a fast sequence, holds it through four or five techniques, and then gasps. Holding the breath makes the body rigid and the mind tense, and it is exhausting. Train yourself to breathe out on techniques, even quietly, and to breathe in during transitions.

### The kiai

*Kiai* is the shout that appears at fixed points in each kata. It is an extreme form of the focused out-breath: a short, loud, explosive release that comes from the abdomen, not the throat. A good kiai is not a scream. It is a contraction of the whole trunk that happens to make a noise. Each kata chapter marks the kiai points, which vary between associations more than almost any other detail.

## Kime, chinkuchi and the moment of power

*Kime* is usually translated as focus. It describes the instant at the end of a technique when the body, the breath and the mind all come together. A technique without kime is a gesture; a technique with kime is a strike.

In Okinawan karate you will also hear the word *chinkuchi*, which refers to the use of the muscles, tendons and breath to create a sudden firmness in the body at the moment of impact. The two ideas overlap. Both describe the hard phase of the hard–soft rhythm. The important thing to understand is that kime lasts for a fraction of a second. If you hold the tension after the technique has landed, you are no longer focused; you are rigid. Strike, tense, release, move.

## Conditioning: the body that kata need

Gōjū-ryū has a long tradition of supplementary training, *hojo undō*, using traditional tools: the *chi ishi* (a weighted stick for the wrists and shoulders), the *nigiri game* (gripping jars for the hands and the stance), the *ishi sashi* (stone padlocks), the *makiwara* (striking post) and others. There is also partner conditioning, *kote kitae*, in which two students strike forearms, shins and bodies together to toughen them.

I mention these here because they are not optional extras. Many of the techniques in the kaishu kata, the grips, pulls, presses and short strikes, depend on strong hands, forearms and shoulders. A student whose only training is kata will find that their kata look weak however much they practise, because the body has not been built to perform them. Even ten minutes of hojo undō a few times a week will make a visible difference.

If you have no access to traditional equipment, a pair of light dumbbells, a grip trainer and a heavy bag will cover a surprising amount of the same ground.

## Practising stances on your own

Here is a simple daily routine for building stances, which needs no more space than a bedroom floor.

1. **Two minutes in heiko dachi.** Breathe slowly. Check that the knees are soft, the weight is in the middle of the feet and the shoulders are relaxed.
2. **Three minutes in sanchin dachi, each side.** Stand still. Check the four faults in Figure 2.2. Breathe in through the nose for four counts and out through the mouth for six.
3. **Two minutes in shiko dachi.** Sink as low as you can while keeping the knees over the toes and the spine vertical. If two minutes is too long, do four sets of thirty seconds.
4. **Ten crescent steps in sanchin dachi**, forward and back, with a partner or a mirror checking that your head stays level.
5. **One minute in neko ashi dachi, each side**, checking that you can lift the front foot without moving.

That is fifteen minutes. Done five days a week for three months, it will transform your kata.

## Problems and solutions: stances and breathing

**Problem: "My knees hurt in sanchin dachi."** Usually the knees are being twisted inwards from the feet, rather than the whole leg being rotated from the hip. Turn the front foot in a little less, and think of rotating the thigh rather than squeezing the knee. If the pain is sharp or persists, stop and see a physiotherapist.

**Problem: "My knees hurt in shiko dachi."** Almost always the knees are falling inside the line of the toes. Narrow the stance until you can keep the knees over the toes, and widen it gradually over months as your hips open.

**Problem: "I lose my balance on turns."** Check three things. Are you turning on the balls of the feet rather than the heels? Is your head turning first? Are you rising up during the turn? A turn should be made at the same height as the stance before and after it.

**Problem: "I run out of breath halfway through a kata."** You are probably holding your breath through fast sequences. Practise the kata slowly, breathing out on every technique, until the pattern is automatic. Then build up speed.

**Problem: "My techniques look weak even though I am trying hard."** Trying hard often means tensing all the time. Remember that kime is a moment, not a state. Relax during the movement, tense for an instant at the end, relax again. Practise single techniques against a pad or makiwara to feel the difference.

**Problem: "My stance looks fine standing still but collapses when I step."** Your stance is being built from the feet up rather than held from the centre. Practise crescent stepping very slowly, keeping the abdomen and buttocks engaged throughout, and have someone watch from the side.

**Problem: "I cannot get my hips low enough in shiko dachi."** Flexibility in the hips and groin takes time. Do not force it. Add some gentle hip-opening stretches to your warm-up and cool-down, and accept a slightly higher stance in the meantime. A high stance with good structure beats a low stance with collapsed knees every time.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Stances are not platforms for techniques; in Gōjū-ryū they are techniques in their own right, the means by which you stay rooted under pressure.
- Sanchin dachi is the heart of the style. Its four commonest faults are a front foot turned too far, a lifting rear heel, a stance that is too long and hips that push back.
- Keep the height level, move from the centre and make the stance, technique and breath arrive together.
- Ibuki is the slow power breath of Sanchin; nogare is the quieter breath of fast movement. The commonest breathing fault is holding the breath.
- Kime lasts an instant. Tense, release, move.
- Conditioning is part of kata training, not separate from it.
:::

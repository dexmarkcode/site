# Saifa

::: chapter-intro
Saifa is the first of the classical open-hand kata and, for many students, the first kata that feels genuinely Gōjū. It moves on the diagonals, it grips and tears and releases, it lifts a knee and kicks and drops a hammer fist, and it finishes with the mawashi uke that closes almost every kaishu kata. It is short enough to learn in a few weeks and deep enough to study for a lifetime.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Saifa* is usually written with characters meaning "smash and tear" or "destroy and defeat", and the kata lives up to its name. Its opening movements are about tearing free from a grip; its middle section about smashing down with the hammer fist.

Saifa is generally believed to have been taught by Miyagi Chōjun, and some accounts link it to material he studied during his own time in Fujian, though the details are uncertain. It is the first of the eight kaishu kata in most syllabuses, and in many Okinawan-line associations it is required for the middle kyū grades, around fifth or fourth kyū. Some associations teach it rather later.

Saifa is also found, with differences, in other styles descended from Naha-te, including Shitō-ryū and Isshin-ryū. If you train with practitioners of those styles you will recognise the outline of the kata, the diagonal opening and the hammer fist, but the details may surprise you.

## Character of the kata

Saifa is a kata of sudden changes of direction and of close-range, two-handed techniques. Where Gekisai moves in straight lines and uses one hand at a time, Saifa moves on the diagonals and uses both hands together. Where Gekisai blocks and then counters, Saifa grips, releases, strikes and controls in one continuous action.

Three ideas dominate the kata:

1. **The release.** The opening movement, repeated three times, is usually understood as a release from a wrist grab. The fist is placed in the opposite palm and the whole body moves to pull the trapped hand free.
2. **The knee and the hammer fist.** The middle section lifts the knee in sagi ashi dachi while the forearms rise and part, kicks, and then smashes down with a hammer fist. It is done to the front and then to the back.
3. **The hook and the strike.** The fourth section, to the left and right, hooks an arm with an open hand and strikes over the top with a hammer fist.

The tempo is brisk and varied. There are no long ibuki breaths, but the opening releases are often performed with a deliberate, slower pull followed by a fast strike, and the mawashi uke at the end is slow and strong.

## Before you begin

You should be comfortable with everything in Gekisai Dai Ni, and in particular with:

- shiko dachi on the diagonal, turning between forty-five-degree angles;
- sagi ashi dachi, standing on one leg with the other knee raised;
- mae geri from sagi ashi dachi;
- uraken uchi and tettsui uchi (hammer-fist strike);
- kake uke;
- mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

The diagonal turns are the main new skill. If you have not practised turning forty-five degrees accurately, spend a week doing so before you start Saifa. Stand in the middle of the room and, without any techniques, step out to the north-east corner, then back, then to the north-west, then back, checking each time that you face the corner squarely.

## The embusen

Saifa uses an X across the front of the floor for its opening, a straight line forward and back for its middle section, and a line to the left and right for its fourth section. It finishes close to where it began.

## The sequence, count by count

I divide Saifa into five sections.

### Opening: rei, yōi and heiko dachi

Bow in musubi dachi, take kaishu yōi and step out to heiko dachi with closed fists in front of the thighs (count 1).

### Section one: the three releases (counts 2 to 7)

**Count 2.** Step the right foot forward diagonally to the north-east, and bring the left foot up to it so that you stand with the feet together or nearly together, facing north-east. As you step, bring the right fist across to the left side of your chest and cover it with the open left hand, as if placing it in a pocket. The whole body pulls slightly back and to the left.

Most teachers describe this as freeing the right wrist from an opponent's grip. Imagine that someone has grabbed your right wrist with their right hand. You step in close, place your free hand over your trapped fist, and pull it towards your left shoulder, using the strength of both arms and the body against the weakest part of their grip, the thumb.

**Count 3.** Step the left foot back to the south-west into shiko dachi, facing north-east, and as you sink, the right arm swings out: the elbow drives first, then the back of the fist snaps out in uraken uchi to the north-east, while the left hand presses down, palm open, in front of the body. The press and the strike happen together.

In application, the right elbow and fist strike the face of the person whose grip you have just broken, while the left hand controls or presses down their arm.

**Counts 4 and 5.** Repeat to the north-west on the other side: step the left foot to the north-west, bring the right foot up, left fist into right palm, pull; then step the right foot back into shiko dachi with left uraken uchi and right palm pressing.

**Counts 6 and 7.** Repeat counts 2 and 3 to the north-east a third time.

The three repetitions are not mere padding. Each one should be the same, precise and committed. Examiners look closely at the consistency of the three angles and the three stances.

### Section two: knee, kick and hammer fist to the front (counts 8 to 10)

**Count 8.** Turn to face north. Draw the feet together and lift the right knee high into sagi ashi dachi, standing on the left leg. As the knee rises, both forearms rise in front of the face and part, open hands, as if spreading apart two arms that are pushing towards you.

**Count 9.** Kick right mae geri from the raised knee.

**Count 10.** Step down forward into right zenkutsu dachi and strike down with right gedan tettsui, a downward hammer fist, pulling the left fist to the hip. Kiai.

These three counts are a single action in three parts. In application, someone pushes you with both hands; you raise your knee to guard and to prepare, part their arms, kick, and as they double over, drop the hammer fist onto the back of their head or neck.

### Section three: the same to the back (counts 11 to 13)

**Count 11.** Turn round to face south, lifting the left knee into sagi ashi dachi on the right leg, forearms rising and parting.

**Count 12.** Kick left mae geri.

**Count 13.** Step down into left zenkutsu dachi with left gedan tettsui. Kiai. (Many associations have only one kiai in this part of Saifa; see the variations section.)

### Section four: hook and strike to the sides (counts 14 to 17)

**Count 14.** Turn to face west, into left zenkutsu dachi, and perform left kake uke, the open hand hooking and drawing.

**Count 15.** Keeping the stance, strike right tettsui uchi over the top of the hooking hand, the hips turning into the strike, while the left hand pulls to the hip.

**Counts 16 and 17.** Turn to face east into right zenkutsu dachi with right kake uke, then strike left tettsui uchi.

In application, the hooking hand catches the opponent's punching arm or grabs their wrist and draws them forward and off balance, and the hammer fist strikes over the top to the side of the head or the collarbone.

### Section five: mawashi uke (count 18)

**Count 18.** Turn to face north, stepping back into left neko ashi dachi, and perform mawashi uke, finishing with the double palm push.

### Closing

**Count 19.** Draw back into musubi dachi, hands open, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Saifa should feel like a series of short bursts separated by moments of stillness. The main rhythm points are:

- **Counts 2 and 3.** The pull of the release is firm and slightly slower; the step back into shiko dachi and the uraken are fast and sharp. Breathe in during the pull, out on the strike. Pause briefly before the next release.
- **Counts 8 to 10.** The knee lift and the parting of the arms should be smooth and controlled, then a fast kick and an explosive landing with the hammer fist. Breathe in as the knee lifts, out on the kick and again on the hammer fist with the kiai.
- **Counts 14 to 17.** The hook should be smooth and the strike sharp. Each side is a single short breath.
- **Count 18.** Slow, deliberate and strong, with a long out-breath on the double push.

A good Saifa usually takes between forty-five and sixty-five seconds.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### The release (counts 2 and 3)

Your partner grabs your right wrist with their right hand. Step forward diagonally towards them, so that you are close and slightly to their outside. Place your right fist in your left palm and pull it sharply up and across towards your left shoulder, turning your body as you do so. The pull works against their thumb, the weakest point of the grip. As your hand comes free, step back into shiko dachi and swing the right elbow and fist into their face, while your left hand presses their arm down out of the way.

Practise this slowly and gently at first. Grip releases are surprisingly effective when the angle is right and surprisingly useless when it is not. Experiment with the direction of the pull until you can feel it working against the thumb every time.

![**Figure 5.1** Saifa, count 2: stepping in close to a wrist grab and preparing the release. Both hands and the whole body work together against the thumb.](figures/bk_saifa_1.png)

### The knee and hammer fist (counts 8 to 10)

Your partner pushes you in the chest with both hands. Instead of being pushed back, lift your knee between you as a guard, and bring your forearms up inside their arms, parting them. The kick follows to the groin or lower abdomen. As your partner bends forward, step in and drop the hammer fist onto the back of the neck or the base of the skull. In training, of course, the hammer fist stops well short.

An alternative application sees the parting arms as clearing a two-handed grab to the collar, and the hammer fist as a strike to the collarbone or to an arm.

![**Figure 5.2** Saifa, count 10: the hammer fist drops onto an opponent who has been bent forward by the kick.](figures/bk_saifa_2.png)

### Hook and strike (counts 14 and 15)

Your partner punches with their right hand. Turn and meet it with your left kake uke, hooking around the wrist and drawing it to your left so that their body turns away from you. Strike over the top with your right hammer fist to the side of the head, the neck or the collarbone. The pull of the hooking hand and the turn of your hips into the strike should happen together.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: the diagonal angles are wrong.** *Cause*: guessing the angle rather than knowing it. *Solution*: mark the corners of a square on the floor with tape, with you at the centre. Practise stepping to each corner and checking that your chest faces it squarely.

**Problem: the release looks like a gesture.** *Cause*: the hands move but the body does not. *Solution*: the whole body pulls. Practise with a partner holding your wrist. If they do not lose their grip, your body is not working.

**Problem: the uraken lacks power.** *Cause*: it is thrown from the shoulder without the drop into shiko dachi. *Solution*: practise stepping back into shiko dachi with the hands on the hips, feeling the weight drop. Then add the elbow, then the fist. The power comes from the drop.

**Problem: falling over in sagi ashi dachi.** *Cause*: weak balance or a supporting knee that is locked. *Solution*: keep the supporting knee soft. Practise standing on one leg with the knee raised for thirty seconds on each side every day.

**Problem: the hammer fist is pushed rather than dropped.** *Cause*: the arm is doing the work. *Solution*: the hammer fist drops with the body as you land in zenkutsu dachi. Practise landing from the kick with the fist striking at the moment the front foot touches the floor.

**Problem: the hook in section four is a block, not a hook.** *Cause*: the hand stops on contact. *Solution*: the hand should continue round and draw back slightly, as if it has caught something. Practise with a partner's arm.

**Problem: rushing the mawashi uke.** *Cause*: relief at reaching the end. *Solution*: decide in advance that the final count will take as long as the previous four put together.

## Deeper study: why Saifa moves on the diagonal

Students often ask why Saifa begins by stepping to the corners rather than straight ahead. The answer becomes obvious the moment someone grabs your wrist. If you pull straight back against a grip, you are pulling against the strongest part of the opponent's hand and against their whole body weight. If you step diagonally forward and to the outside of the grip, three things happen at once. You move off the line of their free hand, so that a follow-up punch is harder to land. You turn your trapped hand so that the gap between their thumb and fingers faces the direction of your pull. And you bring your own body weight close behind your arm, so that the release is driven by your legs and hips rather than by your biceps.

Try this with a partner. Have them grip your right wrist firmly with their right hand. First, pull straight back with the arm alone; notice how hard it is. Then step forward to the forty-five-degree angle, bring the feet together, cover the fist with your left palm and pull up and across towards your left shoulder. Most people are astonished at how easily the grip opens. That single experiment explains the first seven counts of the kata better than any amount of description.

The same logic explains the drop into shiko dachi that follows. Having broken the grip, you are standing very close to your opponent with your feet together, which is a poor position to strike from. Stepping the rear foot back into shiko dachi does two things: it lowers your centre of gravity, so that you cannot easily be pushed or pulled, and it gives the strike a vertical drop as well as a horizontal swing. A strike that combines a falling body with a swinging arm carries far more force than either alone.

### The three repetitions

Why three releases, and why the third back to the original corner? Many teachers point out that the repetitions train you to release either hand, and to do it repeatedly under pressure. In the dojo you will meet partners who grip harder the second time, or who grab the other hand as soon as you free the first. Saifa's opening rehearses exactly that: free the right, free the left, free the right again.

There is also a less obvious lesson. Each release ends in shiko dachi, and each new release begins by stepping out of shiko dachi diagonally forward. So the three repetitions are also three practices of moving smoothly from a low stance to a high one and back again. That transition, from rooted to mobile and back, is one of the most important skills in the whole system, and Saifa introduces it in its first minute.

### The hammer fist and the parted arms

The middle section of Saifa (counts 8 to 13) is sometimes performed as if the arms were simply rising for decoration while the knee lifts. In fact the parting of the forearms is a technique in its own right. Picture someone pushing you hard in the chest with both hands, or seizing your lapels. Your forearms come up between their arms and part outwards, breaking their structure at the elbows. At the same moment your knee rises between you, protecting your groin and loading the kick. When the kick lands and your opponent folds forward, their head drops exactly where your hammer fist is coming down.

The lesson for performance is that counts 8, 9 and 10 should feel like a single event in three beats, not three separate moves. The arms part as the knee rises; the kick snaps from the top of the knee lift; the hammer fist lands as the foot touches down. If there is any pause between them, the logic of the sequence is lost.

## Partner drills

These drills build the skills of Saifa progressively. Practise each one slowly and with control, under supervision, and with a partner who agrees to the level of resistance.

**Drill 1: the release, cooperative.** Your partner grips your right wrist with their right hand. Perform counts 2 and 3: step diagonally in, cover and pull, drop back into shiko dachi with the uraken stopping short of the face. Ten repetitions each side, then swap roles. The partner should hold firmly but not resist the release.

**Drill 2: the release, resisting.** As drill 1, but the partner tries to keep hold. Start at about thirty per cent resistance and build up over several sessions. If the release fails, slow down and look at the angle of your pull rather than pulling harder.

**Drill 3: double release.** Your partner grips both wrists. Release one hand with the Saifa action, strike, then release the other. This rehearses the switching between sides in counts 2 to 7.

**Drill 4: push and knee.** Your partner pushes your chest with both hands at walking pace. Lift the knee and part their arms as in count 8, then step back down without kicking. The aim is to absorb the push without being moved backwards. Twenty repetitions.

**Drill 5: hook and hammer.** Your partner punches slowly to your face. Turn into zenkutsu dachi, hook the arm with kake uke and draw it across you, then bring the hammer fist over the top to stop just short of the side of the head. Ten each side.

## Case files from the dojo

The following cases are composites drawn from many students over the years. Each one describes a problem, the diagnosis and the solution.

::: casefile
**Case file 5.1: the student whose releases never worked**

*The problem.* A fifth-kyū student had a neat, accurate Saifa, but in partner work could not escape from a grip at all. Frustrated, the student had concluded that the release in the kata was symbolic.

*The diagnosis.* Watching closely, the instructor noticed that the student stepped diagonally with the feet but kept the shoulders square to the original front. The body never moved behind the arm, so the pull came from the arm alone, and the hand turned the wrong way, pulling against the fingers rather than the thumb.

*The solution.* The student practised the step and turn without the hands, making sure that the chest faced the corner squarely. Then the hands were added slowly, with the partner calling out "thumb" as the wrist turned towards the gap. Within two sessions the release was working against moderate resistance, and the student's kata, too, became noticeably more convincing.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 5.2: the wobbling heron**

*The problem.* A student repeatedly lost balance in sagi ashi dachi at counts 8 and 11, and occasionally had to put the foot down before kicking.

*The diagnosis.* The supporting knee was locked straight, and the student was looking down at the raised knee.

*The solution.* A soft supporting knee and a level gaze. The student practised standing in sagi ashi dachi for thirty seconds on each side at the start of every session, eyes fixed on a point at head height on the far wall. Within three weeks the balance problem had disappeared.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 5.3: the hammer that pushed**

*The problem.* An adult beginner with a background in boxing performed the hammer fist as a long, pushing punch rather than a dropping strike.

*The diagnosis.* Old habits: the student was driving the arm horizontally from the shoulder, as in a jab.

*The solution.* The instructor had the student practise counts 9 and 10 against a pad held low, at about the height of a bent-over opponent's neck, insisting that the fist should land at exactly the moment the front foot touched the floor. The timing changed the technique: once the strike was tied to the landing, it began to drop rather than push.
:::

## Self-test

Answer these questions without looking back at the chapter. Then check your answers.

1. What do the characters of Saifa's name usually mean?
2. In which direction do you step for the first release, and which foot moves first?
3. Against which part of the opponent's grip does the release work?
4. What does the left hand do while the right hand strikes uraken in count 3?
5. How many releases are there, and in which directions?
6. What happens to the arms as the knee lifts in count 8?
7. When should the hammer fist land in count 10?
8. What is the difference between kake uke and an ordinary block?
9. In which stance and direction is the closing mawashi uke performed?
10. Roughly how long should a good performance of Saifa take?

*Answers*: (1) smash and tear, or destroy and defeat; (2) north-east, right foot; (3) the thumb; (4) presses down, open palm; (5) three, to the north-east, north-west and north-east; (6) they rise and part, open-handed; (7) as the front foot touches the floor; (8) kake uke hooks and keeps hold; (9) left neko ashi dachi, facing north; (10) between forty-five and sixty-five seconds.

## Variations between associations

Saifa varies more than the Gekisai kata. Some of the main differences are:

- In some versions, the releases are performed with the feet coming fully together into musubi dachi; in others, the rear foot comes up only partly.
- The strike in the shiko dachi of section one is variously described and performed as uraken, as an elbow strike followed by uraken, or as a forearm strike.
- Some lineages perform section one with the third release to the north-west rather than the north-east, or perform four releases rather than three.
- The order of the front and back sections differs in some versions, and the turn to the south may be made with a different foot.
- Some versions have only one kiai, on the first hammer fist; others have two.
- Section four in some lineages is performed in shiko dachi rather than zenkutsu dachi, or uses a different hand technique for the hook.
- Saifa in Shitō-ryū and Isshin-ryū differs considerably, though the opening release is recognisable in all of them.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Saifa**

*Typical grade*: around fifth to fourth kyū in many Okinawan-line associations; sometimes later.

*What the examiners look for*:

- Accurate, consistent forty-five-degree angles on the three releases.
- A release that involves the whole body, not just the arms.
- A strong drop into shiko dachi with the strike.
- Good balance in sagi ashi dachi, with the arms rising and parting smoothly.
- A fast, controlled kick and a hammer fist that lands with the front foot.
- Kake uke that hooks, rather than stops.
- A slow, circular, powerful mawashi uke.
- Clear contrast between the slower pulls and the fast strikes.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: wrong angles; collapsing shiko dachi; wobbling on one leg; hammer fist pushed with the arm; mawashi uke rushed.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Walk the diagonals and the lines without techniques, ten times each session. Practise the release with a partner gripping your wrist.

**Week two.** Practise each section five times slowly. Add the one-leg balance drill (thirty seconds each side, five times) to every session.

**Week three.** Work on rhythm: practise section one ten times, concentrating on the slow pull and fast strike. Practise sections two and three as continuous actions. Then perform the whole kata five times.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience three times each session, using the grading notes as a checklist. Practise the bunkai at least twice in the week.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Saifa, "smash and tear", is the first classical open-hand kata in most syllabuses.
- It moves on the diagonals, uses both hands together, and introduces the release, the knee lift and the hammer fist.
- The release is a whole-body action against the thumb, followed by a strike as you drop into shiko dachi.
- The knee, kick and hammer fist are one action in three parts.
- Kake uke hooks and keeps hold. Mawashi uke is slow and circular.

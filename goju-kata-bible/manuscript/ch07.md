# Shisochin

::: chapter-intro
Shisochin is often described as the most graceful of the Gōjū kata, and it is also one of the most powerful. It opens with three slow spear-hand thrusts in sanchin dachi, moves to the four directions with palm strikes and elbow strikes, and stamps through an opponent's knee. It was reportedly one of Miyagi Chōjun's favourite kata in later life. Learning it marks the point at which many students stop being beginners.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Shisochin* is usually translated as "destroy in four directions", "fighting in four directions" or "four calm battles". The four directions are clearly visible in the kata, which turns to face east and west, and then attacks on the diagonals.

Like Saifa and Seiyunchin, Shisochin is generally associated with Miyagi Chōjun's own teaching. It is said that in his later years he particularly liked to perform it. Whether or not that is true, it is easy to see why a mature karateka might be drawn to it: it requires little athleticism but a great deal of control, and it rewards depth of understanding rather than youthful strength.

In many Okinawan-line associations, Shisochin is the kata of first kyū or shodan, the threshold of black belt. It is also found in Shitō-ryū.

## Character of the kata

Shisochin combines three qualities that we have met separately in earlier kata.

**The breath and tension of Sanchin.** The opening three nukite are performed slowly, in sanchin dachi, with dynamic tension and deep breathing. They are, in effect, an open-handed version of the opening of Sanchin.

**The open hands of Tenshō.** Shisochin is full of palm strikes (*shōtei*), hooking blocks and pushes. The hands are open for most of the kata, and they move with the flowing, rotating quality of Tenshō.

**The low, destructive kicks of Naha-te.** Shisochin introduces the *kansetsu geri*, a stamping kick to the knee joint, which appears again in Seisan and Kururunfa. Unlike the mae geri of the earlier kata, it is not a snapping kick but a crushing one.

The overall character is often described as soft on the outside and hard on the inside. The movements look fluid and elegant; the techniques they contain are anything but gentle.

## Before you begin

You need a good Sanchin and a reasonable Tenshō before learning Shisochin. In particular you should be comfortable with:

- sanchin dachi, stepping forward and back with the crescent step, and turning;
- slow nukite with dynamic tension and ibuki breathing;
- shōtei (palm-heel strike), forward and to the side;
- kake uke;
- kansetsu geri (practise it gently against a pad, never against a partner's knee);
- empi uchi;
- mawashi uke.

## The embusen

Shisochin moves straight forward for its opening, then back a step, turns east and west in shiko dachi, attacks on the two forward diagonals, comes forward again, turns south and finally comes back to finish near the start. The shape is roughly a cross with arms on the diagonals.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow, kaishu yōi, step out into heiko dachi with open hands (count 1).

### Section one: the three slow nukite (counts 2 to 5)

**Count 2.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and bring both open hands up into *kaishu morote chūdan uke*, the open-handed double middle block, as in Sanchin but with the hands open. Breathe in.

**Count 3.** Slowly thrust right *yonhon nukite*, a four-finger spear hand, forward at chest height, while the left hand draws back to the hip, open. Breathe out with tension throughout the thrust.

**Count 4.** Step forward with the left foot into left sanchin dachi, the right hand returning to guard, and slowly thrust left nukite.

**Count 5.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and slowly thrust right nukite.

As in Sanchin, the hands and the breath move together. The thrust begins with the in-breath complete and finishes as the out-breath ends. Do not rush.

### Section two: hook, palm and push (counts 6 and 7)

**Count 6.** Quickly perform right kake uke, the right hand hooking out and around, and strike with left shōtei to the body.

**Count 7.** Step back with the right foot into left sanchin dachi and push forward with both palms (*morote shōtei oshi*), a strong double push driven from the back leg and the breath.

### Section three: east and west (counts 8 and 9)

**Count 8.** Turn to face north in shiko dachi, moving towards the east, and strike right yoko shōtei, a sideways palm-heel strike, to the east.

**Count 9.** Shift to the west, still in shiko dachi, and strike left yoko shōtei to the west.

These two counts are the first of the "four directions". The strikes travel from the centre of the body directly out to the side, driven by the shift of the hips.

### Section four: the diagonals (counts 10 to 15)

**Count 10.** Step to the north-east into right zenkutsu dachi and perform right kake uke.

**Count 11.** Holding the hook, perform right kansetsu geri, stamping down and forward through the line of an opponent's knee.

**Count 12.** Land forward into right zenkutsu dachi and drive right empi uchi.

**Counts 13 to 15.** Turn to the north-west and repeat on the left: left zenkutsu dachi with left kake uke; left kansetsu geri; land with left empi uchi.

These are the second and third of the four directions. Each diagonal is a complete encounter: catch the arm, break the knee, strike with the elbow.

### Section five: forward, then south (counts 16 to 19)

**Count 16.** Step forward to face north, then turn into shiko dachi facing west. Strike right uraken uchi to the north while the left hand pulls in.

**Count 17.** Right gedan barai, with a kiai.

**Count 18.** Turn round to face south into left sanchin dachi with left kake uke.

**Count 19.** Right shōtei uchi.

### Section six: mawashi uke (count 20)

**Count 20.** Turn to face north in left neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke with the double push.

### Closing

**Count 21.** Musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

- **Section one** is slow and tense, with ibuki breathing. Each nukite takes about six to eight seconds.
- **Sections two and three** are faster but still deliberate. The double push in count 7 should be performed with a strong, audible out-breath.
- **Section four** is fast and explosive on each diagonal, with a pause between them.
- **Section five** is fast to the kiai, then calmer for the turn to the south.
- **Section six** is slow and strong.

A good Shisochin takes around one and a half to two minutes.

The great danger in Shisochin is that the slow opening sets a tempo that the student then carries through the rest of the kata, so that the whole kata becomes slow and heavy. Work hard on the change of pace at count 6.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### The slow nukite (counts 2 and 3)

Taken literally, the nukite is a spear-hand strike to the throat or the solar plexus. But a slow, tense spear hand is hardly a practical strike. Many teachers understand the slow nukite as a training method, conditioning the fingers, wrists and forearms, and the movement itself as a reaching, grasping action: the hand slides forward along an opponent's arm to take hold of the collar, the throat or the back of the neck.

Try it with a partner who holds your forearm. Instead of pulling away, slide your hand forward along their arm as you breathe out and sink into sanchin dachi. You will find that your hand reaches their body with surprising force, and that their grip on you becomes a lever for you to use.

### The double push (count 7)

Your partner closes in and grabs you, or punches with both hands. You step back to make space, absorb their momentum in sanchin dachi, and push them away explosively with both palms to the chest. The step back and the push should feel like a single movement: retreat, root, release.

The double push recurs in the mawashi uke at the end of almost every kaishu kata. Here in Shisochin it stands on its own, and it repays study. A good double push can move a much heavier person, because it uses the whole body and the breath rather than the arms.

![**Figure 7.2** Shisochin, count 7: the double palm push from sanchin dachi, driven by the back leg and the breath.](figures/bk_shisochin_2.png)

### Hook, stamp and elbow (counts 10 to 12)

Your partner punches. Step forward and diagonally outside the punch, catching the arm with kake uke. Holding the arm, stamp down onto the front of their knee or thigh with kansetsu geri. As they buckle, land forward and strike with the elbow.

**A safety warning.** Kansetsu geri is designed to damage the knee. Never practise it against a partner's knee with any force, and never at speed. Practise the stamp against a pad or a kicking shield, and in partner work, place the foot gently on the thigh or let it stop short.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: the slow nukite become slow punches with open hands.** *Cause*: no sense of what the hand is doing. *Solution*: practise the bunkai above. Feel the hand reaching, sliding and gripping.

**Problem: the whole kata becomes slow.** *Cause*: the opening sets the tempo. *Solution*: practise from count 6 onwards only, at full speed, many times. Then join the opening on and make the change of pace deliberate.

**Problem: weak palm-heel strikes.** *Cause*: the wrist bends back too far or not enough, or the strike comes from the shoulder. *Solution*: practise shōtei against a pad. The wrist should be bent back firmly, the fingers pointing up or out, and the force should come from the hips. Feel the heel of the hand, not the fingers, meeting the pad.

**Problem: the yoko shōtei in section three are arm movements.** *Cause*: the hips are not shifting. *Solution*: practise shifting from one side to the other in shiko dachi with the hands on the hips, then add the strikes.

**Problem: kansetsu geri looks like mae geri.** *Cause*: snapping the kick out and back. *Solution*: the kansetsu geri is a stamp, driving down and through with the heel or the edge of the foot. Practise against a low pad on the floor.

**Problem: losing balance on the diagonal kicks.** *Cause*: the hooking hand pulls you forward. *Solution*: in application, the hook draws the opponent towards you, not you towards them. Keep your hips over your supporting foot.

**Problem: the kiai comes from the throat.** *Cause*: shouting rather than contracting. *Solution*: practise the kiai on the out-breath of the double push, using the abdomen.

## Deeper study: Shisochin and its relationship with Sanchin and Tenshō

Of all the kaishu kata, Shisochin shows most clearly how the two heishugata, Sanchin and Tenshō, feed into the rest of the system. It is worth spending some time on this relationship, because understanding it will improve your Shisochin and your Sanchin and Tenshō at the same time.

Sanchin teaches the body to be a single, rooted unit. Its stance is closed and strong, its breath long and powerful, its techniques straight and driven from the ground. Tenshō, created by Miyagi as Sanchin's partner, keeps the same stance and breath but opens the hands and lets them circle, press, hook and push. Sanchin is predominantly *gō*; Tenshō is predominantly *jū*.

Shisochin's opening takes the stance and breath of Sanchin and combines them with the open hands of Tenshō. The three slow nukite are, in effect, Sanchin performed with open hands. Then, from count 6 onwards, the kata starts to move. The hook and palm strike, the double push, the sideways palm strikes, the hooks on the diagonals: these are Tenshō's hand techniques taken out of a fixed stance and put into motion.

This gives you a practical training method. If your Shisochin opening is weak, practise Sanchin. If your hooks and palm strikes are weak, practise Tenshō. If both are strong but your Shisochin is still unconvincing, the problem lies in the transitions, in taking those qualities into movement, and you should practise Shisochin's sections in isolation.

### The four directions

The name Shisochin refers to four directions, and the kata works through them in an interesting order. It begins by advancing to the front. It then turns to the east and the west, striking sideways from shiko dachi without stepping. Then it moves out to the two forward diagonals, stepping and kicking. Finally it turns to the rear.

One way to understand this pattern is as an encounter with several opponents. Having driven forward against the first, you are threatened from the sides and strike out without moving your feet; then you step out to deal with two more on the diagonals; finally you turn to meet someone behind you. Another way, more useful for bunkai, is to treat each direction as a different angle of approach by a single opponent, and each set of techniques as a different response depending on where they come from.

### The palm strike

Shōtei, the palm-heel strike, is the signature hand technique of Shisochin. It deserves special attention, because it is easy to perform badly and very effective when performed well.

A good palm strike lands with the heel of the hand, the hard area at the base of the palm just above the wrist. The wrist is bent firmly back, so that the forearm bones line up behind the heel of the hand. The fingers are bent slightly and held together, pointing up or out depending on the direction of the strike. The force comes, as always, from the hips and legs.

The palm strike has several advantages over the fist. It is far less likely to injure the hand when striking the head. It transmits force into a larger area, which makes it effective for pushing and unbalancing. And it is naturally linked with grabbing, because an open hand that has just struck can immediately take hold.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: sliding nukite.** Your partner grips your forearm with both hands. Breathing out slowly in sanchin dachi, slide the hand forward along their arm towards their chest. Feel how the grip becomes a lever. Five each side.

**Drill 2: the double push.** Your partner stands in front of you holding a kick shield against their chest. Step back into sanchin dachi and push with both palms, driven from the back leg and the breath. Your partner notes how far they are moved. Ten repetitions.

**Drill 3: side palms.** Two partners stand to your east and west holding pads at head height. Strike right and left yoko shōtei from shiko dachi, shifting the hips. Ten each side.

**Drill 4: hook and stamp, padded.** Your partner punches slowly; you hook the arm on the diagonal, then stamp onto a kick shield held low against the side of their leg, never the knee itself. Ten each side.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 7.1: the sleepy kata**

*The problem.* A first-kyū candidate's Shisochin was technically accurate but drew the comment from the examiners that it "never woke up".

*The diagnosis.* The slow opening had set a tempo that lasted the whole kata. Every technique after count 6 was performed at about two-thirds of fighting speed.

*The solution.* For three weeks the student practised Shisochin only from count 6 onwards, at full speed, five times per session. Then the opening was added back, with a deliberate pause of one breath after count 5, followed by an explosive start to count 6. The change of pace transformed the kata.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 7.2: the sore wrists**

*The problem.* A student complained of sore wrists after practising palm strikes on a pad.

*The diagnosis.* The wrist was not bent back far enough, so that the force went through the base of the fingers and the wrist joint rather than through the heel of the hand.

*The solution.* The instructor reduced the force, corrected the wrist angle and added wrist-strengthening exercises with a light chi ishi. The soreness disappeared and the palm strikes became noticeably more solid.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 7.3: the kick that tipped forward**

*The problem.* On the diagonal kansetsu geri, a student kept falling forward onto the kicking foot.

*The diagnosis.* The hooking hand was pulling the student towards the imaginary opponent instead of drawing the opponent in.

*The solution.* The student practised the hook with a partner holding a belt, pulling the belt in towards the hip while keeping the hips over the supporting foot. Once the pull was correct, the stamp could be delivered from a stable base.
:::

## Variations between associations

- The opening nukite are performed in some versions with the hands rotating as they thrust; in others, straight.
- Some versions include a slow open-hand block between each nukite, as in Sanchin.
- The east-west palm strikes in section three are performed in some lineages in sanchin dachi rather than shiko dachi, or are preceded by a turn and a double block.
- The order and direction of the diagonals vary, and some versions perform them in sanchin dachi rather than zenkutsu dachi.
- Some versions include a throwing action or a double strike in section five, and the kiai point varies.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Shisochin**

*Typical grade*: first kyū or shodan in many Okinawan-line associations.

*What the examiners look for*:

- A Sanchin-quality opening: rooted sanchin dachi, controlled tension, audible but unforced breathing.
- A clear change of pace after the slow section.
- Strong palm strikes with the heel of the hand, driven by the hips.
- Accurate turns to the four directions.
- Kansetsu geri performed as a stamp, not a snap, with good balance.
- Elbow strikes landing with the front foot.
- A slow, powerful mawashi uke.
- Overall, the impression of softness containing power.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: a slow, heavy kata throughout; weak sanchin dachi; arm-only palm strikes; poor balance on the stamps.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Sanchin every session, three times. Then practise Shisochin's section one five times, slowly.

**Week two.** Practise sections two to four five times each. Add palm-strike work on a pad: twenty strikes each hand, forward and to the side.

**Week three.** Whole kata five times, concentrating on the change of pace. Practise kansetsu geri against a low pad, twenty each side.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience. Practise the bunkai of the nukite and the double push with a partner.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Shisochin, "destroy in four directions", combines the breathing of Sanchin, the open hands of Tenshō and the low stamping kicks of Naha-te.
- The opening three nukite are slow, tense and breathing; treat them as a reaching, grasping action as well as a strike.
- After the opening, change pace sharply. Shisochin is not a slow kata.
- Palm strikes use the heel of the hand and come from the hips.
- Kansetsu geri is a stamp. Practise it on pads, never on knees.
:::

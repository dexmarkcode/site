# Kururunfa

::: chapter-intro
Kururunfa is the most subtle of the Gōjū kata. It is quiet where others are loud, light where others are heavy, sudden where others build slowly. It lives in neko ashi dachi, turns to the four corners, stamps through the knee, scoops a leg and drops an opponent. Senior karateka often say that it is the kata they understand least and love most.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

The name *Kururunfa* is usually translated as "holding on long and striking suddenly", "forever peace and tranquillity" or "tranquillity and sudden violence". The first and last of these capture the character of the kata exactly: a long, patient holding followed by an explosion. As with most kata names, the characters and their reading are not certain, and some teachers prefer not to translate it at all.

Kururunfa is traditionally attributed to Higaonna Kanryō, and it is also found in Shitō-ryū. In many Okinawan-line associations it is a sandan or yondan kata.

## Character of the kata

Kururunfa's character comes from three things.

**Neko ashi dachi.** Much of the kata is performed in the cat stance, with the weight on the back leg and the front foot light. This makes the kata mobile and evasive, and it means that every kick can be launched without warning.

**The sudden stamp.** Each of the four opening sequences ends with a kansetsu geri that seems to come from nowhere. The kick is not prepared with a chamber; the front foot is already light, and it simply drops through the target.

**The quiet breath.** Kururunfa has none of the heavy, audible breathing of Sanchin or Seiyunchin. The breath is quiet and controlled during the slow movements and short and sharp on the strikes. Some teachers describe the ideal Kururunfa as a kata that can hardly be heard until the kiai.

## Before you begin

Before learning Kururunfa you should be confident in all the earlier kata and especially in:

- neko ashi dachi, stepping and turning to forty-five-degree angles while keeping the weight back;
- slow, sticky kake uke;
- kansetsu geri without a chamber, directly from neko ashi dachi;
- osae (pressing down with the palm) and shōtei;
- sagi ashi dachi with the forearms crossed high;
- sukui uke (scooping block) in zenkutsu dachi.

## The embusen

Kururunfa opens by moving to the four corners in turn, north-east, north-west, south-east and south-west, returns to the centre, moves forward up the centre line and finishes by stepping back.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow, kaishu yōi, step out to heiko dachi with open hands (count 1).

### Section one: the four corners (counts 2 to 11)

**Count 2.** Step to the north-east into right neko ashi dachi. The right hand rises slowly in kake uke, open, the left hand guarding the chest. Breathe in quietly.

**Count 3.** Suddenly, the right foot stamps out in kansetsu geri, and the right hand presses down sharply in osae. Breathe out.

**Count 4.** Land back in neko ashi dachi and strike right shōtei.

**Counts 5 to 7.** Turn to the north-west into left neko ashi dachi and repeat on the other side: slow left kake uke; sudden left kansetsu geri with left osae; left shōtei.

**Counts 8 and 9.** Turn to the south-east into right neko ashi dachi with slow right kake uke, then the sudden right kansetsu geri and osae.

**Counts 10 and 11.** Turn to the south-west into left neko ashi dachi with slow left kake uke, then the sudden left kansetsu geri and osae.

The four corners take a long time and should look identical. They are the heart of the kata and the part examiners watch most closely.

### Section two: the centre (counts 12 to 17)

**Count 12.** Turn to face north and lift the left knee into sagi ashi dachi, crossing the forearms high in front of the face, open-handed.

**Count 13.** Step down into shiko dachi, the open hands parting downward.

**Count 14.** Step forward into right zenkutsu dachi with right sukui uke, a scooping block, as if scooping up an opponent's kicking leg.

**Count 15.** Right mae empi uchi.

**Count 16.** Turn into shiko dachi facing west and snap right uraken uchi.

**Count 17.** Right tettsui uchi, with a kiai.

### Section three: mawashi uke (count 18)

**Count 18.** Step back into left neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke with the double push.

### Closing

**Count 19.** Musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Kururunfa's rhythm is "long hold, sudden strike".

- **Each corner**: a slow, quiet rising of the hand over three or four seconds, then a sudden stamp and press on a short out-breath, then a crisp palm strike. There should be no warning before the stamp.
- **Count 12**: a moment of stillness on one leg.
- **Counts 13 to 17**: a single flowing sequence building to the kiai.
- **Count 18**: slow and strong.

A good Kururunfa takes around one minute to one minute thirty.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### Hook, stamp and palm (counts 2 to 4)

Your partner punches. You step diagonally off the line into neko ashi dachi, and your hand rises to meet the punch, sticking to it and guiding it past you. The slowness of the movement in the kata represents the sticky, controlling quality of the contact. Then, without warning, your front foot drops onto their knee or shin while your hand presses their arm down, breaking their posture. As they buckle, you strike with the palm to the face or jaw.

The stamp and the press are one technique: the hand pulls the opponent's weight onto the leg you are about to kick.

### The scoop (count 14)

Your partner kicks. You step in and scoop the kicking leg with sukui uke, lifting it, and drive your elbow into their body as they lose balance. In application the scoop can continue into a throw, the opponent falling backwards as their supporting leg is swept or their body is pushed.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: the stamp is telegraphed.** *Cause*: lifting the knee or shifting weight before kicking. *Solution*: the weight must already be on the back leg in neko ashi dachi, so the front foot can drop straight through without preparation. Practise the stamp from a stationary neko ashi dachi against a low pad, with a partner watching for any warning movement.

**Problem: the four corners are at different angles or heights.** *Cause*: lack of attention to consistency. *Solution*: mark the corners on the floor. Perform each corner in front of a mirror, checking that the stance height and hand position are identical.

**Problem: the kata is too loud.** *Cause*: carrying the heavy breathing of Sanchin or Seiyunchin into Kururunfa. *Solution*: breathe quietly through the slow movements. Save the sound for the strikes and the kiai.

**Problem: the balance on one leg at count 12 is poor.** *Cause*: weak supporting leg or rushing. *Solution*: hold count 12 for a full two seconds in practice.

**Problem: the neko ashi dachi has too much weight forward.** *Cause*: settling into the stance rather than sitting back. *Solution*: test it as in Chapter 2. Can you lift the front foot without moving the body?

## Deeper study: the art of the sudden

Kururunfa's most striking quality is its suddenness. A hand rises slowly and quietly for three or four seconds, and then, with no warning at all, the foot drops through a knee and the hand presses down. Anyone watching a good Kururunfa for the first time tends to flinch at the first stamp.

This suddenness is not a performance trick. It reflects a central principle of close-range fighting: the most effective technique is the one your opponent does not see coming. A kick prepared by lifting the knee, shifting the weight or tensing the shoulders gives the opponent a fraction of a second to react. A kick that simply happens, from a stance in which the foot was already free, gives them nothing.

Neko ashi dachi makes this possible. Because the weight is already on the back leg, the front foot can move without any preparatory shift. Because the front heel is already raised, the foot does not need to be lifted from the floor. The kansetsu geri of Kururunfa is therefore not so much a kick as a release: the foot was always ready to fall, and you simply let it.

To achieve this, the stance has to be right. If there is too much weight on the front foot, you will have to shift back before you can kick, and the shift will give you away. Test your neko ashi dachi constantly: can you lift the front foot without any movement of your body? If not, your weight is too far forward.

### Quietness

Kururunfa is a quiet kata. In contrast to Sanchin, Seiyunchin and the openings of Shisochin and Suparinpei, its slow movements are performed with soft, nearly silent breathing. This is deliberate. A loud breath is a signal, just like a lifted knee. In Kururunfa, nothing signals.

Students who have spent years developing a strong, audible Sanchin breath often find this difficult. Their slow movements in Kururunfa come out with a heavy, hissing exhalation, which makes the kata sound like Seiyunchin and destroys its character. If this is you, practise the corners of Kururunfa with your mouth closed, breathing gently through the nose, until the slow movements are quiet.

### The four corners

Kururunfa's four corners are the heart of the kata and its greatest test. Each corner should be identical: the same angle, the same stance height, the same speed of rising hand, the same suddenness of kick, the same sharpness of press and palm. Any inconsistency stands out immediately.

The order of the corners is also worth thinking about. You begin at the front right, move to the front left, then turn to the rear right and the rear left. In application, this suggests attacks coming from four directions, each met in the same way. In practice, it means that two of the corners involve turning almost completely round, which makes it harder to keep them consistent with the first two. Practise the turns from the front corners to the rear corners separately.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: the silent stamp.** Stand in neko ashi dachi facing a partner who holds a low kick shield against the side of their own shin. Stamp onto the shield without any visible preparation. Your partner calls out if they see you move before the kick. Ten each side.

**Drill 2: sticky hand and press.** Your partner punches slowly. Step to the diagonal into neko ashi dachi, meet the punch with a rising, sticky kake uke, guide it past you, then press it down sharply. Ten each side.

**Drill 3: the full corner, padded.** Combine drills 1 and 2: rising hand, press and stamp onto the shield, then palm strike to a pad held at head height. Slowly at first.

**Drill 4: the scoop.** Your partner throws a slow front kick. Step in and scoop the leg with sukui uke, lifting it gently, while the other hand guards. Then release. Never lift the leg high enough to throw your partner unless you are supervised and they can break-fall.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 10.1: the loud Kururunfa**

*The problem.* A senior student's Kururunfa was technically strong, but the examiners described it as "Seiyunchin in a cat stance".

*The diagnosis.* The student was using heavy, audible ibuki breathing throughout the slow movements, and the stamps were preceded by a noticeable lift of the knee.

*The solution.* Two months of quiet practice: corners performed with the mouth closed and breathing through the nose, and drill 1 with a partner calling out any preparation. The student later said it was the hardest change they had ever made to a kata, and the most rewarding.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 10.2: four different corners**

*The problem.* A student's four corners were at four slightly different heights and speeds.

*The diagnosis.* The student was performing each corner by feel, without a fixed reference.

*The solution.* Video from the front, and practice in front of a mirror with a tape mark at head height for the rising hand. After a fortnight the corners were consistent.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 10.3: the heavy front foot**

*The problem.* A student shifted visibly backwards before each kansetsu geri.

*The diagnosis.* Too much weight on the front foot in neko ashi dachi.

*The solution.* The neko ashi dachi test: lift the front foot without moving the body. The student practised holding the stance for one minute each side, lifting and replacing the front foot every five seconds, until the weight sat naturally on the back leg.
:::

## Self-test

1. What does Kururunfa usually mean?
2. In which stance is most of the kata performed, and where is the weight?
3. In what order are the four corners performed?
4. What happens at each corner?
5. Why can the kansetsu geri be delivered without preparation?
6. How does the breathing in Kururunfa differ from that in Seiyunchin?
7. What stance is used at count 12, and what do the arms do?
8. What technique is performed at count 14?
9. What is the simple test for a correctly weighted neko ashi dachi?
10. What is the commonest fault in the four corners?

*Answers*: (1) holding on long and striking suddenly, or tranquillity and sudden violence; (2) neko ashi dachi, with the weight on the back leg; (3) north-east, north-west, south-east, south-west; (4) a slow sticky kake uke, a sudden kansetsu geri with a press, and (on the first two) a palm strike; (5) because the weight is already on the back leg and the front foot is free; (6) it is quiet rather than loud; (7) sagi ashi dachi, with the forearms crossed high; (8) sukui uke, the scooping block; (9) you can lift the front foot without moving the body; (10) inconsistency in angle, height or speed.

## Variations between associations

- The opening corners are sometimes performed in a different order, beginning to the north-west or to the south.
- Some versions include a throwing action or a drop to one knee in the centre section.
- The scooping block is sometimes performed in shiko dachi rather than zenkutsu dachi.
- Some versions perform the first two corners in full and the second two in shortened form; others the reverse.
- The kiai point varies.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Kururunfa**

*Typical grade*: sandan or yondan in many Okinawan-line associations.

*What the examiners look for*:

- A light, correctly weighted neko ashi dachi throughout.
- Four consistent corners at accurate angles.
- A clear contrast between the slow rising hand and the sudden stamp.
- A stamp with no preparatory movement.
- Quiet, controlled breathing.
- Balance on one leg at count 12.
- A strong finish from the scoop to the kiai.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: telegraphed kicks; weight forward in neko ashi dachi; inconsistent corners; heavy, loud breathing.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Neko ashi dachi drill: five minutes daily, stepping to the corners. Practise the four corners slowly.

**Week two.** Stamp drill from neko ashi dachi against a low pad: twenty each side. Practise section two five times.

**Week three.** Whole kata five times, concentrating on consistency and quiet breathing.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience. Bunkai of the corners with a partner.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Kururunfa, "holding on long and striking suddenly", is quiet, light and deceptive.
- It lives in neko ashi dachi. Keep the weight on the back leg.
- The four corners are its heart: slow sticky hand, sudden stamp and press, palm strike.
- No warning before the kick. No noise before the kiai.
:::

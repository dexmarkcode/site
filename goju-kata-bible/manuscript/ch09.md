# Seipai

::: chapter-intro
Seipai, "eighteen", is a kata of contrasts. It moves slowly and then suddenly. It drops low into a crossed stance and then spins a full circle on one foot. It pulls and lifts with the open hand and strikes with the elbow, the back-fist and the palm. Of all the Gōjū kata it is perhaps the one that looks most like its Chinese ancestors, and it is the one in which hard and soft are most visibly woven together.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Seipai* means eighteen. As with Seisan and Suparinpei, the number almost certainly has symbolic rather than literal significance: eighteen is six times three, and both six and three carry weight in Buddhist numerology. Some teachers connect it with the eighteen hands of the Lohan, the disciples of the Buddha associated with the Shaolin tradition; others treat it simply as a name.

Seipai is traditionally attributed to Higaonna Kanryō. It is also practised, with differences, in Shitō-ryū. In many Okinawan-line associations it is a nidan or sandan kata.

## Character of the kata

Seipai is unusual in several ways.

**It moves on unexpected lines.** The opening steps back diagonally rather than forward, so that the kata begins by retreating into a defensive position. Later it spins a full three hundred and sixty degrees.

**It changes level.** The kata drops suddenly into kōsa dachi and rises again. It moves between shiko dachi, neko ashi dachi and zenkutsu dachi more freely than earlier kata.

**It mixes slow and fast in a single breath.** In the opening, the hand rises slowly and then strikes suddenly, without a pause. This is the clearest expression in the whole syllabus of the *gō* and *jū* rhythm.

**It is full of grappling.** The slow rising hand, the pull, the elbow, the drop and the spin can all be understood as parts of throws, joint locks and escapes.

## Before you begin

Seipai is demanding. Before learning it you should be confident in all the kata up to Seisan. Particular skills are:

- stepping back diagonally into shiko dachi while keeping the stance strong;
- slow hiki uke with tension, changing instantly to a fast strike;
- age hiji (rising elbow) and uraken uchi;
- neko ashi dachi with shōtei;
- dropping into kōsa dachi and rising from it;
- spinning on one foot with the other knee raised, and landing in balance;
- fumikomi or kansetsu geri.

The spin is the hardest part. Practise it separately, on a smooth floor, before attempting the kata.

## The embusen

Seipai begins by moving back to the rear diagonals, comes forward to the centre, drops and spins, faces south and then north again, and finishes slightly behind the starting point.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow, kaishu yōi, heiko dachi with open hands (count 1).

### Section one: back to the north-west (counts 2 to 4)

**Count 2.** Step the right foot back diagonally to the south-east into shiko dachi, so that you face north-west. As you sink, the left hand rises slowly in hiki uke, hooking and drawing, while the right hand guards the chest, open. Breathe in and then out with tension.

**Count 3.** Suddenly the left hand pulls back to the left side, as if pulling an arm, and the right elbow rises in age hiji, a rising elbow strike.

**Count 4.** Without pause, the right fist snaps out in uraken uchi from the top of the elbow strike.

### Section two: back to the north-east (counts 5 to 7)

**Counts 5 to 7.** Shift across into shiko dachi facing north-east, and repeat on the other side: slow right hiki uke with the left hand guarding; sudden pull and left age hiji; left uraken uchi.

### Section three: forward (counts 8 and 9)

**Count 8.** Step forward to the centre into left neko ashi dachi facing north, with left kake uke.

**Count 9.** Strike right shōtei.

### Section four: the drop and the spin (counts 10 to 13)

**Count 10.** Step forward and drop into kōsa dachi, the right foot crossing, and strike down with left gedan tettsui.

**Count 11.** Rise on the left foot, lifting the right knee, and spin a full turn to the right, finishing facing south with the right hand in kake uke. Spot the turn with the head: look over your shoulder before the body turns.

**Count 12.** Stamp down with the right foot in fumikomi or kansetsu geri.

**Count 13.** Land in right zenkutsu dachi facing south and punch right chūdan tsuki.

### Section five: turning to the front (counts 14 to 17)

**Count 14.** Turn to face north in left zenkutsu dachi with left jōdan age uke.

**Count 15.** Right gyaku tsuki.

**Count 16.** Turn into shiko dachi facing east, snapping left uraken uchi to the north.

**Count 17.** Left gedan barai, with a kiai.

### Section six: mawashi uke (count 18)

**Count 18.** Step back into left neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke with the double push.

### Closing

**Count 19.** Musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

The rhythm of Seipai is its soul.

- **Counts 2 to 4** (and 5 to 7) are performed as one breath: a slow, tense in-and-out with the rising hand, followed instantly by an explosive release on the pull, elbow and back-fist. There must be no pause between the slow phase and the fast phase. The change from soft to hard should be like a door slamming.
- **Counts 8 and 9** are crisp.
- **Counts 10 to 13** are one continuous action: drop, rise, spin, stamp, punch.
- **Counts 14 to 17** build to the kiai.
- **Count 18** is slow and strong.

A good Seipai takes around one minute to one minute twenty seconds.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### The opening (counts 2 to 4)

Your partner punches with their right hand. You step back diagonally, taking yourself off the line of the attack, and your left hand meets the punch from the outside in a slow, sticky hiki uke, hooking the wrist and drawing it past you. Having caught the arm, you pull it sharply down and back, and your right elbow rises into the side of their head or under the jaw. The back-fist follows to the face.

The slow phase of the hiki uke is not slow in application. It represents the sticky, controlling quality of the catch, the moment of softness in which you take control of the opponent's arm. The fast phase is the hardness that follows.

### Drop, spin and stamp (counts 10 to 13)

Several applications are taught. In one, the drop into kōsa dachi follows a thrown opponent to the ground; the hammer fist strikes them; the spin turns you to face a second attacker behind you. In another, the spin itself is a throw: you have hold of the opponent's arm, and the rotation of your body takes them over your hip, with the stamp finishing them on the ground.

Practise the second application only with a qualified instructor and a partner who can break-fall.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: a pause between the slow hand and the fast strike.** *Cause*: treating the two phases as two separate techniques. *Solution*: practise the opening on a single breath. Begin the out-breath slowly with the hiki uke and finish it explosively on the elbow.

**Problem: the step back is to the wrong angle.** *Cause*: stepping straight back or straight sideways. *Solution*: the right foot goes back to the south-east, and you face north-west. Tape the floor.

**Problem: the spin is unbalanced or incomplete.** *Cause*: spinning on the heel, or the head not leading. *Solution*: spin on the ball of the supporting foot, with the knee soft. Turn the head first, and pick a spot to look at. Practise spins separately, ten each session.

**Problem: the drop into kōsa dachi is a collapse.** *Cause*: dropping without control. *Solution*: practise lowering into kōsa dachi slowly, then at normal speed. The rear knee tucks behind the front knee and hovers above the floor.

**Problem: the kata looks Chinese in places and Japanese in others.** *Cause*: a mixture of soft, flowing movements and hard, stopped ones without a unifying rhythm. *Solution*: this is actually close to right. Seipai is meant to contain both qualities. What makes it look unified is the breath. Make the breathing pattern strong and consistent and the movements will follow.

## Deeper study: hard and soft in a single breath

If you wanted to show someone what *gō* and *jū* mean in practice, the opening of Seipai would be the place to start. In the space of a single breath, the left hand rises slowly and softly, and then, without any pause, the body explodes into a pull, a rising elbow and a back-fist. Soft becomes hard as suddenly as a door slamming shut.

This is not merely a dramatic contrast for the sake of performance. It reflects something true about close-range fighting. When you first make contact with an opponent's arm, you need to be soft: sensitive, sticky, able to feel where their force is going and to follow it without resistance. A hard block at this moment would simply bounce off, or tell your opponent exactly where you are. Once you have control, you need to be hard: decisive, fast and powerful, before they can recover. The slow phase of the hiki uke is the soft phase of control. The fast phase is the hard phase of attack.

The breath ties the two together. The slow phase is performed on the beginning of the out-breath, steady and controlled; the fast phase on the end of it, sharp and sudden. Because the breath never stops, there is no pause between soft and hard. If you breathe in between the slow hand and the elbow, the connection is lost and the opponent, in application, has time to escape.

### The Chinese flavour

Many observers comment that Seipai looks more Chinese than the other Gōjū kata. Its open hands, its sudden changes of level, its spin and its flowing transitions all recall the southern Chinese boxing styles from which Naha-te is believed to have drawn. Some of its movements have close parallels in certain crane and tiger styles.

For the practitioner, this flavour is a reminder that Gōjū-ryū is not a purely Japanese art, and that its kata were not designed for the long straight lines and stopped techniques of later Japanese karate. Seipai should flow. The stops are there, at the end of each technique, but they are brief, and the transitions between them should be smooth and continuous.

### The spin

The full spin of count 11 is the technical showpiece of the kata, and it is where many otherwise good performances come unstuck. The principles are the same as for any turn in karate, only more so. The head leads: look over your right shoulder before your body turns. The spin happens on the ball of the supporting foot, never the heel. The supporting knee stays soft. The raised knee stays close to the body, which makes the spin faster and more stable, in the same way that a skater spins faster when they pull their arms in. And the eyes find the new direction, the south, before the body arrives there, so that you land knowing exactly where you are.

Practise spins separately, ten in each direction, at the start of each session, until they are completely reliable. Then practise counts 10 to 13 as a unit. Only then put them back into the kata.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: sticky hands.** Your partner extends their right arm slowly towards your face. Step back diagonally into shiko dachi and meet the arm with your left hand from the outside, staying in contact and guiding it past you. Feel their force; do not oppose it. Ten each side.

**Drill 2: soft to hard.** As drill 1, but once you have control, pull the arm sharply down and bring the rising elbow towards the side of their head, stopping short. Practise on one breath.

**Drill 3: follow down.** Your partner lies on a mat. Step forward and drop into kōsa dachi beside them, pressing with one hand and bringing the hammer fist down to stop just above them. Ten repetitions.

**Drill 4: spin and find.** Two partners stand in front of and behind you. On a signal, spin to face the partner behind you, landing ready to strike. The partners change position after each repetition so that you cannot predict exactly where you will need to land.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 9.1: the hesitation**

*The problem.* A nidan candidate performed a beautiful slow hiki uke at the start of Seipai, followed by an equally good elbow and back-fist, but with a clear pause between them.

*The diagnosis.* The candidate was breathing in between the slow and fast phases.

*The solution.* Breath-only practice of the opening, then drill 2 with a partner, insisting on one breath for the whole sequence. After two weeks the pause had gone, and the opening had the explosive quality the kata demands.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 9.2: the dizzy spin**

*The problem.* A student landed from the spin facing south-west instead of south, and was visibly disoriented.

*The diagnosis.* The student was spinning with the eyes fixed on nothing in particular.

*The solution.* Spotting, as dancers do: pick a mark on the south wall before the spin, turn the head to find it as quickly as possible, and let the body follow. Within a week the landings were accurate.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 9.3: the uniform kata**

*The problem.* A student's Seipai was accurate but monotonous, every technique at the same speed and tension.

*The diagnosis.* The student had learned the kata section by section with a teacher counting each movement aloud at an even rhythm, and had never unlearned the count.

*The solution.* The instructor marked the count sheet with S (slow), F (fast) and P (pause), and the student practised to these markings, at first exaggerating the differences. Over a month, the exaggeration was reduced until the rhythm felt natural.
:::

## Self-test

1. What does Seipai mean?
2. In which direction do you step for the first movement, and which way do you face?
3. What does the slow hiki uke represent in application?
4. What must not happen between the slow phase and the fast phase of the opening?
5. Which techniques follow the pull in counts 3 and 4?
6. What stance do you drop into at count 10?
7. On which part of the foot do you spin, and what leads the spin?
8. Why does keeping the raised knee close to the body help the spin?
9. Which direction do you face after the spin?
10. Why is Seipai often said to look Chinese?

*Answers*: (1) eighteen; (2) the right foot steps back to the south-east; you face north-west; (3) the soft, sticky phase of controlling an opponent's arm; (4) a pause or an in-breath; (5) a rising elbow and an uraken; (6) kōsa dachi; (7) the ball of the supporting foot, led by the head; (8) a compact body turns faster and more stably; (9) south; (10) its open hands, changes of level, spin and flowing transitions recall southern Chinese boxing.

## Variations between associations

- The opening steps back to the diagonal in most versions, but some step forward or sideways.
- The hand action in the opening varies: some versions use an open hand rising, others a closed fist, and the follow-up is sometimes a palm strike rather than an elbow.
- The spin is a full turn in most versions, but some perform a half turn.
- Some versions include a throw explicitly, with a drop to one knee.
- The order of the final sections and the kiai point differ between lineages.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Seipai**

*Typical grade*: nidan or sandan in many Okinawan-line associations.

*What the examiners look for*:

- Correct diagonal steps back at the opening.
- A seamless change from slow to fast in counts 2 to 4.
- Strong shiko dachi throughout the opening.
- A controlled drop into kōsa dachi.
- A balanced, complete spin with the head leading.
- A powerful landing and punch after the stamp.
- An overall rhythm that is varied but unified.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: pause between slow and fast; unbalanced spin; drop and rise disconnected; rhythm monotonous.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Walk the embusen. Practise the opening five times on each side, on one breath. Spin drill: ten spins each direction.

**Week two.** Sections three and four, five times each. Kōsa dachi drill: ten drops and rises.

**Week three.** Whole kata five times. Concentrate on the rhythm.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience. Bunkai of the opening with a partner.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Seipai, "eighteen", is a kata of contrasts: slow and fast, high and low, soft and hard.
- The opening steps back off the line and catches the attack slowly, then strikes suddenly, on one breath.
- The drop into kōsa dachi and the full spin are its technical challenges. Practise them separately.
- The breath unifies the kata. Make it strong and consistent.
:::

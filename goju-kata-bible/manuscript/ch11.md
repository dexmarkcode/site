# Suparinpei

::: chapter-intro
Suparinpei, "one hundred and eight", is the longest and most complete kata in Gōjū-ryū. It begins like Sanchin, turns to the four diagonals in shiko dachi, kicks and strikes to the front and back, drops, rises, stamps, scoops and hooks, and finishes with two mawashi uke. It contains, in one form or another, nearly every principle of the system. It is the kata of the senior grades, and many karateka spend the rest of their lives with it.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Suparinpei* means one hundred and eight. The kata is also known as *Pechurin*, a different reading of the characters. One hundred and eight is a deeply significant number in Buddhism: there are said to be one hundred and eight earthly desires or defilements, and Buddhist rosaries traditionally carry one hundred and eight beads. Temple bells in Japan are rung one hundred and eight times at the new year. Some teachers connect the name of the kata with this symbolism, seeing the practice of Suparinpei as a way of purifying the mind. Others note that 108 is three times thirty-six, linking it with Sanseiru, and three times eighteen times two, linking it with Seipai. As with the other numbered kata, it is wise not to count techniques looking for 108.

Suparinpei is traditionally attributed to Higaonna Kanryō and regarded as the most advanced kata of the system. In many Okinawan-line associations it is reserved for the senior dan grades, often yondan or above, though some teach it earlier.

## Character of the kata

Suparinpei has the character of a summary. It is long, it is heavy, and it covers the whole range of the system.

**The opening** is closely modelled on Sanchin, with three slow steps forward in sanchin dachi performing double spear hands with dynamic tension and breathing, followed by steps back.

**The diagonals** turn to all four corners in shiko dachi with side elbow strikes, back-fists and downward blocks, recalling Seiyunchin.

**The front and back sections** lift the knee with a double block, kick and strike, recalling Saifa and Gekisai.

**The low section** drops into kōsa dachi and rises into neko ashi dachi with a stamp, recalling Seipai and Kururunfa.

**The end** turns to the sides with hooks and palms, recalling Shisochin, and finishes with a double punch and two mawashi uke.

To perform Suparinpei well, therefore, you need to have digested all the earlier kata. It is not a kata to learn early, however tempting.

## Before you begin

You should be confident in every kata in this book and in Sanchin and Tenshō. Suparinpei also demands stamina. A full performance can take two and a half to three minutes, much of it in low stances with tension. Make sure your fitness is equal to it.

## The embusen

Suparinpei moves forward and back on the centre line, turns to all four diagonals, moves forward and back again, branches to the left and right and finishes by stepping back twice.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow, kaishu yōi, step out to heiko dachi with open hands (count 1).

### Section one: Sanchin opening (counts 2 to 7)

**Count 2.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and bring both open hands into kaishu morote chūdan uke. Breathe in.

**Count 3.** Slowly thrust both hands forward in *morote nukite*, a double spear hand, with dynamic tension, breathing out.

**Count 4.** Step forward with the left foot into left sanchin dachi and repeat the slow double nukite.

**Count 5.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and repeat.

**Count 6.** Step back into left sanchin dachi and push quickly with both palms (*morote shōtei*).

**Count 7.** Step back into right sanchin dachi and perform *morote kake uke*, a double hooking block.

### Section two: the four diagonals (counts 8 to 17)

**Counts 8 to 10.** Turn to the north-east into shiko dachi. Right yoko empi; right uraken; right gedan barai.

**Counts 11 to 13.** Turn to the north-west into shiko dachi. Left yoko empi; left uraken; left gedan barai.

**Counts 14 and 15.** Turn to the south-east into shiko dachi. Right yoko empi; right uraken and gedan barai.

**Counts 16 and 17.** Turn to the south-west into shiko dachi. Left yoko empi; left uraken and gedan barai.

This section is long and very demanding on the legs. Each turn must be accurate and each shiko dachi as low and strong as the first.

### Section three: front and back (counts 18 to 27)

**Count 18.** Turn to face north and lift the right knee, both arms in chūdan uke.

**Count 19.** Right mae geri.

**Count 20.** Land in right zenkutsu dachi with right empi.

**Count 21.** Right uraken.

**Count 22.** Left gyaku tsuki.

**Counts 23 to 27.** Turn to face south and repeat on the other side: left knee lift with double block; left mae geri; left zenkutsu dachi with left empi; left uraken; right gyaku tsuki.

### Section four: low and high (counts 28 to 32)

**Count 28.** Turn to face north and drop into kōsa dachi with left gedan tettsui.

**Count 29.** Rise into left neko ashi dachi with left kake uke.

**Count 30.** Right kansetsu geri.

**Count 31.** Land in right zenkutsu dachi with right sukui uke.

**Count 32.** Turn into shiko dachi facing west with right tettsui.

### Section five: the sides and the finish (counts 33 to 39)

**Count 33.** Turn to face west into left zenkutsu dachi with left kake uke.

**Count 34.** Right shōtei.

**Count 35.** Turn to face east into right zenkutsu dachi with right kake uke.

**Count 36.** Left shōtei.

**Count 37.** Turn to face north into right sanchin dachi and punch with both fists, *morote tsuki*, with a kiai.

**Count 38.** Step back into left neko ashi dachi with mawashi uke.

**Count 39.** Step back into right neko ashi dachi with mawashi uke.

### Closing

**Count 40.** Musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Suparinpei's rhythm is that of a long journey with several distinct stages.

- **Section one** is slow and heavy, with Sanchin breathing, ending in two quicker movements.
- **Section two** is powerful and deliberate, each diagonal a short burst followed by a turn.
- **Section three** is fast and athletic.
- **Section four** is sudden: drop, rise, stamp.
- **Section five** is crisp, building to the double punch and kiai, then slow and strong in the two mawashi uke.

The greatest challenge is pacing. Students who give everything in section one have nothing left for section five. Experienced performers save a little in the middle sections so that the finish is as strong as the start. Typical duration: two and a half to three minutes.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### Double nukite (counts 2 to 5)

As in Shisochin, the slow spear hands can be understood as both a conditioning method and a reaching, grasping action. With both hands, the action becomes a reach for the opponent's collar, both arms or both sides of the neck. Combined with the rooted advance in sanchin dachi, it drives the opponent backwards.

![**Figure 11.1** Suparinpei, count 3: the double nukite as a two-handed reach into the partner's guard, driving forward from sanchin dachi.](figures/bk_suparinpei_1.png)

### Knee lift and double block (counts 18 and 19)

Your partner kicks and punches together. The raised knee covers the kick; the double block covers the punch. The kick follows from the raised knee before they can recover.

![**Figure 11.2** Suparinpei, count 18: the raised knee and double block together cover an attack to two levels.](figures/bk_suparinpei_2.png)

### Hook and palm to the sides (counts 33 to 36)

Each side is a complete encounter: catch the punch with kake uke, draw the opponent across you, strike with the palm to the face or jaw. Performed with two partners, one to each side, this is a good exercise in turning quickly and finding the second attacker.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: running out of energy.** *Cause*: poor pacing, or insufficient fitness. *Solution*: train the kata in sections, then in halves, then whole. Add general fitness training. Pace the middle sections.

**Problem: the diagonals in section two drift.** *Cause*: fatigue and inaccurate turns. *Solution*: tape the four corners. Practise section two alone, ten times, checking each angle.

**Problem: the opening is weaker than the student's Sanchin.** *Cause*: the student treats it as a preamble. *Solution*: the opening is Sanchin. Perform it with the same intensity.

**Problem: forgetting the order of the middle sections.** *Cause*: the kata is long and the sections resemble one another. *Solution*: give each section a name ("Sanchin", "corners", "front and back", "low and high", "sides") and learn the names in order before learning the techniques.

**Problem: the final mawashi uke are weak.** *Cause*: fatigue. *Solution*: practise the end of the kata first, when you are fresh, and practise it again at the end of hard sessions, when you are tired.

## Deeper study: Suparinpei as a map of the system

Suparinpei is often described as the summary of Gōjū-ryū. It is worth taking that description literally and using the kata as a map, because doing so will help you learn it, remember it and understand it.

Look at the sections again with the earlier kata in mind.

- **Section one** is Sanchin with open hands: three steps forward in sanchin dachi with slow, tense double spear hands, then two quicker movements stepping back. Anyone who has studied Shisochin's opening will recognise the idea, now performed with both hands at once.
- **Section two** turns to all four diagonals in shiko dachi with side elbows, back-fists and downward blocks. This is the language of Seiyunchin and of the middle of Gekisai, extended to four directions.
- **Section three** lifts the knee with a double block, kicks and strikes to the front and then the back. This is Saifa's knee-and-kick sequence combined with Gekisai's elbow, back-fist and reverse punch.
- **Section four** drops into kōsa dachi with a hammer fist, rises into neko ashi dachi with a hook and a stamp, and scoops a leg. Here are Seipai's drop and Kururunfa's stamp and scoop.
- **Section five** hooks and strikes with the palm to each side, recalling Shisochin, and ends with a double punch from sanchin dachi and two mawashi uke, the closing signature of every kaishu kata.

If you already know the other kata, then, you already know most of the techniques of Suparinpei. What is new is their combination, their order and the stamina needed to perform them all in one piece. This is one reason why Suparinpei is taught last: it assumes everything else.

### Pacing a long kata

Suparinpei is long enough that pacing becomes a real issue. A full performance takes two and a half to three minutes, much of it in low stances with tension. Students who attack the opening with everything they have often find that by section four their legs are failing and their breath is ragged.

Experienced performers pace the kata as a distance runner paces a race. The opening is performed with full Sanchin intensity, because it sets the tone and the examiners watch it closely. Section two is strong but economical: the shiko dachi are low and accurate, but the muscles are not braced more than necessary. Section three is fast and athletic but brief. Section four is sudden. And section five is given whatever is left, which should be plenty.

Training for this is partly a matter of fitness and partly a matter of rehearsal. Practise the kata whole at least twice a week once you know it, so that your body learns the rhythm of effort. And practise the last section first, when you are fresh, so that it is as well known as the opening.

### The number 108

We discussed the meaning of 108 at the start of the chapter. One further thought is worth adding. In Buddhist practice, the 108 beads of a rosary are counted one by one during repetitive prayer or meditation. Whatever the original reason for the name of the kata, Suparinpei rewards a similar attitude: patient, repeated practice, one count at a time, over many years. It is not a kata to be mastered quickly. It is a kata to live with.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: double reach.** Your partner stands in sanchin dachi with a firm guard. Step forward in sanchin dachi and slowly slide both hands forward between their arms in double nukite, breathing out. Feel their guard open. Five repetitions.

**Drill 2: four-corner response.** Four partners stand at the four diagonals. A coach calls a direction at random; you turn into shiko dachi to face it and deliver a side elbow to a pad. Ten calls.

**Drill 3: knee and double block.** Your partner throws a slow front kick and a slow punch together. Lift the knee to meet the kick and double block the punch. Then reset. Ten repetitions.

**Drill 4: the stamina drill.** Perform Suparinpei three times in succession with only a minute's rest between performances. Note where your form begins to fail. Work on that section in the next session.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 11.1: the fading finish**

*The problem.* A senior candidate gave a superb opening to Suparinpei but was visibly exhausted by the final section, with high stances and a weak kiai.

*The diagnosis.* Poor pacing and insufficient specific stamina. The candidate practised the kata mostly in sections and rarely performed it whole.

*The solution.* Whole performances twice a week, including the stamina drill, and practising section five first in every session. The candidate also learned to hold a little back in section two. At the next grading, the finish was as strong as the start.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 11.2: the lost middle**

*The problem.* A student repeatedly confused section three and section four, performing the drop into kōsa dachi too early.

*The diagnosis.* The sections had been learned as a list of techniques without a clear sense of their structure.

*The solution.* The student named the sections ("Sanchin", "corners", "front and back", "low and high", "sides") and walked through the kata saying the names aloud before each section. Within a week the confusion had gone.
:::

## Self-test

1. What does Suparinpei mean, and what is its other name?
2. Which kata does the opening of Suparinpei most resemble?
3. How many diagonals does section two visit, and in what stance?
4. Which earlier kata does section three recall?
5. What happens at count 28?
6. What is the kiai technique in this book's version?
7. How many mawashi uke close the kata?
8. Roughly how long does a full performance take?
9. Why is pacing so important in Suparinpei?
10. What is a good way to make sure the finish is as well known as the opening?

*Answers*: (1) one hundred and eight; Pechurin; (2) Sanchin (and the opening of Shisochin); (3) four, in shiko dachi; (4) Saifa and Gekisai; (5) a drop into kōsa dachi with a downward hammer fist; (6) a double punch from sanchin dachi; (7) two; (8) two and a half to three minutes; (9) because the kata is long and demanding, and the finish must be as strong as the start; (10) practise the last section first, when fresh.

## Variations between associations

Suparinpei varies a great deal, as one might expect of the longest kata in the system. Some common differences:

- The opening nukite may be performed with one hand at a time rather than both together.
- The number and direction of the diagonal turns vary, and some versions include a turn to the rear.
- Some versions include additional kicks, throws or a kneeling posture.
- The order of the middle sections varies considerably.
- The number of mawashi uke at the end varies between one and two.

As always, follow your teacher.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Suparinpei**

*Typical grade*: senior dan grades, often yondan or above.

*What the examiners look for*:

- A Sanchin-quality opening.
- Accurate, low, consistent shiko dachi on all four diagonals.
- Fast, athletic front-and-back sections.
- A controlled drop and rise.
- A strong finish, as strong as the start.
- Overall, the impression of a complete karateka: stance, breath, rhythm, power, softness and understanding all in one performance.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: fading energy; drifting angles; weak finish; sections performed as separate kata rather than one whole.
:::

## A four-week training plan (for consolidation)

Suparinpei takes months or years to learn properly. This plan is for polishing it once the sequence is known.

**Week one.** Sections one and two, five times each. Sanchin three times.

**Week two.** Sections three and four, five times each. Fitness: three sessions of interval training.

**Week three.** Section five first, five times, then the whole kata three times.

**Week four.** Whole kata in front of an audience, three times. Bunkai of two sections with a partner.

### Key points

- Suparinpei, "one hundred and eight", is the longest and most complete Gōjū kata.
- It summarises the whole system: Sanchin, Seiyunchin, Saifa, Seipai, Kururunfa and Shisochin can all be found inside it.
- Pacing is the great challenge. The finish must be as strong as the start.
- Name the sections and learn their order before you learn the techniques.

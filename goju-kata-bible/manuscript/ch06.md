# Seiyunchin

::: chapter-intro
Seiyunchin is the kata of the low stance. It is performed almost entirely in shiko dachi, it contains no kicks, and it begins with long, slow, breathing movements that test the legs and the lungs together. It is a kata about pulling, gripping, unbalancing and striking from a deeply rooted base, and it is the place where many students first understand what Gōjū-ryū means by power.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

The name *Seiyunchin* is written in several ways, and its meaning has been rendered as "control, pull off balance and fight", "attack, conquer and suppress", or "storm within the calm". None of these translations is certain, and the characters themselves are read differently in different sources. What is certain is that the various interpretations agree on the content of the kata: control of an opponent, unbalancing and pulling, and striking from a stable base.

Seiyunchin is usually attributed to Miyagi Chōjun's teaching, and some accounts suggest it was among the kata he learned or developed after his own studies in China. Like Saifa, it is found in Shitō-ryū (as Seienchin) and in other Naha-te derived styles, with considerable differences.

In many Okinawan-line syllabuses, Seiyunchin is required for the senior brown-belt grades, around third or second kyū. Some associations teach it before Saifa; others after Shisochin. Its place in your own syllabus will depend on your association.

## Character of the kata

Seiyunchin has three distinctive features.

**First, the stance.** Most of the kata is performed in shiko dachi, often held for long periods. This makes it one of the most physically demanding kata in the system for the legs, and one of the most revealing of weak stances. A student with collapsing knees or a forward-leaning spine cannot hide it in Seiyunchin.

**Second, the opening.** The kata begins with three slow, breathing sequences on the diagonals, each consisting of a double downward block, a grasping hand that rises, a pull and a short punch. They are performed with deliberate tension and ibuki-style breathing, though not usually as strongly as in Sanchin.

**Third, the absence of kicks.** Seiyunchin is one of the few kata in any karate style with no kicks at all. Its techniques are entirely of the hands, the arms, the elbows and the body weight. This makes it an excellent study of close-range grappling and striking, and a reminder that karate is not about kicking.

The overall rhythm is slow at the start, faster in the middle, and then sharp and sudden towards the end.

## Before you begin

You need a strong, comfortable shiko dachi above all. Before learning Seiyunchin, you should be able to hold shiko dachi with correct structure for at least two minutes. If you cannot, spend a month on the stance routine in Chapter 2 first.

You should also be comfortable with:

- turning in shiko dachi from one diagonal to the other;
- slow, controlled breathing with tension;
- hiki uke (pulling block) and ura tsuki or a short chūdan punch;
- nukite (spear hand);
- yoko empi (side elbow strike), uraken uchi and gedan barai;
- kōsa dachi, the crossed stance;
- mawashi uke in neko ashi dachi.

## The embusen

The embusen of Seiyunchin opens with a zig-zag on the diagonals, then moves straight forward, with the shiko-dachi sections facing alternately left and right, before turning back and finishing near the start.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow in musubi dachi and take kaishu yōi. Step out into heiko dachi with the open hands in front of the body (count 1).

### Section one: the three slow diagonals (counts 2 to 11)

**Count 2.** Step the right foot out to the north-east into shiko dachi facing north-east. As you sink, bring both open hands up and cross them in front of the chest, breathing in slowly through the nose.

**Count 3.** Slowly push both hands down and apart, open, in a double downward block (*morote gedan uke*), finishing with the hands above the knees. Breathe out slowly and strongly. The whole body tenses as the hands descend.

**Count 4.** Slowly raise the left hand in front of the body in hiki uke, fingers hooked, as if catching an arm, while the right fist draws to the right hip.

**Count 5.** Pull the left hand sharply back to the hip, as if dragging the caught arm towards you, and at the same moment punch right chūdan tsuki. This is the first fast movement of the kata, and its suddenness after three slow counts is part of its character.

**Counts 6 to 9.** Step the left foot out to the north-west into shiko dachi and repeat the sequence on the other side: arms cross; slow double downward block; slow right hiki uke with the left fist at the hip; sharp pull and left punch.

**Counts 10 and 11.** Step the right foot out to the north-east again and repeat the sequence a third time. In my count sheet I have combined the four parts into two counts for this third repetition, because by now you know it: count 10 is the crossing and slow double block, count 11 is the hiki uke, pull and punch.

These ten counts can take a full minute, and they should. Do not rush them. They are where the examiners judge your stance, your breath and your composure.

### Section two: forward to the left (counts 12 to 15)

**Count 12.** Step forward with the left foot into left zenkutsu dachi facing north and thrust left nukite, a spear hand, forward. This is fast, the first truly fast forward movement of the kata.

**Count 13.** Turn the hips into shiko dachi facing west, so that your right side is towards the north, and drive right yoko empi, a side elbow strike, to the north. In application, the nukite has caught or struck the opponent, the left hand pulls them in and the elbow meets them as they come.

**Count 14.** Right uraken uchi to the north.

**Count 15.** Right gedan barai to the north.

### Section three: forward to the right (counts 16 to 19)

**Count 16.** Step forward with the right foot into right zenkutsu dachi and thrust right nukite.

**Count 17.** Turn into shiko dachi facing east, with left yoko empi to the north.

**Count 18.** Left uraken uchi.

**Count 19.** Left gedan barai, with a kiai.

### Section four: the drop and the turn (counts 20 to 22)

**Count 20.** Turn to face south, dropping into kōsa dachi, the crossed stance. The left hand presses down (*osae*) and the right fist strikes downward in a short punch. The body drops suddenly, as if following an opponent to the floor.

**Count 21.** Rise into shiko dachi, the left side forward, and perform left chūdan uchi uke.

**Count 22.** Punch right chūdan tsuki.

### Section five: mawashi uke (count 23)

**Count 23.** Turn to face north, stepping into left neko ashi dachi, and perform mawashi uke with the double palm push.

### Closing

**Count 24.** Draw back into musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Seiyunchin has the most varied rhythm of any kata we have yet met.

- **Section one** is slow and breathing. Each diagonal takes around fifteen to twenty seconds: a slow in-breath as the arms cross, a long out-breath with tension as the hands descend, a slow in-breath as the grasping hand rises, then a short, sharp out-breath on the pull and punch.
- **Sections two and three** are fast and continuous. Each is one burst of four techniques, on two or three short out-breaths.
- **Section four** is sudden. The drop into kōsa dachi should surprise anyone watching.
- **Section five** is slow and strong, as always.

A complete Seiyunchin usually takes between one and a half and two minutes.

A note on tension in section one. In Sanchin, the dynamic tension is total and continuous. In the opening of Seiyunchin, it should be firm but not rigid, and the stance should remain alive. Some teachers describe it as "Sanchin tension in the arms and torso, shiko dachi structure in the legs". If your legs are shaking violently by the third diagonal, you are probably locking them with tension rather than holding them with structure.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### The slow diagonal (counts 2 to 5)

Your partner pushes you, or grabs your belt or clothing at the waist with both hands. You drop into shiko dachi, lowering your centre of gravity so that you cannot easily be moved, and your crossed arms come up between theirs. The double downward block drives their arms apart and down, breaking their grip. Your left hand then catches one of their arms in hiki uke, pulls it sharply towards you, and your right fist strikes their body as they are pulled in.

The lesson of this sequence is that stance is a technique. The drop into shiko dachi does as much to defeat the push as anything the hands do.

![**Figure 6.1** Seiyunchin, count 3: dropping into shiko dachi against a low attack, the double downward block drives the partner's arms apart.](figures/bk_seiyunchin_1.png)

### Spear hand, pull and elbow (counts 12 to 15)

Your partner is close in front of you. Thrust the spear hand to the throat or solar plexus, or use it to reach past them and grasp their collar or the back of their neck. Pull them towards you as you turn into shiko dachi, so that their head meets your side elbow. Follow with the back-fist and then the downward strike.

This is a good example of how a Gōjū kata hides a grappling technique inside a striking sequence. Performed alone, the nukite, elbow and back-fist look like three separate strikes. Performed with a partner, the nukite becomes a grip, and the turn into shiko dachi becomes a pull.

![**Figure 6.2** Seiyunchin, count 13: having caught the partner with the left hand, the turn into shiko dachi pulls them onto the side elbow.](figures/bk_seiyunchin_2.png)

### The drop into kōsa dachi (count 20)

You have pulled or thrown your partner to the ground in front of you. You follow them down into kōsa dachi, pressing them with your left hand and striking downward with your right. The crossed stance lets you drop very quickly while staying balanced and mobile.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: legs shaking or giving out in section one.** *Cause*: the stance is held with tension rather than structure, or the legs simply are not yet strong enough. *Solution*: practise shiko dachi daily as in Chapter 2. During the kata, keep the knees out over the toes and the weight in the heels, and relax the thigh muscles as much as the stance allows.

**Problem: leaning forward in shiko dachi.** *Cause*: tight hips, or looking at the floor. *Solution*: keep the eyes level and the spine vertical. Practise with your back against a wall.

**Problem: the slow movements look like slow-motion karate rather than breathing techniques.** *Cause*: the movement is slow but the breath and body are not connected to it. *Solution*: practise breathing the section without moving the arms, feeling the tension build and release. Then add the arms, letting them follow the breath.

**Problem: the pull and punch at count 5 are not simultaneous.** *Cause*: the student pulls, then punches. *Solution*: in application, the pull brings the opponent onto the punch, so the two must arrive together. Practise with a partner's arm or a belt tied to something solid.

**Problem: the diagonals drift.** *Cause*: as in Saifa, guessing the angles. *Solution*: tape the corners and check.

**Problem: the nukite in sections two and three is weak.** *Cause*: it is thrown with the arm alone from a long stance. *Solution*: drive it from the back leg, and think of it as reaching for something to grab rather than poking at the air.

**Problem: the drop into kōsa dachi is unbalanced.** *Cause*: dropping too far or with the feet too close together. *Solution*: practise the stance on its own, rising and dropping, until it is stable. The rear knee should tuck behind the front knee, not touch the floor.

## Deeper study: the stance as a weapon

Seiyunchin is the clearest demonstration in the syllabus of an idea that runs through all of Gōjū-ryū: that a stance is not something you stand in while doing techniques, but a technique in its own right. To understand why, you need a little physics.

When someone pushes or pulls you, they are trying to move your centre of mass outside your base of support. Your base of support is the area enclosed by your feet. Your centre of mass sits roughly behind the navel. The higher your centre of mass and the smaller your base, the less force it takes to move one outside the other, and the more easily you fall. Shiko dachi does the opposite of both. It lowers the centre of mass by bending the knees deeply, and it widens the base by placing the feet roughly twice shoulder width apart. A student in a good shiko dachi is very hard to move sideways and surprisingly hard to move forward or back.

But Seiyunchin goes further than simple stability. Watch the opening sequence again. You do not stand in shiko dachi and wait; you step into it diagonally, dropping as you arrive. That drop is the technique. If someone has hold of your belt or clothing, a sudden drop of your weight pulls them forward and down, breaking their posture before your hands have done anything. The double downward block that follows is then working on an opponent who is already off balance.

The same principle reappears in the middle of the kata, when you turn from zenkutsu dachi into shiko dachi with the side elbow. The turn and the drop together pull the opponent onto the elbow. And it reappears at the end, in the sudden drop into kōsa dachi. Three times in one kata, the body weight is used as a tool.

### Tension without rigidity

The slow opening of Seiyunchin is where many students first meet the problem of tension. Asked to perform the double downward block slowly and strongly, they tense every muscle in the body, hold their breath, go red in the face and shake. This is not what the kata asks for.

Good tension in Seiyunchin is selective. The legs hold the shape of shiko dachi through structure: knees over toes, weight in the heels, pelvis tucked. The muscles that hold the stance work, but no more than necessary. The abdomen is firm and the breath is controlled, long and audible but not forced. The arms and shoulders are firm as they press down, but the neck and face are relaxed. A useful image is of pressing two heavy objects slowly down into water: strong, steady, but without strain.

If you find yourself shaking violently, you are probably bracing the thighs instead of letting the skeleton carry the weight. Check that your knees are over your toes, not inside them, and that your spine is vertical. A vertical spine stacks the weight of the upper body onto the pelvis and the legs, so that the muscles have less to do.

### Why no kicks?

Seiyunchin's lack of kicks is often remarked upon. The simplest explanation is that the kata is concerned with a range and a situation in which kicking would be a mistake: close grappling, where lifting a foot would only make you easier to throw. The whole kata assumes that you and your opponent are already in contact. In that situation the legs are needed for rooting, dropping and turning, and the hands, elbows and body weight do the striking.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: the immovable stance.** Stand in shiko dachi. Your partner pushes you steadily from the front, then the side, then pulls you by the belt. Your job is simply to stay where you are, adjusting the stance slightly as the direction changes. Then repeat in heiko dachi and notice the difference. Ten pushes each direction.

**Drill 2: the drop.** Your partner holds your belt with both hands. Step diagonally into shiko dachi, dropping sharply as you land. Feel your partner pulled forward and down. Cooperative at first, gradually adding resistance.

**Drill 3: catch and pull.** Your partner punches slowly to your body. Catch the arm with hiki uke, pull it to your hip and punch with the other hand, stopping short. Ten each side. Concentrate on the pull and the punch arriving together.

**Drill 4: grip, pull and elbow.** Your partner faces you. Reach with the left hand to take hold of their collar or shoulder (the nukite of count 12), then turn into shiko dachi, drawing them towards your right elbow, which stops short. Slowly at first.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 6.1: the shaking legs**

*The problem.* A second-kyū student could perform Seiyunchin accurately but was visibly trembling by the third diagonal and stood up out of the stance in the fast middle section.

*The diagnosis.* The student was holding the stance through muscle tension rather than structure. The knees were slightly inside the toes, the bottom pushed back and the breath held during the slow movements.

*The solution.* The instructor took the student back to daily shiko dachi practice, four sets of one minute, checking knee alignment against a mirror. During the kata, the student was asked to exhale fully on each slow movement and to sink deliberately as the hands descended. After a month, the trembling had stopped and the stance stayed low to the end.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 6.2: the slow-motion karateka**

*The problem.* A student's opening sequence was slow, but looked like ordinary karate in slow motion rather than a breathing exercise.

*The diagnosis.* The breath and the movement were not linked. The student breathed whenever necessary rather than in a set pattern.

*The solution.* Breath-only practice. The student stood in shiko dachi and breathed the opening without moving the arms: slow in-breath for the crossing, long out-breath with tension for the descent, slow in-breath for the rising hand, sharp out-breath for the pull and punch. After a week the arms were added, following the breath. The difference was immediately visible.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 6.3: the lost diagonals**

*The problem.* A student consistently performed the second diagonal at nearly ninety degrees to the first, so that the opening looked lopsided.

*The diagnosis.* The student was stepping out with the left foot to the side rather than to the corner.

*The solution.* Tape marks at the two front corners and practice of the stepping pattern alone, ten times each session, with the chest facing each corner squarely.
:::

## Self-test

1. In what stance is most of Seiyunchin performed?
2. What notable kind of technique does Seiyunchin not contain?
3. How many slow diagonal sequences open the kata, and in which directions?
4. What happens in the slow downward movement of the opening?
5. Which two actions must arrive together at the end of each opening sequence?
6. What is the nukite of count 12 often understood to be doing in application?
7. Which three techniques follow the nukite, and from which stance?
8. What happens at count 20?
9. What is the difference between tension and rigidity in the opening?
10. Roughly how long should a good performance take?

*Answers*: (1) shiko dachi; (2) kicks; (3) three, north-east, north-west and north-east; (4) a slow double downward block with open hands, with tension and breath; (5) the pull and the punch; (6) reaching to take hold of the opponent; (7) yoko empi, uraken and gedan barai, from shiko dachi; (8) a sudden drop into kōsa dachi with a press and a downward strike; (9) tension is selective and released; rigidity is total and held; (10) one and a half to two minutes.

## Variations between associations

- The number of slow diagonals in the opening is three in most versions, but the direction of the third varies.
- The hand action in the opening varies considerably. Some lineages perform the rising hand as kake uke; some include a short upward punch (*ura tsuki*); some add a strike after the pull.
- In some versions, sections two and three are performed in shiko dachi throughout rather than beginning in zenkutsu dachi.
- The ending varies. Some versions include a throwing action and a drop to one knee, or a different sequence in kōsa dachi.
- Seienchin in Shitō-ryū is considerably longer and contains different techniques in its middle section.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Seiyunchin**

*Typical grade*: senior kyū grades (often third or second kyū); sometimes later.

*What the examiners look for*:

- A strong, correct shiko dachi held throughout: knees out, spine vertical, weight low.
- Accurate diagonals in section one.
- Breathing coordinated with the slow movements, audible but not forced.
- A clear contrast between slow sections and fast bursts.
- Pull and punch arriving together.
- Hips driving the elbow, back-fist and downward block.
- A sudden, balanced drop into kōsa dachi.
- Composure: the long opening tests nerves as much as legs.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: collapsing stance; visible exhaustion; rushed opening; disconnected pulls and strikes.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Shiko dachi, every day: four sets of one minute. Walk the embusen. Practise section one alone, slowly, three times.

**Week two.** Add sections two and three. Practise each fast section ten times. Continue the stance work.

**Week three.** Whole kata, five times each session. Concentrate on the transition from slow to fast. Practise the drop into kōsa dachi in isolation.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience. Practise the bunkai of section one and section two with a partner who pushes and resists.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Seiyunchin is the kata of shiko dachi and has no kicks.
- Its opening is three slow, breathing diagonals: cross, double downward block, grasp, pull and punch.
- The middle sections hide a grip and pull inside the nukite, elbow, back-fist and downward block.
- Stance is a technique: the drop into shiko dachi defeats a push before the hands do anything.
- Keep the knees out, the spine upright and the rhythm varied.
:::

# Seisan

::: chapter-intro
Seisan, "thirteen", is one of the oldest and most widely practised kata in Okinawan karate. Versions of it are found in Gōjū-ryū, Uechi-ryū, Shōrin-ryū, Isshin-ryū and many other styles. The Gōjū version opens like Sanchin with blocks and punches stepping forward in sanchin dachi, bursts into a rapid chain of punches, attacks the diagonals with knee strikes, stamps through the knee and finishes with a rising elbow. It is fast, close and relentless, and it is the kata in which many students first feel what fighting at Gōjū range is really like.
:::

## Name, meaning and origins

*Seisan* means thirteen, read in a southern Chinese pronunciation. Why thirteen? Several explanations are offered. Some say the kata originally contained thirteen key techniques; some that it represents thirteen hands or thirteen moments of attack and defence; some that it relates to Buddhist numerology, in which multiples and combinations of three are significant. The truth is that no one knows. I prefer to treat the number as a name, like the number of a house, rather than as a puzzle to be solved by counting.

Seisan is probably the most widely distributed kata in Okinawan karate. Its presence in styles descended from both Naha-te and Shuri-te suggests that it is very old, and that it was known in Okinawa before the styles divided. The Gōjū-ryū version is traditionally attributed to Higaonna Kanryō, who is said to have brought it from his studies in China.

In many Okinawan-line associations Seisan is a shodan or nidan kata. In some, it is taught earlier, at the brown-belt grades.

## Character of the kata

Seisan has four distinguishing features.

**The Sanchin opening.** Like Sanchin, Seisan begins by stepping forward three times in sanchin dachi with blocks and punches. Unlike Sanchin, it is performed at a moderate speed, with natural breathing rather than full ibuki.

**The chain punch.** At the end of the opening comes a rapid chain of punches, *renzuki*, delivered from a stationary sanchin dachi. It is one of the most exciting moments in any Gōjū kata and one of the most often done badly.

**The knees and the stamps.** Seisan uses the knee as a weapon (*hiza geri*), and it uses kansetsu geri, the stamping kick to the knee joint that we met in Shisochin.

**Close range throughout.** Almost every technique in Seisan works at arm's length or less. It is a kata for the clinch.

## Before you begin

You should have a sound Sanchin and be comfortable with everything in Shisochin. Particular skills for Seisan are:

- moving and turning in sanchin dachi at moderate speed;
- chūdan uke and chūdan tsuki from sanchin dachi, alternating smoothly;
- rapid punching from a fixed stance without losing structure;
- hiza geri, a knee strike, with both hands pulling down;
- kansetsu geri;
- age empi (rising elbow strike) and uraken uchi.

## The embusen

The embusen of Seisan runs forward in a straight line, turns back, branches out on the two rear diagonals, returns to the centre line and comes forward again before stepping back to finish.

## The sequence, count by count

### Opening (count 1)

Bow, kaishu yōi, step out to heiko dachi with fists (count 1).

### Section one: forward in sanchin dachi (counts 2 to 8)

**Count 2.** Step forward with the right foot into right sanchin dachi and bring both arms up into *morote chūdan uke*, a double middle block, as in the opening of Sanchin.

**Count 3.** Punch right chūdan tsuki, the left arm staying in chūdan uke.

**Count 4.** Draw the right fist back to the hip and bring the left arm into chūdan uke.

**Count 5.** Step forward into left sanchin dachi and punch left chūdan tsuki.

**Count 6.** Step forward into right sanchin dachi and punch right chūdan tsuki.

**Count 7.** From the fixed stance, punch rapidly: left, right, left, right. This is the renzuki. Every punch is a complete punch, with a hikite, but the punches overlap, each one starting as the previous one returns.

**Count 8.** Perform right kake uke, the open hand hooking, and follow with a spear-hand or open-hand strike.

### Section two: turning back (counts 9 and 10)

**Count 9.** Turn round to face south into left sanchin dachi with left chūdan uke.

**Count 10.** Punch right chūdan tsuki.

### Section three: the rear diagonals (counts 11 to 14)

**Count 11.** Step to the south-east into right zenkutsu dachi and perform right gedan barai.

**Count 12.** Reach out with both hands as if grasping the head or shoulders, and pull them down as you drive the right knee up in hiza geri.

**Counts 13 and 14.** Turn to the south-west into left zenkutsu dachi with left gedan barai, then reach, pull down and strike with the left knee.

### Section four: forward again (counts 15 to 19)

**Count 15.** Turn to face north into left sanchin dachi with left kake uke.

**Count 16.** Holding the hook, stamp right kansetsu geri.

**Count 17.** Land forward into right zenkutsu dachi and strike upward with right age empi, a rising elbow.

**Count 18.** Snap right uraken uchi.

**Count 19.** Turn into shiko dachi facing west and perform right gedan barai, with a kiai.

### Section five: mawashi uke (count 20)

**Count 20.** Step back into left neko ashi dachi and perform mawashi uke with the double push.

### Closing

**Count 21.** Musubi dachi, zanshin and bow.

## Breathing, rhythm and timing

Seisan should feel steady and powerful in its first section, explosive in the renzuki, sharp and fast in the diagonals, and decisive at the end.

- **Section one**: moderate speed, a natural out-breath on each technique. Not Sanchin breathing, but Sanchin structure.
- **Count 7**: the renzuki should be performed on one or two short out-breaths. It should be fast, but every punch must be distinct. Four blurred half-punches are worse than four slower, complete ones.
- **Section three**: each diagonal is a burst of two techniques: block and knee.
- **Section four**: the hook, stamp, elbow, back-fist and downward block flow together as one long combination ending in the kiai.

A typical Seisan takes between one minute and one and a half minutes.

## Bunkai: applying the movements

### Double block and punch (counts 2 and 3)

Your partner grabs your lapels with both hands or pushes you. Your double chūdan uke rises inside their arms, spreading them, and your punch drives into the body through the gap. The block and the punch work as one: the left arm stays in place, holding their right arm out of the way, while the right arm strikes.

![**Figure 8.1** Seisan, counts 2 and 3: the double block opens the partner's arms; the punch follows through the gap while the other arm stays in place.](figures/bk_seisan_1.png)

### The renzuki (count 7)

The rapid punches are often explained as a continuous attack to the body of an opponent who is already off balance. A more interesting application sees each punch as a strike-and-clear: one hand clears or traps the opponent's guard while the other strikes, then the roles swap. Practise slowly with a partner holding their hands up in guard: punch with the right while the left moves their guard aside, then the reverse.

### Pull and knee (counts 11 and 12)

Your partner kicks or punches low; you clear it with gedan barai. As they come forward, reach up, take hold of their head or shoulders with both hands and pull them down onto your rising knee. In training, the knee stops short and the pull is gentle.

![**Figure 8.2** Seisan, count 12: both hands draw the partner's head down onto the rising knee.](figures/bk_seisan_2.png)

### Hook, stamp and rising elbow (counts 15 to 17)

Catch the punching arm with kake uke, stamp through the knee, and as your partner buckles forward, drive the rising elbow up under their chin. The same safety warning applies as in Shisochin: never practise kansetsu geri against a real knee.

## Problems and solutions

**Problem: the opening looks like a fast Sanchin.** *Cause*: copying Sanchin's tension and breathing. *Solution*: Seisan's opening uses Sanchin's stance and structure, but its techniques should be performed at a fighting pace with natural breathing.

**Problem: the renzuki are a blur.** *Cause*: speed without structure. *Solution*: practise the four punches at half speed until every punch has a full extension and a full hikite. Then speed up a little at a time. Use a metronome if you have one.

**Problem: the shoulders rise during the renzuki.** *Cause*: tension in the upper body. *Solution*: keep the shoulders down and the armpits closed. The power comes from the rotation of the hips and the connection to the stance, not from the shoulders.

**Problem: the knee strike is weak.** *Cause*: lifting the knee without pulling down with the hands. *Solution*: the hands and knee meet. Practise with a partner holding a pad at chest height and pulling it down onto your knee.

**Problem: the diagonals are performed at ninety degrees.** *Cause*: misjudging the angle. *Solution*: tape the corners. The diagonals in Seisan are at forty-five degrees behind you.

**Problem: the rising elbow lacks power.** *Cause*: it is thrown from the shoulder. *Solution*: it should rise with the landing of the front foot and the drive of the back leg.

## Deeper study: Seisan across the styles

Seisan is one of the few kata that you can meet in almost any Okinawan karate club, whatever its style. Gōjū-ryū, Uechi-ryū, Shōrin-ryū, Isshin-ryū, Ryūei-ryū and many others practise a kata called Seisan or Sēsan, and in Japanese styles it often appears as Hangetsu. The versions differ, sometimes greatly, but many share the same backbone: a slow or steady advance in a short, rooted stance with blocks and punches, followed by sections of turning and striking.

This wide distribution tells us something. Seisan is probably very old, older than the division of Okinawan karate into Naha-te and Shuri-te, and it was valued highly enough to be kept by teachers who otherwise had little in common. Whatever its origins, it must have been regarded as a complete and useful kata.

For the Gōjū student, comparing Seisan across the styles is a valuable exercise. Uechi-ryū's Seisan, for instance, keeps a sanchin-like stance throughout and is full of open-hand techniques and short kicks. Shōrin-ryū's version is lighter and longer in its stances. Shōtōkan's Hangetsu, "half moon", takes its name from the crescent stepping of its opening. Watching them, you see the same underlying ideas expressed through the habits of different schools. You also see more clearly what is distinctive about the Gōjū version: its directness, its chain punch, its close-range knee strikes and stamps.

### The chain punch

The renzuki of count 7 is the most memorable moment in Seisan and the most often spoiled. Its purpose is to teach you to strike repeatedly without losing your structure. Most people, asked to punch fast, lose their stance, lift their shoulders, lean forward and shorten every punch. Seisan asks you to punch fast while keeping everything you learned in Sanchin: the rooted feet, the tucked pelvis, the dropped shoulders, the closed armpits.

There is a good way to train this. Stand in sanchin dachi facing a partner who holds a pad. Punch once, completely, with full hikite. Then twice, the second punch starting as the first returns. Then three times, then four. Only add a punch when the previous number is perfect. Over weeks, the speed will come without your structure falling apart.

A useful image for the renzuki is a piston engine. Each punch goes out and comes back along the same line, and as one goes out the other comes back, so that the hips rock very slightly from side to side and drive each punch in turn. The body does not move forward; the stance holds you in place, and all the power goes into the target.

### Close range

Every technique in Seisan works at close range. The double block meets arms that are already reaching for you. The knee strike works on an opponent whose head you are holding. The stamp works on a leg that is within half a step. The rising elbow works under a chin that is a forearm's length away.

This makes Seisan an excellent kata for studying the clinch: the moment in a real encounter when two people are tangled together, gripping, pushing and pulling. It is the moment that many karate styles neglect, and it is where Gōjū-ryū is at its strongest.

## Partner drills

**Drill 1: block-and-punch ladder.** Your partner pushes your chest with both hands at walking pace. Double block, then punch to a pad held on their chest. Ten repetitions, then swap.

**Drill 2: the renzuki ladder.** As described above, against a pad, building from one punch to four over several sessions.

**Drill 3: guard clearing.** Your partner holds their hands up in a guard. Perform renzuki slowly, each punch accompanied by the other hand moving their guard aside. Ten slow sequences.

**Drill 4: pull and knee.** Your partner holds a kick shield at chest height. Reach over the top, pull it down and drive the knee into it. Ten each side.

**Drill 5: clinch kakie.** From the kakie position (Chapter 3), work for openings in which to use the double block and punch, or the pull and knee, at slow speed.

## Case files from the dojo

::: casefile
**Case file 8.1: the frantic renzuki**

*The problem.* A shodan candidate's renzuki was extremely fast but looked frantic, with the shoulders hunched and the stance creeping forward.

*The diagnosis.* Speed had been trained before structure. Each punch was short, the hikite drifted and the hips did not rotate.

*The solution.* The renzuki ladder, starting from one punch, for three weeks. The candidate was not allowed to add speed until every punch was full length with a complete hikite. The final renzuki was slightly slower but far more powerful, and the examiners commented favourably on it.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 8.2: the floating knee**

*The problem.* A student's knee strike looked weak and was performed with the hands hanging in the air.

*The diagnosis.* The student did not understand that the hands were pulling the opponent's head down.

*The solution.* Drill 4, with a partner pulling the shield gently down as the student's hands drew it in. Once the student felt the hands and knee meet, the movement in the kata changed completely.
:::

::: casefile
**Case file 8.3: the drifting diagonals**

*The problem.* A student's rear diagonals went out at about thirty degrees, too close to the centre line.

*The diagnosis.* After turning to face south, the student did not re-orient before stepping out.

*The solution.* Tape marks at the two rear corners, and a deliberate glance to each corner before stepping. The glance soon became the natural chakugan of the kata.
:::

## Variations between associations

Seisan is so widely practised that its variations are almost endless. Within Gōjū-ryū:

- The number of forward steps in the opening is three in most versions, but some include additional double blocks.
- The renzuki vary in number, from three to six punches.
- Some versions place the rear-diagonal section before the turn rather than after it, or perform it with a different block.
- The knee strike is performed in some versions as a rising knee to the face, in others as a knee to the thigh or body.
- The ending of the kata varies more than any other part, with versions including a throwing action, a double strike or a second mawashi uke.

Outside Gōjū-ryū, Seisan in Uechi-ryū, Shōrin-ryū and Isshin-ryū differ considerably from the Gōjū version, though the opening in sanchin dachi is often recognisable.

::: grading
**Grading notes: Seisan**

*Typical grade*: shodan or nidan in many Okinawan-line associations; sometimes earlier.

*What the examiners look for*:

- Sanchin-quality structure in the opening, at a fighting pace.
- A renzuki of complete, distinct punches.
- Strong, accurate diagonals with balance on the knee strikes.
- A stamping kansetsu geri.
- A rising elbow driven by the legs.
- A single, decisive kiai.
- Evidence that the candidate understands the close-range nature of the kata.

*Common reasons for a marginal result*: renzuki blurred or weak; shoulders rising; diagonals at the wrong angle; kicks unbalanced.
:::

## A four-week training plan

**Week one.** Sanchin three times every session. Seisan section one, five times. Renzuki drill: four sets of ten, slowly.

**Week two.** Sections two to four, five times each. Knee strikes against a pad, twenty each side.

**Week three.** Whole kata five times. Renzuki at increasing speed.

**Week four.** Perform for an audience. Bunkai of the double block and the knee strike with a partner.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Seisan, "thirteen", is one of the oldest and most widespread kata in Okinawan karate.
- It opens with Sanchin's stance at a fighting pace, then explodes into a chain of punches.
- Every punch in the renzuki is complete. Speed comes after structure.
- The diagonals pull the opponent down onto the knee.
- It is a kata for the clinch. Stay close.

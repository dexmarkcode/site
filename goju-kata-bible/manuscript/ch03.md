# Reading Kata: Notation, Embusen, Bunkai and How Grading Works

::: chapter-intro
A kata is a text written in movement, and like any text it has to be read before it can be understood. This chapter teaches you to read the diagrams in this book, explains the floor patterns, sets out a sensible way of finding applications for the movements, describes how kata are actually learned and practised, and walks you through what happens on the grading floor.
:::

When students first learn a kata, they learn it as a dance: step here, hands like this, turn, step there. That is fine, and necessary, as a first stage. But a student who never moves beyond that stage will find that their kata stays fragile. Forget one move and the whole thing collapses. Change the room and they lose their direction. Ask them what a movement is for and they shrug.

The aim of this chapter is to give you the tools to move beyond the dance. We will start with the practical business of reading the diagrams, because you will use them constantly, and then move on to the floor pattern, the meaning of the movements, the method of practice and finally the examination.

## How to read the diagram sequences

Every kata in this book is drawn count by count. Figure 3.1 shows a single panel with its parts labelled.

![**Figure 3.1** Anatomy of a sequence panel. Every panel in Chapters 4 to 11 follows this layout.](figures/fig_notation.png)

### The count number

The number in the black box at the top left is the count. Counts are numbered as they are commonly called in the dojo, starting at 1 after the formal opening. The bow (*rei*) and the ready position (*yōi*) are given their own panels, labelled with words rather than numbers, and the closing position (*naore*) is given the final number.

Different associations count the same kata differently. Some count each technique; some count each step; some count combinations as one. I have generally counted each new position or each distinct technique, so that every important shape in the kata has its own panel. If your dojo counts the kata in fewer numbers, you will simply find that some of your counts are split across two or three of my panels.

### The facing dial

The small dial in the top right corner shows the compass direction your chest faces at the end of the count. North is always the front of the dojo, the direction you face at the start of the kata and the direction in which the examiners usually sit. East is to your right as you begin, west to your left, south behind you. The letters below the dial repeat the direction in words.

This convention matters because a figure drawn from the side always appears to be facing to the right of the page. Without the dial, you would have no way of knowing that one panel faces north and the next faces west. With it, you can always place yourself on the floor.

### White and grey limbs

The figure is drawn so that the performer's left side is towards you, in white, and the right side is further away, in grey. So if the white arm is blocking and the grey arm is at the hip, it is the left arm that blocks. If the grey leg is forward, it is the right leg that is forward.

In the panels drawn from the front (heiko dachi, shiko dachi and some others), the figure faces you. In these, the performer's left side is on your right, as it would be if a real person stood facing you. All limbs are white in front views, and the text below the panel tells you which hand is working.

### The text and the coaching cue

Below each figure you will find the stance and technique, in the form *stance · technique*, and below that, in italics, a coaching cue: a short reminder of what to pay attention to. The cues are the corrections I find myself giving most often for that count. When you are polishing a kata, read the cues more than the techniques.

### The count sheet

After the diagram sequence for each kata comes a complete count sheet in table form. It lists every count, the direction you face, the stance and technique and the coaching cue. The count sheet is designed to be used without the pictures, as a memory test. Cover the right-hand columns with a sheet of paper and try to name each count before you uncover it.

## The embusen

*Embusen* means the line of performance, the pattern a kata traces on the floor. Each kata in this book has an embusen map showing every position on the floor where something happens, labelled with a letter, and a list of the counts performed at each station. The black station is the start and finish.

The embusen is far more important than most students realise. A kata performed on the wrong lines is not just untidy; it is a different kata, because the angles of the turns carry meaning. A turn of forty-five degrees and a turn of ninety degrees put you in very different relationships with an opponent. Examiners notice the embusen straight away, because it is the most visible part of a kata from the side of the room.

### The common shapes

Most kata embusen are built from a few basic shapes, shown in Figure 3.2.

![**Figure 3.2** The basic embusen shapes. Most Gōjū kata combine two or more of these.](figures/fig_embusen_shapes.png)

The **I** shape is a straight line forward and back, as in Sanchin. The **T** shape adds movement to the left and right at one end, as in the opening of Gekisai. The **cross** (*jūji*) moves in four directions from a centre point. The **X** uses the diagonals, which are prominent in Saifa, Seiyunchin and Kururunfa. The **box** moves around a square, and appears in parts of Seipai and Suparinpei.

### Starting and finishing on the same spot

A traditional test of a well-performed kata is that it finishes on exactly the spot where it began. This is a useful check on the length of your stances and steps, but do not treat it as an absolute rule. Some kata, in some versions, do not return exactly to the start. If your kata consistently drifts forward, though, your steps are probably too long; if it drifts backward, they are too short or you are stepping back further than you step forward.

A good habit is to place a small coin or a piece of tape on the floor where you begin, and check it at the end. Over a few weeks you will learn to feel your stances so precisely that you rarely need the coin.

## Bunkai: reading the meaning

*Bunkai* means analysis, or taking apart. In karate it refers to the study of what the movements in a kata are for. It is one of the most interesting and most argued-about subjects in the martial arts, and I want to give you a sensible way of approaching it before you meet the bunkai sections in each kata chapter.

### Three levels of bunkai

It helps to think of bunkai at three levels.

**Basic bunkai** takes each movement at face value. An age uke blocks a punch to the head; a gedan barai blocks a kick; a tsuki is a punch. This is where everyone starts, and it is not wrong. It teaches distance, timing and the basic shape of each technique against a partner.

**Applied bunkai** asks what each movement would be most effective against in a real, close-range encounter. At this level, a so-called block may become a strike, a grip, a release from a grab or a way of controlling an arm. The hikite becomes a pull. A stance change becomes a way of unbalancing someone. This is the level at which the kaishu kata start to make sense.

**Free bunkai** takes the principles found in the kata and applies them flexibly, in drills that become less and less predictable, until they shade into *kakie* (the pushing-hands practice described below) and free sparring.

### Principles for sensible bunkai

There are a great many books and courses on bunkai, some excellent, some fanciful. Here are the principles I find most useful for keeping it sensible.

1. **Start from the attack, not from the kata.** Real attacks are usually simple: grabs, pushes, wild swinging punches, headlocks, attempts to take someone to the ground. If your bunkai only works against a step-in lunge punch delivered from two metres away, it is a drill, not an application.
2. **Assume close range.** Gōjū-ryū is a close-range art. Most applications work best at arm's length or closer.
3. **Every movement does something.** If a hand goes to the hip, ask what it is pulling. If a hand rises slowly, ask what it is lifting or controlling. If the body turns, ask whom it is turning.
4. **Respect the direction.** An application that requires you to face a completely different direction from the kata is suspect. The embusen tells you where the opponent is.
5. **There is more than one right answer.** A movement may have several valid applications. The kata is a library, not a single book.
6. **It must work under pressure.** Test your bunkai with a partner who resists, gradually and safely. If it falls apart at the first sign of resistance, think again.

### The partner in the bunkai drawings

In the bunkai figures in this book, the person performing the kata technique (*tori*, or you) is drawn on the left in white, and the partner (*uke*) is drawn on the right in pale grey, facing you. The drawings show the key moment of each application. The text describes how you get there and what happens next.

## Kakie: the bridge between kata and fighting

*Kakie* is a two-person exercise found in Gōjū-ryū and other Okinawan styles, somewhat similar to the pushing-hands exercises of Chinese martial arts. Two partners stand facing each other, usually in sanchin dachi or a similar stance, with their forearms or wrists in contact. They push, pull and circle against each other, each trying to find an opening to strike, grab, lock or unbalance while staying rooted and protected.

![**Figure 3.3** Kakie. Partners stand with the backs of their wrists in contact, each in a rooted stance, and circle, push and pull while looking for openings.](figures/fig_kakie.png)

Kakie matters for kata because it teaches you to feel what the techniques of the kaishu kata are for. The hooking block, the pulling block, the circular block, the sudden grip and pull: all these appear naturally in kakie. A student who practises kakie regularly will find that their kata become more alive, because their hands have learned what it is like to meet resistance.

## How kata are learned

Learning a kata well happens in stages. Students who try to skip stages end up with kata that look finished but are hollow inside. The stages I teach, and the ones I recommend you follow with each kata in this book, are these.

### Stage one: the shape

First you learn the sequence: which way to face, which stance, which technique, in what order. At this stage, speed and power do not matter. Accuracy and memory do. Use the embusen map and the count sheet. Walk the pattern without hands, then add the hands. Expect this stage to take several weeks of regular practice for a short kata and several months for a long one.

### Stage two: the structure

Next you work on each position: the correct stance, the correct height and angle of the hands, the line of the back, the direction of the eyes. Practise the kata slowly, stopping on each count to check. Have a teacher or a training partner correct you. This is the stage where the stance work in Chapter 2 pays off.

### Stage three: the transitions

Then you work on how you move from one position to the next: the crescent steps, the turns, the shifts of weight. The transitions are where power is generated and where it is lost. Practise each transition many times in isolation.

### Stage four: rhythm and breath

Now you add the breathing pattern and the rhythm of the kata: which movements are slow, which fast, where the pauses fall, where the kiai comes. A kata performed at one constant speed is dead. Each kata chapter in this book describes its rhythm in detail.

### Stage five: meaning

Then you study the bunkai, with a partner, so that you know what each movement is for. Once you know, you will find that your kata changes. Techniques that were vague become precise, because you know what they are hitting. Movements that were awkward become natural, because you understand why they are there.

### Stage six: making it your own

Finally, after years, the kata stops being something you perform and becomes something you express. You still perform the same movements as everyone else in your association, but they carry your own understanding and your own body. This is the stage at which senior karateka are said to have a good kata, and no book can teach it.

## How to practise a kata you already know

Once you know a kata, how should you practise it? Simply performing it over and over from start to finish is the least efficient way. Here are some better methods, which I recommend mixing in every session.

**Section practice.** Divide the kata into sections (each kata chapter suggests how) and practise one section at a time, repeating it five or ten times before moving on.

**Slow practice.** Perform the whole kata at a quarter of normal speed, with perfect stances and smooth transitions. This exposes every weakness.

**Reverse practice.** Practise the last section first, then the last two, and so on. Most students know the beginning of every kata far better than the end, because they always start from the beginning.

**Mirror practice.** Perform the kata starting to the right instead of the left, so that every movement is reversed. This is mentally demanding and shows you how well you really know the pattern.

**Eyes-closed practice.** In a safe space, perform a short section with your eyes closed, to feel the stances and turns without visual cues. Start with very short sections.

**Different rooms.** Practise in a different room, facing a different wall, or outdoors. A kata that only works in your own dojo facing your own mirror is not yet learned.

**Breath-only practice.** Stand still and breathe the kata, imagining each movement while making the breathing pattern. This is surprisingly effective for fixing breathing faults.

## How grading works

Gradings vary greatly between associations, and the only reliable source of information about your next grading is your own instructor. Even so, the basic shape of a karate grading is remarkably consistent across the Gōjū world, and knowing it in advance takes away a great deal of anxiety.

### The panel

A grading is usually assessed by a panel of senior grades, often three, sometimes more for black-belt examinations. They sit at the front of the room, which is why north is the front in every diagram in this book. They will have a marking sheet listing the elements of the grading and the criteria for each. Some associations use numerical scores; others use pass, fail or refer.

### The content

A typical kyū (coloured-belt) grading will include:

- **Kihon**: basic techniques, in stances, on the spot and moving.
- **Kata**: one or more kata from the syllabus for your grade, and often a kata from a previous grade.
- **Bunkai**: applications from your grading kata, performed with a partner.
- **Kumite**: some form of partner work or sparring, from prearranged drills to free sparring at senior grades.
- **Conditioning or fitness**: some associations test this; many test it implicitly by the length of the grading.
- **Knowledge**: questions on terminology, history or the meaning of techniques.

Dan (black-belt) gradings are longer and more demanding, and may include teaching assessments, written work and more complex bunkai.

### Where the kata fit

Each association has its own syllabus, specifying which kata are required for each grade. Figure 12.1 in Chapter 12 shows a typical progression. In most Okinawan-line associations, the Gekisai kata are required for the early kyū grades, Saifa and Seiyunchin for the middle and senior kyū grades, Shisochin around first kyū or shodan, and the remaining kata at various dan grades. Some associations require Sanchin at every grading, as a test of foundation.

### What examiners look for in kata

Chapter 12 deals with this in detail, and every kata chapter has its own grading notes. In summary, examiners look for:

- **Correct sequence**: the kata performed without hesitation or error.
- **Correct embusen**: the right lines, angles and finishing position.
- **Stances**: strong, correct and consistent.
- **Techniques**: accurate in shape, height and target.
- **Breathing**: appropriate to the kata and to each section.
- **Rhythm**: the contrast of slow and fast, hard and soft.
- **Kime**: focus at the end of techniques.
- **Chakugan**: the eyes leading, especially on turns.
- **Zanshin**: continued awareness at the end of the kata.
- **Understanding**: shown in bunkai and in the precision of the techniques themselves.

### Forgetting the kata

Every examiner has seen a candidate forget their kata, and every examiner has done it themselves. What matters is what happens next. If you lose your place, stop, keep your composure, return to the ready position if necessary and ask, quietly, whether you may begin again. Do not invent movements, do not laugh, do not look at your feet. A candidate who handles a mistake with dignity often makes a better impression than one who performs a technically correct but nervous kata.

## Etiquette in kata

Kata begin and end with a bow, and the way you bow says a great deal. Stand in musubi dachi, hands open at your sides, eyes forward. Bow from the hips, not the neck, to about thirty degrees, keeping your eyes on the panel or slightly lowered according to your association's custom. Return to upright. Then announce the name of the kata clearly, if your association does so, and move into the ready position.

At the end, after the final position, hold *zanshin* for a moment. Zanshin means remaining mind: the continued awareness that the encounter may not be over. Then return to musubi dachi, bow and wait to be dismissed. Do not relax the moment the last technique is finished. Examiners mark the end of a kata as carefully as the beginning.

## Problems and solutions: reading and practising kata

**Problem: "I keep getting lost on turns and finishing facing the wrong way."** Use the facing dial on each panel and walk the embusen without techniques, calling out the compass direction of each count aloud: "west, west, north, east". Once the directions are automatic, add the stances, then the hands.

**Problem: "I know the kata but it always looks rushed."** You are probably performing every movement at the same speed. Read the rhythm section for your kata and mark the slow movements, the pauses and the fast combinations on the count sheet. Practise with exaggerated pauses at first.

**Problem: "My bunkai looks nothing like my kata."** That is common at the applied level, where techniques are adapted to a real partner. But if your bunkai requires completely different stances or directions, reconsider it. Ask whether there is an application that stays closer to the shape of the kata.

**Problem: "I learned a kata years ago and now my club does it differently."** Kata do change, sometimes because a senior teacher refines them, sometimes because an association standardises its syllabus. Learn the current version for grading purposes. If you want to keep the old version alive in your personal practice, there is no harm in that, but do not mix them up in front of a panel.

**Problem: "I freeze in gradings even though I know the kata perfectly in class."** This is nerves, and it is normal. Chapter 12 has a full section on preparation and nerves. In the meantime, practise performing your kata in front of people as often as possible: family, friends, the rest of your class. The more often you perform under observation, the less the grading floor will frighten you.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- In every diagram, the number is the count, the dial shows the direction you face (north is the front) and white limbs belong to the performer's left side.
- The embusen is part of the meaning of the kata, not just its tidiness. Walk it without techniques until it is automatic.
- Bunkai works at three levels: basic, applied and free. Start from realistic attacks and assume close range.
- Learn each kata in stages: shape, structure, transitions, rhythm and breath, meaning and finally expression.
- Practise in sections, slowly, in reverse, mirrored and in unfamiliar places, not just from start to finish.
- In a grading, examiners look at sequence, embusen, stances, techniques, breathing, rhythm, kime, eyes and zanshin, and at how you handle mistakes.
:::

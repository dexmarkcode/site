# How to Use This Book

This is a reference book and a workbook at the same time. You will get the most from it if you read it in two different ways: once from cover to cover, to see how the whole system fits together, and then many times in small pieces, on the dojo floor or in your kitchen, with one kata open in front of you.

## The layout of each kata chapter

Chapters 4 to 11 each deal with one kata (Chapter 4 deals with the two Gekisai kata together). They all follow the same pattern, so that once you have learned to use one chapter you can find your way around all of them.

1. **Name, meaning and origins.** What the name means, where the kata is thought to come from and how it fits into the syllabus.
2. **Character.** The feel of the kata: its rhythm, its breathing, where its power comes from and what makes it different from its neighbours.
3. **Before you begin.** The stances and techniques you need to be confident with before you start learning the form.
4. **The embusen map.** A floor plan of the kata, with lettered stations showing where every count is performed.
5. **The sequence, count by count.** A full written walkthrough, section by section, followed by the diagram sequence and the complete count sheet.
6. **Breathing, rhythm and timing.** Which movements are slow, which are fast, where the breath is held and where it is released.
7. **Bunkai.** Practical applications for the key movements, with drawings of the partner work.
8. **Problems and solutions.** The faults I see most often in this kata, why they happen and how to fix them.
9. **Variations.** The main points where other associations perform the kata differently.
10. **Grading notes.** What examiners look for, and a short checklist.
11. **A training plan.** A four-week plan for learning or polishing the kata.
12. **Key points.** A one-box summary to read before you train.

## Reading the diagrams

Every count of every kata is drawn as a panel in a sequence. Chapter 3 explains the notation in detail, but the essentials are simple. The number in the black box is the count. The small dial in the corner shows which compass direction your chest faces, with north always the front of the dojo, where the examiners sit. White limbs belong to the performer's left side; grey limbs belong to the right side. Below the figure you will find the stance and technique, and below that, in italics, a short coaching cue.

## Learning a kata from this book

A book cannot replace a teacher, and I would never suggest learning a new kata from these pages alone. What the book can do is help you remember, check and deepen what you have been shown. The method I recommend is this:

- After a lesson in which you are shown new material, read the relevant section of the walkthrough the same evening, while it is fresh.
- Walk the embusen slowly, without techniques, using the map. Most early mistakes in a kata are floor-pattern mistakes.
- Add the stances, then the hands, a section at a time, checking each panel of the diagram sequence.
- Use the count sheet as a test. Cover the right-hand columns and call out each count from memory.
- Read the problems and solutions section before your next class, so that you know what to listen for when your teacher corrects you.

## A note on Japanese terms

Japanese words are explained the first time they appear and collected in the glossary at the back. I have used the spellings most common in English-speaking dojo. Long vowels are marked with a macron in the running text, so you will see Gōjū-ryū and Chōjun, but they are often dropped in the count sheets and diagrams, as they are on most dojo walls.

## Safety

Please read the notice on the copyright page. Warm up before every session. Do not practise bunkai at speed or with force without a qualified instructor present. If something hurts in a sharp or unusual way, stop. Kata are meant to be practised for a lifetime, and a lifetime is a long time to carry an injury that could have been avoided with a little patience.

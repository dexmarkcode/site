# Preface

There is a moment in every Gōjū-ryū student's life when the kata stop being a list of moves and become something else. It rarely arrives on a good day. More often it comes on a cold evening, in a half-empty dojo, on the fortieth repetition of a form you thought you already knew. The legs are tired, the breath is ragged, and you stop trying to remember what comes next. For a few seconds the kata simply happens, and you understand, without being able to say it, why people have bothered to preserve these strange old sequences for more than a century.

This book is written to help you get to that moment sooner, and to help you get back to it when you lose it.

I have been asked many times, by students and by instructors from other clubs, for a single book that sets out the Gōjū-ryū kata properly. Not a pamphlet with a photograph for every third move, and not a philosophical essay that never tells you which foot to step with. They wanted something they could open on the dojo floor, find count fourteen of Seipai, see exactly what it looks like, and read why it is done that way. They also wanted to know what an examiner is looking for when they perform it, because, whatever we say about grading not mattering, it matters to the person standing in front of the panel.

So that is what you have here. Each of the kata from Gekisai Dai Ichi to Suparinpei is given a chapter of its own. Every count is drawn. Every kata has a floor map, a complete count sheet, a section on breathing and rhythm, practical applications, a fault-finding guide written as problems and their solutions, a set of grading notes and a training plan. The first three chapters lay the foundations: the history of the style, the stances and breathing that drive it, and a way of reading and practising kata that will serve you for the rest of your life. The final chapter is about the grading floor itself and about the long years of practice that come after the last grading you will ever take.

## Why kata still matter

It is fashionable in some circles to dismiss kata as dance. I understand the complaint. Kata performed badly, without intent or understanding, are exactly that. But kata performed well are something quite different: a compressed record of how a particular group of fighters believed the body should be organised under pressure. They teach you to stand, to breathe, to turn, to generate force from the ground and to keep your structure when someone is trying to take it from you. They are also a library of techniques for close-range, chaotic, real conflict, written in a code that each generation has to learn to read for itself.

Miyagi Chōjun, the founder of Gōjū-ryū, did not leave us a long written explanation of his kata. He left us the kata themselves, Sanchin and Tenshō to forge the body, and a handful of short writings. Everything else has passed from teacher to student, by demonstration and correction. That is both the strength and the weakness of the tradition. It keeps the art alive and personal, but it also means that details drift. One teacher remembers a turn as ninety degrees, another as forty-five. One association steps forward with the left foot, another with the right. Neither is wrong in any simple sense; each is a faithful copy of what that teacher was shown.

## How this book handles differences

I have chosen to present one consistent teaching version of each kata, in the Okinawan line descending from Miyagi, and to mark the main points where other associations differ. I have not tried to referee between lineages, and I would ask you not to use this book to do so either. If your sensei teaches a kata differently from the way it is drawn here, follow your sensei. Use the drawings to understand the principles underneath, and you will find that most of the differences become small and rather interesting rather than threatening.

The diagram sequences were drawn specifically for this book in a plain black-and-white style. They are not photographs, and that is deliberate. A photograph shows one person's body on one day, with all its quirks. A clean drawing shows the shape you are aiming at: the line of the back, the bend of the knee, the height of the hand. The left side of the figure is always drawn in white and the right side in grey, so that you can tell at a glance which hand and which foot are working. Chapter 3 explains the notation in full.

## Who this book is for

If you are a beginner, start with Chapters 1 to 4 and leave the rest for later. Read the history once and the stance chapter many times. Learn Gekisai thoroughly before you think about anything else.

If you are a coloured belt working towards black belt, Chapters 5 to 7 cover the kata you will be examined on, and Chapter 12 tells you how to prepare.

If you are already a black belt, I hope the chapters on Seisan, Seipai, Kururunfa and Suparinpei give you something to argue with, and that the grading notes are useful when you sit on the other side of the table.

If you teach, the count sheets in each chapter and the checklists in Appendix A are designed to be photocopied for your own students.

## A word of thanks

No one writes a book like this alone. I owe a great deal to the teachers who corrected me patiently for many years, to the students who asked the questions I could not answer and so sent me back to the dojo to find out, and to the many karateka of other associations who have shared their versions of these kata with generosity and good humour. Any mistakes that remain are my own.

Now put the kettle on, find a space on the floor, and let us begin with where it all came from.

*Dr Beverly J. Stivers*

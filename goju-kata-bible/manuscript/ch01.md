# Roots: Naha-te, Miyagi Chōjun and the Meaning of Hard and Soft

::: chapter-intro
You cannot perform a kata well if you think it fell from the sky fully formed. Every Gōjū-ryū kata carries the fingerprints of the people who made, kept and changed it. This chapter tells their story as plainly as the evidence allows, separates what we know from what we have been told, and explains why the history still changes the way you move on the dojo floor.
:::

When I began karate, history was the part of the lesson the teacher rushed through while we caught our breath. A few names, a few dates, a story about a man who trained in China and came home to teach, and then back to the floor. I suspect many of you had the same experience. It took me years to realise how much I had missed. The history of Gōjū-ryū is not decoration. It explains why the kata look the way they do, why one turn is at forty-five degrees and another at ninety, why the hands so often open rather than close, and why two dojo in the same town can perform the same kata in visibly different ways.

So let us begin properly, with an island, a port and a trade route.

## Okinawa, Naha and the China trade

Okinawa is the largest island of the Ryūkyū chain, a string of islands strung out between the southern tip of Japan and Taiwan. For several centuries it was the heart of an independent kingdom, the Ryūkyū Kingdom, which made its living by trade. Ships from Naha, the main port, sailed to China, Japan, Korea and South-East Asia. The kingdom paid tribute to the Chinese emperor and, from 1609, was also under the heavy influence of the Satsuma domain of southern Japan. It lived, in other words, between two great powers, and its culture shows it.

The Chinese connection mattered most for karate. Ryūkyū ships sailed regularly to Fuzhou in Fujian province, on China's south-east coast, and a community of Ryūkyūan officials, students and merchants lived there for long periods. Fujian was, and is, home to a rich variety of southern Chinese boxing: crane styles, tiger styles, styles named after families and villages. Some Okinawans who spent time in Fuzhou studied these arts and brought fragments of them home.

On Okinawa, local fighting methods were generally called *te* (hand) or *tōde* (Chinese hand, or Tang hand). By the late nineteenth century, people had begun to speak of slightly different flavours of *te* associated with different towns. Shuri, the royal capital, gave its name to Shuri-te. Tomari, a neighbouring port, gave its name to Tomari-te. Naha, the busy commercial port, gave its name to Naha-te. These labels were applied loosely, mostly after the fact, and they describe tendencies rather than tidy schools. Still, they are useful. Naha-te, broadly speaking, was characterised by close-range fighting, circular blocking, gripping, rooted stances and a strong emphasis on breathing and body conditioning. Every one of those features is alive in Gōjū-ryū today.

## Higaonna Kanryō

The central figure of Naha-te is Higaonna Kanryō, born in Naha in 1853. The story told in most Gōjū-ryū dojo goes like this. As a young man from a family that had fallen on hard times, Higaonna travelled to Fuzhou, where he spent many years studying Chinese boxing under a teacher usually remembered as Ryū Ryū Ko. He returned to Naha a formidable fighter and teacher, and passed his art to a small group of students, among them a wealthy young man named Miyagi Chōjun.

How much of this story can we trust? The honest answer is: the outline, yes; the details, with care. Higaonna certainly existed, certainly taught in Naha and certainly taught Miyagi. That he spent time in Fuzhou is widely accepted. The length of his stay is given in different sources as anything from a few years to fifteen. The identity of Ryū Ryū Ko has been the subject of decades of research, with several candidates proposed and none universally accepted. The date of his departure for China is traditionally given as 1867, but this, too, is debated.

I mention these uncertainties not to undermine the tradition but to protect it. A tradition that claims more certainty than it has becomes brittle. When a student discovers that a date they were told as fact is actually disputed, they may start to doubt everything else. Far better to say what we know, what we believe and what we simply do not know.

What we can say with confidence is that Higaonna Kanryō's karate was hard, practical and demanding. Accounts from his students describe severe Sanchin training, in which the teacher tested the student's stance and body tension by striking and pushing them. They describe heavy conditioning with traditional tools, and a teacher who was not interested in showing off. He taught only a small number of kata, and taught them slowly. Higaonna died in 1915.

## Miyagi Chōjun

Miyagi Chōjun was born in Naha in 1888 into a prosperous family. He began studying with Higaonna Kanryō as a teenager and trained with him until his teacher's death, a period of more than a decade. After Higaonna died, Miyagi travelled to Fujian himself, to seek out the roots of his teacher's art. Accounts differ on what he found; it is generally agreed that he did not find Higaonna's teacher, and that he studied and observed various Chinese styles while he was there.

Miyagi was a different kind of man from his teacher. He had money and leisure, and he devoted both to karate. He was also, crucially, a systematiser. He looked at the material he had inherited and asked how it could be taught, not only to a handful of private students, but to schoolchildren, police officers and the public. He studied anatomy and physiology as they were understood in his day. He developed preparatory exercises (*junbi undō*) and supplementary conditioning exercises (*hojo undō*). He created new kata, which we will meet in a moment. And he gave his art a name.

## The name Gōjū-ryū

The story of the name is one of the best-documented episodes in Gōjū history. Around 1930, Miyagi's senior student Shinzato Jin'an demonstrated karate at an event in mainland Japan. Asked which school he belonged to, Shinzato had no answer, because Naha-te had never been organised as a formal school with a name. When he reported this back, Miyagi chose a name drawn from a line in the *Bubishi*, an old Chinese text on martial arts and medicine that circulated in manuscript among Okinawan karateka.

The line is usually rendered as *hō gō jū donto*: "the way of inhaling and exhaling is hardness and softness". From it Miyagi took *gō* (hard) and *jū* (soft), and added *ryū* (school or style). Gōjū-ryū: the hard–soft school. By 1933 the style was registered under that name with the Dai Nippon Butokukai, the national body for martial arts in pre-war Japan.

It is worth slowing down over that line from the *Bubishi*, because it is the key to the whole style. Notice that it is about breathing. Hardness and softness are not two separate kinds of technique, one for blocking and one for striking. They are two phases of a single rhythm, as natural and as inseparable as breathing in and breathing out. A Gōjū-ryū karateka is soft while receiving, hard at the moment of impact, soft again in the transition, hard again when it matters. Tension that never releases is not hardness; it is rigidity, and rigidity is slow and easily broken. Softness that never firms up is not softness; it is limpness, and limpness cannot deliver force. The art is in the alternation.

You will see this principle in every kata in this book. Sanchin teaches it in its purest form. Tenshō, its partner kata, teaches its soft side. The kaishu kata weave the two together in countless ways.

## Miyagi's students and the spread of Gōjū-ryū

Miyagi taught many students over his lifetime, and after his death in 1953 several of them became the heads of their own lineages. The family tree of Gōjū-ryū is complicated, and I have simplified it ruthlessly in Figure 1.2. The aim is not to give a complete genealogy but to show why the same kata can look different in different dojo.

Among the most influential of Miyagi's students were:

- **Yagi Meitoku**, who founded the Meibukan and was entrusted with Miyagi's gi and belt by the family. Meibukan Gōjū-ryū added a number of kata of its own.
- **Miyazato Ei'ichi**, who founded the Jundōkan in Naha, one of the most important Gōjū dojo in Okinawa and the place where many overseas karateka have trained.
- **Toguchi Seikichi**, a student of both Higa Seikō and Miyagi, who founded the Shōreikan and developed a large set of supplementary training kata.
- **Higa Seikō**, who had trained under Higaonna Kanryō before Miyagi and became a major teacher in his own right.
- **Yamaguchi Gōgen**, who studied with Miyagi and became the driving force behind Gōjū-kai in mainland Japan. Gōjū-kai's versions of the kata differ in noticeable ways from the Okinawan ones.

A later generation produced **Higaonna Morio** (no relation to Higaonna Kanryō), who trained in Tokyo and at the Jundōkan and founded the International Okinawan Gōjū-ryū Karate-dō Federation (IOGKF) in 1979. The IOGKF is now one of the largest Gōjū organisations in the world, and many English-speaking students learned their kata through it.

### Why the kata differ

Imagine a teacher showing a student a kata in 1935. There is no video. There are no diagrams. The student watches, copies and is corrected. Twenty years later that student teaches the kata to their own students, as they remember it, with whatever adjustments their own body and understanding have made. Another student of the same teacher, who learned the kata in 1950 rather than 1935, may have been shown a slightly different version, because the teacher's own understanding had changed in fifteen years. Now multiply this by several generations and several continents.

That is how variations arise. Very few of them are mistakes in any meaningful sense. A turn performed at forty-five degrees in one lineage and ninety in another is a choice, sometimes a deliberate teaching decision and sometimes an accident that has hardened into tradition. When you meet a karateka from another association who performs Seipai differently from you, the right response is curiosity, not correction.

## The kata of Gōjū-ryū

Gōjū-ryū has a relatively small number of kata. This is a strength. Rather than learning dozens of forms superficially, a Gōjū student spends many years with each one. The standard Okinawan syllabus contains twelve kata, often grouped into three families, as shown in Figure 1.3.

![**Figure 1.3** The three families of Gōjū-ryū kata. This book covers the two Gekisai kata and seven of the eight kaishu kata in detail. Sanchin and Tenshō are discussed in Chapter 2 as the foundation of everything else.](figures/fig_families.png)

### Heishugata: the closed-hand kata

*Heishu* means closed hand, and the heishugata are **Sanchin** and **Tenshō**. They are the foundation of the system. Sanchin, usually translated as "three battles" (or three conflicts: of mind, body and breath), is performed with the fists closed, in a rooted stance, with full-body dynamic tension and deep, audible breathing. It is the oldest kata in the system and was taught by Higaonna Kanryō. Tenshō, meaning "rotating palms", was created by Miyagi around 1921, based on Chinese material, and is the soft complement to Sanchin's hardness. The hands open, circle and flow, while the stance and breathing remain those of Sanchin.

Every other kata in Gōjū-ryū is built on these two. If your Sanchin is weak, every other kata will be weak, however pretty it looks. That is why Chapter 2 spends so long on stances and breathing.

### Fukyūgata: the promotional kata

*Fukyū* means promotion or popularisation. In 1940 the Okinawan prefectural authorities set up a committee to produce simple kata that could be taught in schools. Miyagi created **Gekisai Dai Ichi** and **Gekisai Dai Ni** for this purpose. (Nagamine Shōshin of the Matsubayashi-ryū created Fukyūgata Ichi at the same time; the kata known in Shōrin-ryū dojo as Fukyūgata Ni is a version of Gekisai Dai Ichi.)

The Gekisai kata are short, symmetrical and easy to remember. They introduce the basic stances, the basic blocks and strikes, and the basic idea of moving along an embusen. They are the first kata most Gōjū students learn, and Chapter 4 covers both of them.

### Kaishugata: the open-hand kata

*Kaishu* means open hand, and the kaishugata are the classical kata of the system, many of them believed to have been brought from China or developed from Chinese material by Higaonna Kanryō and Miyagi. They are:

- **Saifa** (Chapter 5)
- **Seiyunchin** (Chapter 6)
- **Shisochin** (Chapter 7)
- **Sanseiru** (not covered in this book)
- **Seisan** (Chapter 8)
- **Seipai** (Chapter 9)
- **Kururunfa** (Chapter 10)
- **Suparinpei** (Chapter 11)

Several of these names are numbers, read in a southern Chinese pronunciation. Sanseiru is thirty-six; Seisan is thirteen; Seipai is eighteen; Suparinpei is one hundred and eight. The numbers are tied up with Buddhist numerology, where multiples of three (and especially thirty-six and one hundred and eight) carry symbolic weight. Attempts to count thirteen techniques in Seisan or a hundred and eight in Suparinpei are entertaining but rarely convincing; the names are better understood as symbolic labels than as inventories. The meanings of the other names are discussed in each kata's own chapter.

You will notice that Sanseiru is missing from this book. That is purely a matter of space. It is a fine kata, usually taught around the first or second dan, and deserves the same treatment as the others. I hope to give it that treatment elsewhere. Readers whose syllabus places Sanseiru between Seisan and Seipai will find that almost everything in the chapters on those two kata applies to it as well.

## What the history means on the floor

All this would be mere scholarship if it did not change the way you train. It does, in at least five ways.

### First: the kata are for close-range fighting

Naha-te grew up in a busy port. Its techniques suit the kind of conflict that happens in crowded places at arm's length or closer: grabs, pushes, strikes to the head and body, attempts to drag someone to the ground. When you look at a Gōjū kata and see an open hand that seems to wave in the air, ask yourself what that hand would be doing if it were holding someone's wrist, or the back of their neck, or their sleeve. Very often the answer makes the movement make sense. Chapter 3 discusses this approach to bunkai in more detail.

### Second: the breath is the engine

The name of the style is a line about breathing. Gōjū-ryū is unique among the major karate styles in the emphasis it places on audible, deliberate breathing in kata. In some kata, such as Sanchin and the opening of Seiyunchin, the breath is long and dynamic. In others, such as Kururunfa, it is quiet and sudden. In all of them, the breath is not an accompaniment to the movement but the thing that organises it. A karateka who breathes well will move well; one who holds their breath or gasps will not.

### Third: hard and soft alternate

Once you understand that *gō* and *jū* are phases of one rhythm rather than two kinds of technique, you will start to see the pattern everywhere. A circular block that softly receives an attack and then firms into a grip. A stance that is relaxed in transition and suddenly rooted on arrival. A hand that is loose until the last few centimetres of a strike. In grading, examiners describe this as rhythm, or as the contrast between tension and relaxation. In practice, it is the difference between karate that looks alive and karate that looks like exercise.

### Fourth: the forms have been passed hand to hand

Because Gōjū kata have been transmitted by demonstration and correction, the details matter less than the principles, and your teacher matters more than any book, including this one. Use the diagrams and walkthroughs to understand what you have been shown and to remember it accurately. Do not use them to tell your teacher that they are doing it wrong.

### Fifth: a small syllabus is meant to be studied deeply

Miyagi did not create a hundred kata. He kept a small number and expected his students to spend years on each one. If you find yourself frustrated that you have been practising Saifa for two years and are not yet allowed to learn Seiyunchin, remember that the people who built this art would have considered two years with Saifa a reasonable beginning.

## The dojo kun and the spirit of practice

Many Gōjū dojo display a set of precepts, the *dōjō kun*, on the wall and recite them at the end of class. Wording varies from dojo to dojo, but they usually include something like: be humble and polite; train considering your physical strength; practise earnestly with creativity; be calm and swift; take care of your health; live a plain life; do not be too proud or too modest; continue your training with patience.

Students sometimes treat the dojo kun as a ritual to be mumbled before the bow. I would ask you to read the list again with the kata in mind. *Train considering your physical strength*: that is a warning not to force your knees into a shiko dachi they are not ready for. *Be calm and swift*: that is a description of good kata, in which the mind is quiet and the body fast. *Continue your training with patience*: that is the whole of this book in five words.

## How Gōjū-ryū relates to other styles

It is useful, finally, to place Gōjū-ryū among its neighbours, because many readers will have trained in another style before, or will train alongside people who have.

Compared with the Shuri-te derived styles (Shōrin-ryū, Shōtōkan and their descendants), Gōjū-ryū generally uses shorter stances, more circular blocks, more open-hand techniques and more grappling. Its kata contain fewer kicks, and those kicks are usually low. Its breathing is more audible and more central. Its conditioning methods are more developed. Shōtōkan karateka visiting a Gōjū dojo often remark that the stances feel strangely short and the hands strangely busy; Gōjū karateka visiting a Shōtōkan dojo often remark that the stances feel long and the hands rather far away from the opponent. Both observations are accurate, and both reflect different ideas about the distance at which a fight is likely to be decided.

Several other styles share Gōjū's roots in Naha-te. Uechi-ryū, founded by Uechi Kanbun, who also studied in Fuzhou, has a similar emphasis on Sanchin and conditioning. Tōon-ryū descends from another student of Higaonna Kanryō, Kyoda Jūhatsu. Itosu's student Mabuni Kenwa, who also trained with Higaonna, combined Naha-te and Shuri-te material to create Shitō-ryū, which is why Shitō-ryū practitioners know many Gōjū kata. If you train with a Shitō-ryū karateka you may find that their Seipai or Kururunfa looks familiar and unfamiliar at the same time: another branch of the same tree.

## A note of caution about stories

Before we leave history behind, a word about legends. Gōjū-ryū, like every martial art, has its share of wonderful stories: teachers who could break a board with a finger, who could not be moved when standing in Sanchin, who defeated a gang of attackers in a single breath. Some of these stories are probably true. Some are certainly exaggerated. A few are inventions. They serve a purpose, giving students something to aspire to, but they can also do harm, by suggesting that the art is about superhuman feats rather than steady, intelligent work.

When you hear a story about a famous karateka, enjoy it. Then go back to the floor and practise your stance. That is what the people in the stories actually did.

## Problems and solutions: history in the dojo

Even history causes practical problems in the dojo. These are the ones I meet most often.

**Problem: "My new club does the kata differently from my old one, and I am being marked as wrong."** This is extremely common when a student moves between associations. The solution is to learn the new club's version thoroughly, without abandoning the old one in your private practice if it means something to you. Grade in the version of the association that is grading you. Ask your new teacher, politely and privately, to show you the specific differences; most teachers are happy to do so and will respect you for asking.

**Problem: "I have read that a kata was created by someone different from the person my teacher named."** Kata origins are often disputed. Treat what your teacher tells you as the tradition of your dojo, and treat what you read as scholarship. They need not be in conflict. If you are interested, the further reading list at the back of this book is a good place to start.

**Problem: "I do not see the point of learning history when I could be training."** Fair enough, as far as it goes. But read the section above called "What the history means on the floor" again. If those five points change the way you practise even slightly, the time spent reading this chapter has paid for itself.

**Problem: "My teacher's account of the history differs from this chapter."** That is quite possible, especially on dates and on the details of Higaonna Kanryō's time in China. I have given the version that seems to me best supported, and I have tried to mark the uncertain points. Your teacher may have good reasons for their version, including information passed down within their lineage that has never been written down.

::: keypoints
### Key points

- Gōjū-ryū descends from Naha-te, the close-range, breath-centred karate of the port of Naha, shaped by contact with southern Chinese boxing.
- Higaonna Kanryō (1853–1915) taught Miyagi Chōjun (1888–1953), who systematised the art, created Tenshō and the Gekisai kata, and named the style around 1930.
- *Gō* and *jū*, hard and soft, are two phases of one rhythm, like breathing in and out, not two separate kinds of technique.
- The syllabus is small: two closed-hand kata, two promotional kata and eight open-hand kata. Each is meant to be studied for years.
- Variations between associations are the natural result of hand-to-hand transmission. Learn your teacher's version, and treat other versions with curiosity.
:::

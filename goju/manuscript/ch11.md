# Nage-waza: The Throws Hidden in the Kata

::: chapter-intro
Goju-Ryu is not usually thought of as a throwing art, yet its kata contain a remarkable number of throws and takedowns. They are hidden inside movements that look like blocks, turns and changes of stance, and they make sense only when you know that the other person is already in your hands. This chapter brings out the most practical of them, shows you how to perform them safely, and explains how each grows from the balance-breaking principles of the previous chapter.
:::

The first time someone showed me a throw from a Goju-Ryu kata, I was sceptical. I had performed Seiyunchin hundreds of times and never once thought of it as anything but a series of low-stance blocks and strikes. Then a visiting instructor took my arm, dropped into the low straddle stance from the kata, slipped his shoulder under my body and lifted me across his back. I landed on the mat with a thump and a new respect for the people who designed the form.

Since then, I have found throws in every one of the kaishu kata. Some are obvious once you see them; others take years to recognise. Not all the interpretations I have been shown pass the four bunkai tests from Chapter 1, but many do, and the best of them are as effective as anything taught in judo or wrestling.

Before we begin, a word of warning. Throws are dangerous. Throwing a training partner onto a hard floor, or throwing someone who does not know how to fall, can cause serious injury, particularly to the head, neck and shoulders. *Do not practise any throw in this chapter until both you and your partner can breakfall safely*, which is covered in Chapter 12. If you have not yet learned to breakfall, read Chapter 12 first and practise its drills for several weeks before attempting these throws. And always practise on proper mats.

## The Problem: Ending Up on the Floor

The problem throws solve is simple: you want the other person on the floor and yourself on your feet. On the street, a person on the floor cannot easily chase you, and you have time to escape. In sparring rules that allow throws, a clean throw scores and often ends the exchange.

But throws have their own problem: if they fail, you may end up on the floor yourself, often underneath the other person. And if you succeed but go down with them, you may find yourself in a ground fight you did not want, possibly on concrete, possibly with other people around.

This means that the best throws for Goju-Ryu purposes are those that keep you on your feet. Many judo throws involve the thrower falling with the person being thrown, which is fine on a mat in a competition but much less desirable on a pavement. The throws in this chapter have been chosen because, done correctly, they leave you standing.

## The Principle: Kuzushi, Entry, Execution

Every throw has three stages, and judo has names for each of them that are worth knowing.

*Kuzushi* is the balance-break, which we studied in Chapter 10. Before the throw can work, the other person's balance must be compromised.

*Tsukuri* is the entry, or fitting in. You move your body into the right position relative to the other person: your hips, your legs, your arms and your head all have to be in particular places for the throw to work.

*Kake* is the execution: the actual throwing action, in which you use the position you have created to send the other person to the ground.

In practice, these three stages overlap and happen very quickly. But when you are learning a throw, it helps to separate them. If a throw is not working, ask yourself which stage is failing. Usually it is the first: the other person was not off balance when you tried to throw them.

## Throw One: The Outer Reap

The outer reap is one of the simplest and most effective throws there is. It appears in judo as *osoto-gari*, and something very like it can be found in several Goju-Ryu kata, where a step forward behind the other person's leg is combined with a strike or push to the upper body.

### The movement

You are facing the other person at close range, with your left foot forward and your left hand controlling their right arm.

*Kuzushi.* Push their upper body back and to their right, so that their weight goes onto their right heel. The push can come from a palm under the chin, a forearm across the collarbone, or a pull on their right arm combined with a push on their left shoulder.

*Tsukuri.* Step forward with your left foot to the outside of their right foot, so that your left leg is level with theirs. Your weight is on your left leg. Your right leg is free.

*Kake.* Swing your right leg forward past their right leg, then back, sweeping your thigh against the back of their right thigh. At the same time, drive their upper body backwards and down. Their right leg is taken away just as their weight is pushed onto it, and they fall backward.

You remain standing on your left leg. As they fall, you can control their arm to slow their fall in training, or release them and move away.

![**Figure 11.1** The outer reap. (1) Push the opponent's head back so their weight goes onto their heel. (2) Step in beside their leg. (3) Reap their leg away with the back of your thigh as you drive the upper body down. You remain standing.](figures/fig11_1.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: Saifa and Seisan**

Both Saifa and Seisan contain movements in which a step forward is combined with a strike or push to head height, often with the other hand pulling back to the hip. Read as an outer reap, the step places your leg beside or behind the other person's, the high hand drives their head back, and the pulling hand controls their arm. The kick that follows in some versions can be understood as the reaping leg itself.
:::

### Common errors

The most common error is trying to reap the leg before the other person's weight is on it. If their weight is on their other foot, you will simply lift their leg and they will hop away. Make sure the kuzushi has put their weight on the leg you are reaping.

The second error is reaping with the foot rather than the thigh. A foot sweep at this distance has little power. Reap with the back of your thigh against the back of theirs, close to the knee.

The third error is leaning back as you reap, which takes your weight away from the throw. Keep your upper body forward, driving into them.

## Throw Two: The Shoulder Wheel

The shoulder wheel, *kata guruma* in judo, is the throw I mentioned at the start of this chapter. It is one of the most commonly cited applications of Seiyunchin, and it is extremely effective when done well.

### The movement

You are facing the other person, and you have hold of their right arm with your left hand.

*Kuzushi.* Pull their right arm forward and up, drawing them towards you and onto their toes.

*Tsukuri.* Drop into a low straddle stance, sliding your head under their right armpit and your right arm between their legs, wrapping around the back of their right thigh. Your right shoulder is in their abdomen. Your body is low, and your back is straight.

*Kake.* Stand up by driving through your legs, lifting them across your shoulders. Pull their arm across your body with your left hand and lift their leg with your right, so that they rotate over your shoulders. Turn and drop them to the floor beside you.

This throw involves lifting, and it requires good technique to do safely. The lift must come from the legs, with the back straight. Do not attempt it with a partner much heavier than you until you have mastered it with lighter partners.

### A safer variation

A much safer variation, suitable for most training and more useful in many real situations, is the *shoulder wheel takedown*. Instead of lifting the person across your shoulders, you enter in the same way but, instead of standing up, you drive forward and turn, pulling their arm and driving their leg so that they topple sideways to the floor. You never lift them off the ground, which is much safer for both of you.

![**Figure 11.2** The shoulder wheel from Seiyunchin. (1) Pull the opponent's arm forward and up. (2) Drop into a low straddle stance with your shoulder under their body and your arm behind their thigh. (3) Drive up and turn, rotating them across your shoulders.](figures/fig11_2.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: Seiyunchin**

Seiyunchin is performed almost entirely in shiko dachi, the low straddle stance, with one arm reaching high and the other low in several of its key movements. As a block, the high–low arm position is hard to explain. As a shoulder wheel, it is exactly the position you need: one hand holding the other person's arm high, the other arm wrapped around their leg low, your stance dropped beneath their centre of gravity.
:::

## Throw Three: The Head Twist

The head-twist takedown uses the principle from Chapter 10 that where the head goes, the body follows. By turning the other person's head sharply, you turn their whole body and topple them.

### The movement

You are in close range, with your right hand able to reach the back of the other person's head and your left hand able to reach their chin.

*Kuzushi and tsukuri.* Place your left palm on their chin and your right hand on the back of their head. Step forward with your right foot slightly to their left side.

*Kake.* Turn their head as if you were turning a steering wheel: chin pushed to their right and up, back of the head pulled to their left and down. Step around in a circle to your right as you turn. Their body follows the head and they topple to the floor.

This must be done slowly and very carefully in training, because neck injuries are serious. The turn should be a smooth rotation of the whole body, not a sharp twist of the neck. In training, your partner should begin to fall as soon as they feel the rotation, rather than resisting it.

::: street
**Street note: neck techniques**

Any technique that twists the neck can cause serious injury or death. In a real self-defence situation, a head-twist takedown may be justified against a serious threat, but it should be applied as a turn of the whole body rather than a twist of the neck, and released as soon as the person is falling. In training, it must always be performed slowly and with complete cooperation.
:::

## Throw Four: The Arm-Bar Takedown

The arm-bar takedown uses pressure on a straightened arm to drive the other person face down to the floor. It is one of the most useful takedowns for self-defence and restraint, because it leaves you standing over a controlled person, and it links directly to the joint-control work of Part Five.

### The movement

The other person reaches for you with their right hand, perhaps grabbing your clothing or throwing a punch.

*Kuzushi and tsukuri.* Take their right wrist with your right hand and pull it forward along its line of travel, straightening their arm. Step forward with your left foot to their right side, so that you are beside their right arm, facing the same way as them. Place your left forearm or palm on the back of their right arm, just above the elbow.

*Kake.* Press down on their elbow with your left arm while you maintain the pull on their wrist with your right. Their arm is straight, and the pressure on the elbow forces their shoulder down. Step forward in a circle as you press, so that they spiral down to the floor face first.

Once they are on the floor, you can maintain control of the arm, as we will discuss in Chapter 14, or release them and move away.

![**Figure 11.3** The arm-bar takedown. (1) Take the wrist and draw the arm straight. (2) Step beside them and place your forearm above their elbow. (3) Press down and circle forward; they spiral to the floor face first.](figures/fig11_3.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: Shisochin**

The opening sequence of Shisochin contains a repeated pressing, opening movement of the hands, followed later by a turning movement in which one arm presses down while the other pulls. Many instructors read parts of this as an arm-bar takedown, with the open hands seizing the wrist and the pressing arm driving the elbow down. Practise it slowly with a partner and see whether you agree.
:::

## Throw Five: The Inner Sweep

The inner sweep is a foot sweep that takes away the other person's leg from the inside. It is useful in sparring, where many rule sets allow sweeps, and it is simple enough to work under pressure.

### The movement

You are facing the other person with your left foot forward, and their left foot is forward too.

*Kuzushi.* Pull or push their upper body so that their weight shifts onto their left foot. The easiest way is to pull their right arm towards you, which turns them and puts weight on their left leg.

*Kake.* With the sole of your left foot, sweep the inside of their left ankle outward and forward, in the direction their weight is moving. At the same time, continue the pull on their arm. Their foot slides away and they fall.

The sweep must be timed to the moment their weight arrives on the swept foot, or just before. Too early, and the foot is too heavy to move; too late, and they have stepped. Sweeps are all about timing.

## Throw Six: The Leg Catch

The leg catch is a defence against a kick that turns into a takedown. It is useful in sparring against round kicks and front kicks, and in self-defence against a kick to the body.

### The movement

The other person throws a right round kick to your body.

*Tsukuri.* Step slightly towards the kick to jam it before it develops full power, and scoop the kicking leg with your left arm, trapping it against your side. Your right hand comes up to protect your head.

*Kuzushi and kake.* Lift the trapped leg slightly, which puts all their weight on their standing leg and makes them hop. Then either sweep the standing leg with your right foot, or step forward and push their upper body backward with your right hand, so that they fall back.

This is a very effective technique, but it is also very dangerous for the person being thrown, because they fall backwards with one leg held and cannot breakfall properly. In training, release the leg as they fall, or support their fall.

## Throw Seven: The Turning Throw

The turning throw uses a circular movement of your whole body to swing the other person off their feet. It is the throw most directly linked to the many turns in the Goju-Ryu kata.

### The movement

You have hold of the other person, perhaps with one hand on their arm and the other on their shoulder or collar.

*Kuzushi.* Push them slightly, and as they push back, step back and begin to turn. Your pull, combined with their push, draws them forward and around.

*Tsukuri and kake.* Continue turning, pivoting on one foot so that your body rotates through ninety degrees or more. As you turn, drop your weight slightly and pull them around you in a circle. Their feet cannot keep up with their upper body, and they are swung off balance and down to the floor, often landing on the side or back.

The turning throw requires no strength at all if the timing is right. It uses the momentum of both bodies and the centrifugal effect of the turn. It is also very safe for the thrower, because you never turn your back on the other person or go to the floor yourself.

![**Figure 11.4** The turning throw, seen from above. (1) You hold the opponent's arm and shoulder. (2) As they push, you pivot back on your lead foot, drawing them forward. (3) Continuing the turn, you swing them around you and down.](figures/fig11_4.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: the turns**

Almost every Goju-Ryu kata contains turns, and many of them are combined with grabbing or pulling hand movements. Seipai, Kururunfa, Shisochin, Seisan and Suparinpei all have turns that can be read as turning throws. When you practise these kata, imagine that you are holding someone as you turn, and feel how your turn would swing them.
:::

## Training Throws Safely

Throwing practice has a higher injury risk than almost anything else in this book. These are the safety rules I insist on in my own classes.

*Mats.* Never throw on a hard floor. Use proper judo-style mats, at least four centimetres thick.

*Breakfalls first.* Both partners must be able to breakfall competently before throwing practice begins. See Chapter 12.

*Control the fall.* As the thrower, keep hold of your partner's arm or clothing as they fall, so that you can slow their descent and keep their head off the mat. This is called *supporting the fall*, and it is the thrower's responsibility.

*Start slowly.* Learn each throw at walking pace, with your partner cooperating fully, before adding any speed or resistance.

*No neck techniques at speed.* Head twists and any throw that involves pressure on the neck must always be done slowly.

*Space.* Make sure there is enough space around you. Throws can carry people further than you expect.

*Stop on request.* If either partner says stop, stop.

::: drill
**Drill 11.1: Uchikomi (fitting practice)**

Choose one throw. With a cooperative partner, practise only the kuzushi and tsukuri: break their balance and fit into position, then stop without throwing. Return to the start and repeat. Do twenty repetitions on each side.

This drill, called *uchikomi* in judo, builds the entry movement without the strain of throwing. It is how judoka learn throws, and it is a safe and efficient way to develop yours. Do it for every throw in this chapter before you start throwing.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 11.2: Throw on the reaction**

Your partner pushes you gently with both hands. You respond with a push–pull: let them push, then pull them forward as they push and enter into a throw of your choice. Begin with the turning throw, which suits a pushing partner perfectly.

Then reverse: your partner pulls you, and you respond by stepping in and pushing as they pull, entering into an outer reap.

This drill links the throws to the action–reaction principle and teaches you to throw on what you feel rather than on what you planned.
:::

## Throws in Sparring

Different rule sets treat throws very differently. Many traditional karate competitions allow foot sweeps and takedowns if followed immediately by a controlled strike. Some knockdown and mixed-rules formats allow a wide range of throws. Most sport karate rule sets prohibit throws that involve lifting or that drop the opponent on their head.

If your club allows throws in sparring, start with foot sweeps and the turning throw, which are safe and effective. Add the outer reap with care. Leave the shoulder wheel and the head twist for controlled drills only.

When you throw someone in sparring, follow up with a controlled strike, usually a punch stopped short of the head, to show that you could have finished the exchange. Then step back and let them get up.

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My throws only work on beginners."

**Solution:** Beginners do not know how to resist, so you can throw them without kuzushi. Experienced partners do, so you cannot. Go back to Chapter 10 and work on breaking balance through action and reaction. Then practise uchikomi until your entry is fast and smooth. Most throws fail at the kuzushi stage.

**Problem:** "I end up on the floor with my partner."

**Solution:** You are committing your own balance too far into the throw. Keep your weight over your own feet, particularly your standing leg in the outer reap and the inner sweep. Let the other person fall; do not fall with them. Practise the throws slowly and check where your weight is at the end.

**Problem:** "The shoulder wheel hurts my back."

**Solution:** You are lifting with your back rather than your legs, or your partner is too heavy. Use the takedown variation instead of the full lift, and work on your squat strength separately. If you have any history of back problems, do not attempt the full shoulder wheel.

**Problem:** "My sweeps never work."

**Solution:** It is almost always timing. Sweep the foot as the weight arrives on it, not when it is fully loaded or after it has left. Practise with a partner who walks slowly towards you, and sweep each foot just as it touches the floor. Once you can feel that moment, add a pull to bring their weight onto the foot.
:::

## Bringing It Together

The Goju-Ryu kata are full of throws, and once you have learned to see them, the kata take on a new depth. The outer reap, the shoulder wheel, the head twist, the arm-bar takedown, the inner sweep, the leg catch and the turning throw give you a practical repertoire for both sparring and self-defence.

Every throw rests on kuzushi. If you break the balance first, the throw is easy. If you do not, it is a contest of strength. Learn the entries through uchikomi, train them on reactions, and always practise safely.

The next chapter covers the other side of throwing: how to fall without being hurt, how to survive on the ground, and how to get back to your feet as quickly as possible.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Every throw has three stages: kuzushi (balance-breaking), tsukuri (entry) and kake (execution). When a throw fails, check the kuzushi first.
- For Goju-Ryu purposes, the best throws leave you standing.
- The outer reap takes away a leg that is carrying the weight, while the upper body is driven back.
- The shoulder wheel from Seiyunchin is powerful but demanding; the takedown variation is safer.
- The head twist and arm-bar takedown use the head and arm as handles; both demand great care in training.
- Sweeps are all about timing: catch the foot as the weight arrives.
- The turning throw uses the turns of the kata to swing the other person around you.
- Never practise throws without mats and competent breakfalls.
:::

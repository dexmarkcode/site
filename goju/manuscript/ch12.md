# Ukemi and Ground Survival: Falling Well and Getting Back Up

::: chapter-intro
If you train close combat, you will fall. You will be thrown in the dojo, you may slip on ice or be pushed in the street, and one day you may go to the ground in a struggle you did not choose. Knowing how to fall without injury, how to protect yourself once you are down, and how to get back to your feet quickly are among the most valuable skills in this book. They are also the most often neglected.
:::

Of all the skills I have learned in martial arts, the one that has protected me most often has nothing to do with fighting. It is breakfalling. I have slipped on wet steps, been knocked off a bicycle, tripped over a dog lead in the dark and fallen down a short flight of stairs carrying a box. Each time, my body did what it had been trained to do thousands of times on the mat: tucked the chin, rounded the back, spread the impact, and rolled. Each time, I walked away with a bruise rather than a broken wrist or a head injury.

Falls are one of the leading causes of injury in everyday life, particularly as people get older. A skill that teaches you to fall safely is worth learning for that reason alone, whatever your interest in martial arts. For close combat, it is essential. You cannot practise throws safely without it, and in a real confrontation the ground is where many people are seriously hurt.

## The Problem: The Ground Is Hard

The danger of going to the ground comes from three sources.

The first is the *impact itself*. A fall from standing height onto a hard surface generates a great deal of force. If that force is concentrated on a small area, such as an outstretched wrist, an elbow, a shoulder or the back of the head, the result can be a fracture, a dislocation or a serious head injury. Head injuries from falls onto concrete are one of the most common causes of death in street violence. Many of them happen not from a punch but from the fall that follows it.

The second is the *position you end up in*. On the ground, you are much more vulnerable to kicks, stamps and being held down. If there are other people involved, the ground is a very dangerous place to be.

The third is the *difficulty of getting up*. Getting up from the ground in a hurry, especially while someone is trying to keep you down or strike you, is surprisingly hard. Many people, trying to stand up, expose their heads and become even more vulnerable.

## The Principle: Spread, Roll, Protect

Safe falling rests on three principles.

*Spread the impact.* The force of a fall is the same whatever you do; what you can change is how it is distributed. Spread it over as large an area of your body as possible, especially the large, padded areas such as the side of the thigh, the buttocks and the back of the shoulders, rather than concentrating it on a joint or the head.

*Roll with the force.* A body that rolls converts some of the downward force into rotation, reducing the impact. A body that lands flat and stops absorbs all of it at once.

*Protect the head.* Above all, keep your head off the ground. Tuck your chin to your chest so that the back of your head cannot strike the floor. This single habit prevents more serious injuries than any other.

There is also a fourth principle, specific to close combat: *do not reach out with a straight arm*. The instinct when falling is to put a hand out to catch yourself. On a hard surface, a straight arm catching your whole body weight is a common cause of broken wrists, elbows and collarbones. Breakfall training replaces this instinct with a better one.

## The Basic Breakfalls

There are four basic breakfalls, known in Japanese as *ukemi*. Learn them in order, and practise them on mats until they are automatic.

### The back breakfall (ushiro ukemi)

The back breakfall is used when you fall straight backwards.

Start by sitting on the mat with your legs out in front of you and your arms crossed in front of your chest. Tuck your chin firmly to your chest. Roll backwards onto your rounded back, and as your shoulders touch the mat, strike the mat with both arms, palms down, at about forty-five degrees from your body. Your head must not touch the mat. Roll forward again and repeat.

When this is comfortable, progress to a squatting position, then to a standing position, lowering yourself into a squat as you fall back so that you roll rather than drop.

The arm slap is not essential for protection, but it helps spread the impact and, more importantly, trains you to keep your arms out of harm's way. Instead of reaching back to catch yourself, your arms strike the mat beside you.

![**Figure 12.1** The back breakfall. (1) From a squat, chin tucked. (2) Roll back onto the rounded spine. (3) As the shoulders touch, both arms strike the mat at about forty-five degrees. The head never touches the floor.](figures/fig12_1.png)

### The side breakfall (yoko ukemi)

The side breakfall is used when you fall to the side, which is how most throws land you.

Start by lying on your back with your legs raised slightly and your chin tucked. Roll onto your left side, and as you do, strike the mat with your left arm, palm down, at about forty-five degrees from your body. Your legs should be slightly apart, with the left leg straighter and the right leg bent, so that you land on the side of your thigh and buttock rather than on your hip bone. Roll onto your right side and repeat.

Progress to falling from a squat, then from standing, by sliding one leg across in front of the other and lowering yourself to the side.

### The forward roll (mae mawari ukemi)

The forward roll is used when you are thrown or fall forward with momentum. It is the most useful breakfall in daily life, because it lets you roll through a forward fall and come back up to your feet.

Start in a kneeling position with your right knee up and your left knee on the mat. Place your right hand on the mat in front of you, fingers pointing back towards you, and your left hand beside it. Look over your left shoulder. Roll forward over your right arm, shoulder and diagonally across your back to your left hip, keeping your body rounded throughout. Your head should not touch the mat. As you complete the roll, either come up to a kneeling or standing position, or finish in a side breakfall.

The forward roll takes some time to learn. Go slowly, and have an experienced person check that you are rolling across the diagonal of your back rather than straight over your spine or your head.

### The forward breakfall (mae ukemi)

The forward breakfall is used when you fall straight forward without the possibility of rolling.

Start kneeling. Fall forward, and as you do, bring your forearms down in front of you so that they strike the mat together, from elbows to palms, forming a triangle with your hands near your face. Turn your head to one side. Your forearms and the balls of your feet take the impact, and your body stays off the mat.

This breakfall needs particular care to avoid jarring the wrists or elbows. Practise from kneeling for several weeks before trying it from a crouch.

![**Figure 12.2** The side breakfall (left) and forward roll (right). In the side breakfall, the arm strikes the mat as the side of the thigh and buttock land. In the forward roll, the body rolls diagonally from the shoulder to the opposite hip, with the head clear of the floor.](figures/fig12_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill 12.1: Daily breakfalls**

Five minutes of breakfall practice at the start of every session: ten back breakfalls, ten side breakfalls each side, ten forward rolls each side, and five forward breakfalls. Begin from low positions and gradually progress to standing as your confidence grows.

Breakfalls are like scales for a musician. They are not exciting, but without them nothing else works. After a year of daily practice, they will be automatic.
:::

## Falling Outside the Dojo

Breakfalls on a mat are one thing. Falls in the real world are another. The ground is hard, you are wearing clothes and shoes, you may be carrying things, and the fall is unexpected.

The principles stay the same, but the emphasis changes.

On hard surfaces, *rolling is better than slapping*. Slapping concrete with your palm hurts and can injure the hand. Instead, aim to roll through the fall, spreading the impact along your arm, shoulder and back.

*Tuck the chin always.* This is the single most important habit. On concrete, the back of the head striking the ground is a potentially fatal injury.

*Keep the arms bent.* If you must use your arms, keep them bent and use them to guide yourself into a roll, not to stop yourself dead.

*Protect your face* when falling forward, by turning your head and taking the impact on bent forearms.

For older readers, or anyone with fragile bones, breakfall practice should be gentle and progressive, but it is especially valuable. Many people find that even a few months of practice changes their confidence and their instinctive reaction to a trip or slip.

## On the Ground

Despite your best efforts, you may end up on the ground in a confrontation. When that happens, your priorities change completely.

### Priority one: protect the head

If you are on the ground and the other person is standing, they can kick and stamp on you. The head is the most vulnerable target. Keep your arms up around your head, your knees up and your feet towards them, so that they have to get past your legs to reach your head.

### Priority two: keep them at bay with your legs

On your back, with your knees bent and your feet towards the other person, you can use your legs to keep them away. Turn on your back so that your feet always face them. If they come close, kick at their knees and shins. This is sometimes called the *ground guard* or *defensive position*.

### Priority three: get up safely

You must get up as soon as you can, but you must do it safely. Getting up in the wrong way exposes your head to kicks and lets the other person push you back down.

## Getting Up Safely: The Technical Stand-Up

The technical stand-up, sometimes called the tactical get-up, is the safest way to get from the ground to your feet when someone is near you. It is taught in many martial arts and in police and military training, and it is worth knowing.

From sitting on the ground, facing the other person:

1. Post your right hand on the floor behind you, beside your right hip.
2. Plant your left foot flat on the floor, knee bent.
3. Raise your left hand in front of your face as a guard, or use it to keep the other person at a distance.
4. Push off your right hand and left foot to lift your hips off the floor.
5. Swing your right leg back underneath you, through the gap between your right hand and left foot, and place it on the floor behind you.
6. You are now in a low, balanced stance, facing the other person, with your guard up. Stand up and step back.

The beauty of the technical stand-up is that at every stage your head is back, your guard is up, and your legs are between you and the other person. You never turn your back on them or put your head forward.

![**Figure 12.4** The technical stand-up. (1) Post one hand behind you and plant the opposite foot. (2) Lift the hips. (3) Swing the posted-side leg back under you. (4) Arrive in a guarded stance, facing the opponent.](figures/fig12_4.png)

::: drill
**Drill 12.2: Get-up circuits**

Lie on your back. On the signal, perform a technical stand-up, step back into a guard, then lie back down. Ten repetitions on each side.

Progress: a partner stands near you holding a kick shield. Each time you get up, they push the shield gently towards you, and you must keep your guard up and your feet under you. Later, the partner may try to push you back down with the shield, and you must use the defensive position and the stand-up to get to your feet safely.
:::

## Ground Work in Goju-Ryu

Goju-Ryu is not a ground-fighting system. There is very little ground work in the traditional syllabus, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. If you want to learn to fight on the ground, you should train with people who specialise in it, such as judoka, wrestlers or Brazilian jiu-jitsu practitioners.

However, there are some things a Goju-Ryu practitioner should know about the ground, and they follow directly from the principles of this book.

*Structure still matters.* On the ground, as on your feet, good alignment and connecting tension let you resist pressure and generate force. Your elbows should stay close to your body, your chin tucked, your spine strong.

*Frames still work.* A forearm across someone's neck or collarbone, with your elbow anchored on the floor or your own body, creates space and stops them pressing down on you.

*Strikes still work*, though they are shorter and weaker. Elbows, knees, headbutts and short punches can all be used from the ground.

*Escape is still the priority.* On the street, the aim on the ground is always to get back up and away. Do not try to win a ground fight; try to end it by getting up.

### When you are underneath

If someone is on top of you, the most important thing is to stop them striking your head and to create enough space to escape. Bring your arms up to protect your head, keep your elbows tight to your body, and try to get your knees between you and them. A *bridge* (driving your hips up explosively from the floor) combined with a *roll* to one side can often dislodge someone sitting on you, especially if you trap one of their arms on that side first.

### When you are on top

If you have taken someone down and are on top of them, you are in a position of advantage, but also of considerable legal responsibility. A person on the ground is generally much less of a threat than a person on their feet. Striking someone who is on the ground, particularly around the head, is very difficult to justify as reasonable force unless they remain a serious danger. In most self-defence situations, the right thing to do once you have someone on the ground is to get up and leave.

::: street
**Street note: the ground and the law**

Many of the most serious injuries in street violence happen to people on the ground. Kicking or stamping on someone who is down, particularly to the head, can kill. In the United Kingdom, continuing to attack someone who is no longer a threat is very likely to fall outside reasonable self-defence. If you have taken someone down, use that moment to escape. If you have been taken down, your priority is to protect your head and get up.
:::

::: kata
**Kata link: Kururunfa and Suparinpei**

Although Goju-Ryu kata are performed standing, several contain dropping movements in which the body sinks very low, sometimes almost to kneeling. Kururunfa contains a movement in which the body drops while the arms circle and press, and Suparinpei has deep stance changes with downward strikes. Some practitioners read these as applications against someone who has been taken to the ground, such as a downward strike or a control maintained as you descend with them. They are also a reminder that the kata assume you may need to change level dramatically.
:::

## Falling with a Partner

There is one more falling skill that is especially relevant to close combat: falling *with* someone. When two people are clinched together and one is thrown, the thrower may be pulled down too. When someone grabs you and falls, they may take you with them.

The key skills are:

*Let go if you can.* If you feel yourself being pulled down, release your grip and step away, unless holding on is protecting you.

*Land on top if you must go down.* If you cannot avoid going down, try to turn so that the other person lands first and you land on them, which cushions your fall.

*Do not put your hands out.* Even when falling with someone, the instinct to catch yourself with a straight arm is dangerous.

*Get up immediately.* As soon as you land, get up using the technical stand-up or a quick roll to your feet.

::: fault
**Fault: the reaching hand**

The most common fault in breakfall practice is reaching back or down with a straight arm to catch the fall. This is the instinct that causes broken wrists and dislocated elbows.

*Correction:* Practise the back breakfall from sitting with a towel held between your hands across your chest. When you roll back, release the towel and slap the mat. Your hands are kept busy with the towel until the right moment, which retrains the instinct.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "I'm frightened of falling."

**Solution:** That is very common, especially for adults who have not fallen deliberately since childhood. Start from the lowest positions, sitting and lying, and progress very slowly. Use thick mats. Practise for a few minutes every session rather than for long periods occasionally. Fear reduces with familiarity, and most people find that within a few months the fear has largely gone.

**Problem:** "I keep hitting my head on the back breakfall."

**Solution:** You are not tucking your chin firmly enough, or you are falling straight back rather than rolling. Practise from sitting, with your chin pressed to your chest and your eyes looking at your belt knot throughout. If you can see your belt, your head is safe.

**Problem:** "The forward roll hurts my shoulder."

**Solution:** You are probably landing on the point of your shoulder rather than rolling across the back of it, or your arm is collapsing. Keep your leading arm curved, like a wheel, and roll along its outside edge onto the back of the shoulder blade. Ask an experienced person to check your technique.

**Problem:** "I'm older and worried about injury."

**Solution:** Breakfall practice is especially valuable as you get older, because it improves your reaction to everyday falls. But progress gently. Stay with the low positions for longer, use thick mats, and avoid forward rolls and forward breakfalls if you have shoulder, neck or wrist problems. Even sitting back breakfalls and side rolls give real benefit. Check with your doctor if you have osteoporosis or any similar condition.
:::

## Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Four. We have studied balance-breaking, the throws hidden in the kata, and the skills of falling, ground survival and getting up.

These last skills are not glamorous, but they may be the most important in the book. Throws are useless if you cannot practise them safely, and the ground is where many people suffer the worst injuries. Learn to fall well. Learn to protect your head. Learn to get up safely. And remember that on the street, the ground is a place to leave as quickly as possible.

In Part Five, we turn to joint controls and restraint: the techniques that let you control another person without striking them.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Breakfalling protects you in daily life as well as in close combat. It is worth learning for that reason alone.
- Spread the impact, roll with the force, and above all protect the head by tucking the chin.
- Never reach out with a straight arm to catch a fall.
- Learn the back, side, forward roll and forward breakfalls in that order, from low positions first.
- On hard surfaces, roll rather than slap.
- On the ground, protect the head, keep your feet towards the other person, and get up as soon as it is safe.
- Use the technical stand-up to rise with your guard up and your head back.
- On the street, the ground is a place to leave, not a place to fight.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Practise Drill 12.1 every training day for a month. Note how your confidence changes from week one to week four.
2. Perform the technical stand-up slowly with your eyes closed. Which stage feels least stable? Why?
3. Think about the surfaces you walk on every day: pavements, stairs, car parks, shop floors. Which would be most dangerous to fall on? How would you adapt your breakfall to each?

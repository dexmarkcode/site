# Hiji-ate: The Elbow at Close Range

::: chapter-intro
The elbow is one of the hardest, heaviest and most reliable weapons the human body possesses. It needs almost no room to work, it is very difficult to injure, and it can be thrown in at least six directions from positions where a punch would be useless. In Goju-Ryu the elbow appears throughout the kata, often disguised as something else. This chapter teaches the main elbow strikes, the mechanics that give them power, the targets they suit, and the way they fit into the trapping and combination work of the previous chapters.
:::

There is an old saying in some Okinawan circles that the fist is for the young and the elbow is for the old. I first heard it from a teacher in his seventies who could no longer close his hands fully because of arthritis, but whose elbows were frighteningly effective. He was not saying that young people should not use their elbows. He was saying that the elbow is the weapon that remains when strength, speed and flexibility have begun to fade, because it relies on structure and position rather than athleticism.

There is another reason the elbow deserves special attention. It is the natural weapon of the close range. When the distance closes and your forearms are folded between you and the other person, a punch has no room to develop. An elbow, however, is already in position. You simply turn your body and it arrives.

## The Problem: No Room to Punch

At close range, punching becomes difficult. A straight punch needs about the length of your arm to reach full power. At elbow distance, there is perhaps half that, and at chest-to-chest distance there is almost none. Many people, when they find themselves this close, try to punch anyway. The punches come out short, weak and smothered, and the other person barely notices them.

Hooks and uppercuts help, because they are shorter and curved, but they still need some room. And they put your hand, with all its small, breakable bones, into contact with hard parts of the other person's head.

What you need is a weapon that is strong, that needs very little space, and that is unlikely to break when it hits something hard. That is the elbow.

## The Principle: Short Lever, Whole Body

The elbow works on a simple mechanical principle. When you punch, your fist is at the end of a long lever, your arm, and much of the force is lost along the way through the wrist, elbow and shoulder. When you strike with the elbow, the striking surface is much closer to your body. The lever is short, so very little force is lost, and the whole mass of your body is directly behind it.

This has an important consequence: *elbow power comes from the body, not the arm*. A good elbow strike is driven by the turning of the hips and shoulders, the dropping or rising of the body, or the step of the feet. The arm itself is just the shape that carries the elbow into position. If you try to throw an elbow with arm strength, it will be weak. If you turn your whole body behind it, it will be devastating.

### The striking surface

There are several parts of the elbow and forearm that can be used for striking, and it is worth being precise.

The *point of the elbow* is the bony tip, the olecranon. This is the hardest and most concentrated surface, used for downward and backward elbows, and for some rising elbows.

The *forearm side* just below the point is broader and slightly softer. It is used for horizontal and some upward elbows, and it is more forgiving if your aim is slightly off.

The *upper-arm side* just above the point is used for some backward and sideways elbows, particularly when the arm is folded tightly.

As a general rule, strike with the area within a few centimetres of the point. It is the strongest and least likely to be injured.

### Protecting your own head

One great advantage of the elbow is that, when thrown correctly, it protects your own head. The striking arm is folded, with the hand close to your head or ear, so the forearm and upper arm form a shield on that side. The other arm should also be up, guarding the other side. This makes the elbow one of the safest strikes to throw at close range, provided you keep your guard up.

## The Six Elbows

I divide elbow strikes into six main directions. You will find different names and classifications in different schools, but these six cover almost everything.

### 1. The horizontal elbow (yoko mawashi hiji-ate)

The horizontal elbow travels sideways across the body, like a hook punch, with the forearm roughly parallel to the floor. It is usually aimed at the jaw, the temple or the side of the head.

To throw it, raise your arm so that your elbow is at about shoulder height, with your hand near your ear or the side of your head. Turn your hips and shoulders sharply towards the target, and let the elbow travel across in a flat arc. The striking surface is the forearm just below the point.

This is the most common and versatile elbow. It can be thrown with either arm, from either the lead or rear side, and it fits naturally after a trap that has opened the side of the head.

### 2. The rising elbow (tate hiji-ate or age hiji-ate)

The rising elbow travels upwards, with the elbow driving towards the chin or the underside of the jaw. It is the close-range equivalent of an uppercut.

To throw it, drop your weight slightly, then drive upwards from the legs as you lift the elbow vertically, with your hand passing close to your own ear. The striking surface is the point or the forearm just below it.

The rising elbow is especially effective when the other person's head is coming forward or down, for example when they are trying to grab you round the waist, or when you have pulled their head down. It also comes up through the gap between their arms, which makes it hard to block.

### 3. The downward elbow (otoshi hiji-ate)

The downward elbow drops vertically onto the target, usually the back of the head, the back of the neck, the spine or the collarbone of someone who is bent forward. The striking surface is the point of the elbow.

To throw it, raise your elbow high, then drop your whole body weight behind it, bending your knees as the elbow falls. The power comes from gravity and the drop of the body, not from arm strength.

This elbow is mainly useful when the other person is bent over, for example after a strike to the body, after you have pulled their head down, or when they are trying to tackle you. In sparring it is almost always prohibited, because the targets are very dangerous.

### 4. The forward elbow (mae hiji-ate)

The forward elbow drives straight forward, with the point of the elbow leading, usually into the chest, the solar plexus or the face. It is sometimes thrown with a step, so that the whole body moves behind it.

To throw it, bend your arm fully so that your fist is near your shoulder, then step or lean forward, driving the point of the elbow straight ahead. The other hand often supports the striking arm by pressing on the fist, which adds power and stability.

This elbow appears in several kata as a supported strike, with one hand pressing behind the other. It is very powerful at chest-to-chest range.

### 5. The backward elbow (ushiro hiji-ate)

The backward elbow drives straight back, usually into the abdomen, ribs or face of someone behind you. The striking surface is the point.

To throw it, look over your shoulder at the target, then drive your elbow straight back as you turn slightly towards it, often with a small step backwards. The other hand can push the fist to add power.

This is mainly a defence against attacks from behind, such as bear hugs and grabs, and we will come back to it in Chapter 15. It also has uses when you have moved to the side of someone and they are slightly behind you.

### 6. The diagonal elbow

The diagonal elbow travels on a slant, either downwards across the body from high to low, or upwards across the body from low to high. It is a variation of the horizontal elbow and is often aimed at the collarbone, the side of the neck or the jaw.

The downward diagonal elbow, in particular, is very effective at chest-to-chest range, because it comes over the top of the other person's arms and is hard to see.

::: drill
**Drill 7.1: Six-direction elbows on pads**

Your partner holds a focus mitt or a Thai pad firmly against their body or in front of their face. Work through each of the six elbows in turn, ten repetitions each side, starting slowly and building to full power. Concentrate on driving each elbow with your body: turn for the horizontal, rise for the rising, drop for the downward, step for the forward, turn back for the backward.

Your partner should feel the difference between an elbow thrown with the arm and one thrown with the body. Ask them to tell you which is which.
:::

## Targets for the Elbow

Because the elbow is so hard and so powerful, the choice of target matters a great deal. Some targets are suitable for training and sparring with protection; others are dangerous and should be reserved for genuine self-defence. I will be honest about which is which.

The *jaw* is the classic target for horizontal and rising elbows. A strike to the side of the jaw can cause a knockout by rotating the head sharply. In training, use pads; in sparring with full head protection and appropriate rules, very light horizontal elbows to the side of the head guard can be allowed.

The *temple* is a very dangerous target. Elbows to the temple can cause serious injury and should never be thrown at any force in training.

The *nose and face* are vulnerable to forward and rising elbows. A strike here causes pain, watering eyes and a strong flinch, which makes it a good opening strike in self-defence.

The *collarbone* is a target for downward and diagonal elbows. It breaks relatively easily, and a broken collarbone disables the arm on that side. This is a self-defence target only.

The *ribs and solar plexus* are targets for forward and horizontal elbows at chest-to-chest range. They are reasonably safe targets in training if thrown with control and with body protection.

The *back of the head and neck* are targets for downward elbows on someone bent over. They are extremely dangerous and should never be struck in training.

The *thigh* can be struck with a downward elbow when someone is trying to tackle you. It is painful but rarely seriously injurious.

The *arm itself* is an often overlooked target. A downward or horizontal elbow into the bicep, the forearm or the inside of the elbow of an arm that is grabbing or punching you can cause pain and numbness that makes the arm useless for a few moments. This is sometimes called *destruction*, and it is a very practical way to deal with a grabbing hand.

::: street
**Street note: elbows and the law**

Elbows can cause severe injuries, including fractured skulls, broken jaws and brain injuries. In a self-defence situation in the United Kingdom, the force you use must be reasonable in the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be. An elbow to the face of someone who is attacking you with a weapon may well be reasonable. A downward elbow to the back of the neck of someone who is already on the ground and no longer a threat almost certainly is not. Know what your weapons can do, and use them only when you must.
:::

## Elbows from Traps

The elbow fits naturally into the trapping and combination work of Part Two. In fact, many traps lead more naturally to elbows than to punches, because the trap brings you close.

### Palm pin to horizontal elbow

You pin the other person's lead arm against their body with your lead hand, as in Chapter 5. Instead of striking over the pin with a rear-hand punch, you step in slightly and throw a horizontal elbow with your rear arm. Because you are already close, the elbow is the natural weapon. The pinned arm cannot rise to block it.

### Hooking pull to rising elbow

You hook the other person's arm and pull it down towards your hip. Their head comes forward and down. As it does, you drive a rising elbow up with your other arm into their face or chin. The pull and the elbow happen together, so their head moves onto the elbow just as it rises.

### Elbow lift to forward elbow

You lift the other person's arm by pushing up under their elbow, exposing their ribs. As the arm goes up, you step in and drive a forward elbow into the ribs or solar plexus with your other arm.

### Cross trap to downward diagonal elbow

From the outside line, you pull the other person's lead arm across their body, tying up both arms. You then throw a downward diagonal elbow over the top of the trapped arms into the side of the neck or the collarbone.

![**Figure 7.4** The hooking pull into a rising elbow. (1) Your lead hand hooks the opponent's wrist. (2) As you pull it down to your hip, their head comes forward. (3) The rear elbow rises into the face as the head arrives.](figures/fig7_4.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: Seiyunchin and Seipai**

Seiyunchin contains a sequence in which one hand pulls while the other rises in a forward-and-upward movement. As a block, it makes little sense. As a pull into a rising elbow, it makes very good sense, especially from the low stance, which drops the other person's posture.

Seipai contains elbow strikes thrown while the other hand holds or pulls, and a turning movement that can be read as a horizontal elbow followed by a throw. Both kata reward close study for elbow applications.
:::

## Elbows as Defence

The elbow is not only a weapon. It is also a very effective defensive tool, and Goju-Ryu makes great use of it in this way.

### The elbow cover

When someone throws a hooking punch at your head, bringing your elbow up so that your forearm covers the side of your head, with your hand near your ear or the back of your head, creates a solid shield. The punch lands on your forearm or the point of your elbow rather than your head.

This cover has an added benefit: a punch that lands on the point of your elbow often injures the puncher's hand. Boxers know this well, and many are cautious of hooking into a tight elbow guard.

### The elbow block

The point of the elbow can also be used to block kicks and knees, particularly to the body. Dropping your elbow onto an incoming knee or kick protects your ribs and can injure the shin or foot of the attacker.

### The elbow frame

When someone is pressing into you at close range, placing your elbow and forearm against their chest or collarbone creates a strong frame that stops them closing further. Because the elbow is close to your body and backed by your structure, it is much harder to collapse than an extended arm.

::: drill
**Drill 7.2: Cover and return**

Your partner throws slow, light hooking punches at your head with gloves on, alternating sides. You cover each one with an elbow cover, keeping your forearm tight to your head. As soon as the punch lands on your cover, you return immediately with a horizontal elbow from the same arm to a pad your partner holds with their other hand.

The idea is that the cover becomes the chamber for the strike. Your arm is already folded and raised; all you need to do is turn. Build up speed gradually over several sessions.
:::

## Developing Elbow Power

Elbow power depends on three things: body mechanics, structure and conditioning.

*Body mechanics* means turning, rising, dropping or stepping behind the elbow. Practise each elbow slowly in front of a mirror until the movement of the body feels natural. Then practise on pads, concentrating on the body rather than the arm.

*Structure* means having a stable base and a connected body at the moment of impact. An elbow thrown while off balance will bounce off the target. Use the Sanchin principles from Chapter 2: grip the floor, connect through the trunk, and breathe out sharply on impact.

*Conditioning* means preparing the elbow and forearm for impact. Fortunately, the elbow needs much less conditioning than the hands, because it is naturally hard. Regular work on pads and a heavy bag is usually enough. Avoid striking hard, unpadded surfaces with your elbows; the joint can be injured, and there is no need.

::: drill
**Drill 7.3: The heavy bag elbow round**

Set a round timer for two minutes. Stand close to a heavy bag, so close that you could touch it with your forehead. Throw only elbows, in any direction, for the whole round. Move around the bag, change angles, and use your other hand to hold or push the bag as if it were an opponent's arm or head.

Rest for one minute and repeat for three to five rounds. This builds elbow power, close-range footwork and the specific endurance needed for clinch work.
:::

## Elbows in Sparring

Most karate rule sets prohibit elbow strikes, and for good reason: they are very dangerous. Even with head guards, a hard elbow can cause concussion or cuts.

However, there are safe ways to include elbows in sparring if you train in a club that allows close-range work.

The first is to use *very light, controlled elbows* to the body only, with body protection. This lets you practise the timing and positioning without much risk.

The second is to use *touch elbows* to the head guard, where the elbow is placed rather than struck. This teaches you to recognise elbow opportunities without causing harm.

The third is to use *pads in sparring*. One partner wears a body shield or holds a pad, and the other may throw elbows only to the pad. This is surprisingly realistic, because the pad holder is moving and resisting, but the target is safe.

In all cases, both partners must agree on the rules beforehand, and an instructor should supervise.

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My elbows feel weak on the pads."

**Solution:** You are almost certainly throwing them with your arm. Practise slowly with your hand placed on your ear and kept there, so that the arm cannot move independently of the body. Now the only way to deliver the elbow is to turn, rise or drop. Once the body movement feels natural, let the arm move a little more freely again.

**Problem:** "I keep hurting my elbow on the pads."

**Solution:** You are probably hitting with the very point of the elbow at an angle, or hyperextending the joint. Use the forearm just below the point for horizontal elbows, keep the arm fully folded, and check that your pads are thick enough. If the pain persists, rest and see a physiotherapist.

**Problem:** "I can never get close enough to use my elbows."

**Solution:** Elbows are close-range weapons, and you need to get to close range first. Go back to the bridging work in Chapter 3 and the traps in Chapter 5. Also look for moments when the other person comes close to you, such as when they rush in, grab you or try to clinch. Those are elbow moments.

**Problem:** "My partner covers up and my elbows just hit their arms."

**Solution:** Use the traps. An elbow thrown into a guard is a waste; an elbow thrown over a trapped arm lands. Also use the elbow on the arm itself, to break the guard down, and change direction: if they cover high, a forward elbow to the body often lands.
:::

## Bringing It Together

The elbow is the close-range weapon par excellence. It needs very little room, it is hard to injure, it protects your head while you use it, and it can be thrown in six directions from almost any position. It fits naturally after the traps and combinations of Part Two, and it is a weapon that you will be able to use long after your punches and kicks have begun to fade.

Learn the six elbows. Drive them with your body. Know the targets and respect their danger. And practise them from traps, so that when the arm is pinned and the head is open, the elbow arrives without thought.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- The elbow is the natural weapon of the close range: strong, hard to injure, and needing very little space.
- Elbow power comes from the body: turning, rising, dropping or stepping. The arm only carries the elbow into position.
- Strike with the area within a few centimetres of the point of the elbow.
- The six main elbows are horizontal, rising, downward, forward, backward and diagonal.
- Elbows to the head, neck and collarbone are dangerous. Use pads for power training and very controlled elbows, if any, in sparring.
- Elbows fit naturally after traps: pin to horizontal, pull to rising, lift to forward, cross trap to diagonal.
- The elbow is also a defensive tool: as a cover against hooks, a block against knees and a frame against pressure.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Throw ten horizontal elbows on a pad with your arm only, then ten with your whole body. Ask your partner to rate the difference on a scale of one to ten.
2. Find three places in the kata you know where an elbow strike might be hidden. Test each against the four bunkai tests.
3. In kakie, look for moments when your partner's head comes within elbow range. How often does it happen? What causes it?
:::

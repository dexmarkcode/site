# Real-World Encounters: Awareness, the Law and the Aftermath

::: chapter-intro
Everything in this book has been leading here. But the real world is not a dojo, and the skills that matter most in a genuine encounter are not the ones we have spent most of our time on. Awareness, avoidance and de-escalation will keep you safe far more often than any trap, elbow or throw. This final chapter looks honestly at what real confrontations are like, how to stay out of them, how to manage them when you cannot, what the law in the United Kingdom expects of you, and how to cope with the aftermath.
:::

I want to begin this chapter with a confession. In more than thirty years of training, I have used physical technique against another person outside the dojo only a handful of times. On each occasion it was brief, untidy and over within seconds, and on each occasion I could probably have avoided it entirely if I had been paying more attention a minute earlier.

Far more often, I have used the skills that come before technique: noticing that something was wrong, crossing the road, leaving a pub when the atmosphere changed, talking calmly to someone who was shouting, standing in a way that discouraged trouble without inviting it. None of these would look impressive in a demonstration. All of them worked.

Most experienced practitioners, and most people whose work brings them into contact with violence, such as door staff, police officers, paramedics and nurses, will tell you the same thing. The fight you avoid is the one you win most completely.

## The Problem: Violence Is Not Like Training

Real violence differs from training in almost every way that matters.

It is *sudden*. There is often little or no warning, and the first blow may land before you have realised what is happening.

It is *chaotic*. People do not attack in neat, predictable ways. They grab, push, swing, fall, scream and flail.

It is *frightening*. The adrenaline response floods your body, narrowing your vision, distorting your sense of time, shaking your hands, emptying your mind and making fine skills almost impossible.

It is *unfair*. The other person may be bigger, armed, drunk, high or accompanied by friends. They will not stop when you tap.

It is *legally consequential*. Whatever happens, you may have to explain and justify your actions to the police and possibly a court.

And it is *physically and emotionally costly*, even when you win. Injuries, legal stress, fear and guilt can last for years.

None of this is meant to frighten you. It is meant to set your priorities. In the real world, the goal is not to win a fight but to get home safely, with as little harm as possible to you, to the people you care about and, as far as reasonable, to the other person.

## The Principle: The Layers of Protection

I think of personal safety as a series of layers, like the walls of a castle. Each layer, if it holds, means you never need the next one. Physical technique is the last layer, not the first.

### Layer one: awareness

Awareness means noticing what is going on around you. It is the foundation of everything else, because you cannot avoid a threat you have not seen.

Awareness does not mean being paranoid or constantly scanning for danger. It means being reasonably alert, especially in situations where trouble is more likely: late at night, around alcohol, in unfamiliar places, at times of tension. It means having your head up rather than buried in your phone. It means noticing when someone's behaviour seems out of place, when an atmosphere changes, when a person is paying unusual attention to you.

A useful habit is to notice the exits whenever you enter a room, the people around you when you stop somewhere, and the route ahead when you are walking. This takes no more than a moment and quickly becomes automatic.

### Layer two: avoidance

Avoidance means staying away from trouble once you have noticed it. Cross the road. Leave the pub. Take a different route. Get off the bus at the next stop. Walk into a shop. Avoidance costs you a little time and a little pride, and it is the most effective self-defence technique in existence.

Many people find avoidance hard because it feels like backing down. It is not. It is choosing not to take part in someone else's problem. There is no shame in walking away from a situation that could have ended with someone in hospital or prison.

### Layer three: de-escalation

De-escalation means calming a situation that has already begun. If someone is angry, shouting or threatening you, your words, tone and body language can often bring the temperature down enough for everyone to walk away.

### Layer four: escape

If de-escalation fails, or is not possible, escape: create distance and leave. Physical technique at this layer is used only to create the opportunity to escape, not to win.

### Layer five: physical defence

Only if all else has failed, and you or someone else is in genuine danger, do you use physical technique. And even then, it is used to stop the threat and create an opportunity to escape, not to punish or dominate.

## Awareness in Practice

Awareness is a habit, and like all habits it can be trained.

*Notice the environment.* Exits, obstacles, lighting, other people, places where you could be cornered.

*Notice people.* Most people around you are going about their business. Pay attention to those who are not: people loitering without obvious purpose, people watching you, people whose behaviour changes as you approach, groups that separate as you pass.

*Notice your own state.* Are you tired, distracted, intoxicated, upset? Each of these reduces your awareness and your ability to respond. Adjust accordingly.

*Notice changes.* The atmosphere in a pub as the evening goes on. A conversation that is becoming heated. A group that has gone quiet. Changes are often more significant than any single observation.

*Trust your instincts.* If something feels wrong, it probably is. You do not need to justify the feeling or prove it. Simply act on it, by leaving, moving, or becoming more alert.

## Recognising the Build-Up

Most violence that is not a sudden ambush follows a recognisable build-up. Knowing the signs gives you time to act.

*Verbal escalation.* Raised voices, insults, challenges ("What are you looking at?"), repeated questions, swearing directed at you.

*Physical posturing.* Puffing up the chest, squaring the shoulders, standing very close, pointing or jabbing a finger, invading your space.

*Pre-attack indicators.* These are the signs that an attack may be imminent, and they are particularly important:

- a sudden change from loud to quiet, or from moving to still;
- looking around, as if checking for witnesses or police;
- glancing at a target on your body;
- clenching fists, or tensing the jaw;
- blading the body, turning one shoulder forward;
- shifting weight, or taking a step back to make room;
- removing glasses, a hat, a jacket or jewellery;
- a friend moving to your side or behind you;
- the face going pale or flushed, the breathing becoming fast and shallow.

These signs are not proof that an attack is coming, but when you see several of them together, you should assume it may be and act accordingly.

## De-escalation

De-escalation is a large subject, and many people whose work involves dealing with aggression receive specific training in it. Here are the essentials.

*Stay calm.* Your calm is contagious, and so is your agitation. Slow your breathing, lower your voice and relax your face.

*Do not challenge.* Avoid anything that might feel like a challenge: staring, raising your voice, arguing, insulting, making demands.

*Acknowledge.* Many angry people want to feel heard. Acknowledging their feelings ("I can see you're really upset") often lowers the temperature, without agreeing with them or accepting blame.

*Offer a way out.* People are more likely to back down if they can do so without losing face. Give them an exit: "Look, mate, I don't want any trouble. Let's both just get on with our night."

*Apologise if it costs nothing.* If you have accidentally bumped into someone, spilled a drink or looked at them the wrong way, a sincere apology can end the matter. It is not weakness; it is good sense.

*Keep your distance.* While talking, keep a safe distance, ideally just outside arm's reach, and keep your hands up in a non-threatening position (see the fence below).

*Know when to stop.* De-escalation does not always work. If the other person is not responding, or the pre-attack indicators are building, stop talking and prepare to leave or defend yourself.

## The Fence

The fence is a concept developed and popularised by self-protection instructors, notably among those with door-security backgrounds. It is a way of standing and using your hands that controls distance, protects you and prepares you to act, while looking non-aggressive.

Stand in the settled stance from Chapter 2: feet about shoulder-width apart, one slightly forward, knees soft. Raise your hands in front of your chest, open, palms forward or slightly turned towards each other, as if to say "calm down" or "hold on". Your lead hand is slightly forward, roughly at the distance where the other person would have to touch it to get closer.

The fence does several jobs at once.

*It controls distance.* If the other person moves forward, they touch your hand, which tells you they are closing. You can step back or to the side to restore the distance.

*It protects you.* Your hands are already up, between you and the other person, in a position to cover, block or strike.

*It looks non-aggressive.* To an onlooker, and to any camera, you appear to be trying to calm the situation, which is exactly what you are doing.

*It prepares you to act.* From the fence, every close-range technique in this book is available. A trap, a palm heel, an elbow, a push, a bridge: all start from where your hands already are.

![**Figure 18.2** The fence. Hands open and up between you and the other person, lead hand at the edge of their reach, settled stance with soft knees. It looks calming; it is also a guard and a starting position for every close-range technique.](figures/fig18_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill 18.1: Fence and talk**

Your partner approaches you, acting angry and verbally aggressive, and tries to close the distance. You adopt the fence and talk to them calmly, using de-escalation language, while managing the distance with small steps. If they touch your lead hand, step back or to the side and restore the distance.

At a random moment, your partner either calms down or makes a pre-agreed attack signal (such as a sudden lunge with a pad). You must recognise which, and either continue de-escalating or respond with a single strike to the pad and a move to escape.

This drill trains the most realistic sequence of all: talking, managing distance, recognising the moment, and acting.
:::

## When Physical Defence Is Necessary

If all the outer layers have failed and you or someone else is in genuine danger, you may have to use physical technique. Here are the principles that should guide you.

*Act decisively.* Hesitation in a real attack is dangerous. If you have decided that you must act, act fully and immediately.

*Use simple techniques.* Under adrenaline, fine motor skills deteriorate. Simple, gross-motor techniques such as palm heels, elbows, knees, pushes and the basic traps work; complicated joint locks and intricate combinations usually do not.

*Aim to stop the threat, not to win.* Strike to create an opening, then use it to escape. Do not stay to finish the fight.

*Keep moving.* Do not stand still in front of an attacker. Move off the line, use angles, and head for your exit.

*Beware of others.* Assume there may be more than one attacker, even if you can only see one. Keep your head up and avoid going to the ground.

*Beware of weapons.* Assume the other person may have a weapon, even if you cannot see one. Many stabbings are not noticed by the victim until afterwards. If a weapon is visible, your priority is to escape; if you cannot, your priority is to control the weapon arm.

*Stop when the threat stops.* Once the other person is no longer a danger, stop. Continuing beyond that point is no longer self-defence.

::: street
**Street note: multiple attackers and weapons**

Nothing in this book will make you safe against several attackers or someone armed with a knife. Close-range skills improve your chances, but the risk of serious injury or death in these situations is high even for experienced martial artists. Awareness, avoidance and escape are your best defences. If you cannot escape, keep moving, use obstacles, keep one attacker between you and the others, and protect your head and vital organs. Run as soon as you can.
:::

## The Law in the United Kingdom

I am not a lawyer, and this section is not legal advice. The law changes, it varies between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and every case turns on its own facts. If you have been involved in an incident, take advice from a qualified solicitor. What follows is a broad, general outline of the principles as they are commonly understood.

### The basic principle: reasonable force

In England and Wales, the law allows a person to use *reasonable force* to defend themselves or another person, to protect property, to prevent crime, or to assist in a lawful arrest. This comes from the common law and from statute, including the Criminal Law Act 1967 and section 76 of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008, which sets out how the question of reasonable force is approached.

There are, broadly, two questions.

*Did you honestly believe it was necessary to use force?* This is judged on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be, even if you were mistaken, provided the belief was genuinely held. (A mistaken belief caused by voluntary intoxication is treated differently.)

*Was the amount of force reasonable in those circumstances?* This is judged objectively, but the law recognises that a person defending themselves cannot weigh to a nicety the exact amount of force needed. If you did only what you honestly and instinctively thought was necessary in the heat of the moment, that is strong evidence that your actions were reasonable.

Force that is *grossly disproportionate* will not be regarded as reasonable. In householder cases, where someone is defending themselves against an intruder in their home, the law allows a wider margin, but grossly disproportionate force is still not permitted.

### You do not have to wait to be hit

The law does not require you to wait to be attacked before you defend yourself. If you honestly believe an attack is imminent, you may act first, provided your response is reasonable. This is sometimes called a pre-emptive strike. However, the belief must be genuine, and you may be asked to explain what made you believe an attack was coming. This is where knowing and recognising pre-attack indicators matters.

### Retreat

There is no absolute duty to retreat before defending yourself. However, whether you had an opportunity to retreat and did not take it is a factor that may be considered when deciding whether your force was reasonable. In practice, leaving when you safely can is both the safest and the most legally defensible course.

### After the threat ends

Self-defence ends when the threat ends. Force used after the other person has stopped being a danger, for example striking someone who is on the ground and no longer resisting, or chasing someone who is running away, is very unlikely to be regarded as self-defence.

### Scotland and Northern Ireland

The law in Scotland is based on different principles, including the idea that there must be an imminent danger, that retreat or escape was not reasonably possible, and that the force used was not excessive. Northern Ireland has its own statutory framework that broadly mirrors England and Wales. If you live or travel in these jurisdictions, take time to understand the differences.

### Weapons

Carrying any article made or adapted for causing injury in a public place is a criminal offence in the United Kingdom, regardless of your intention. So is carrying a bladed or pointed article without good reason. This includes many items sold as "self-defence" tools. Martial artists should never carry weapons for self-defence. Your training is your protection.

## After the Incident

What happens after a violent incident can be as important as the incident itself.

### Immediate safety

Get to a safe place. Do not stay at the scene if there is any risk of further violence. Once you are safe, check yourself for injuries. Adrenaline can mask pain, including the pain of stab wounds, so look carefully.

### Call the emergency services

If anyone is injured, or if there is a continuing danger, call 999 (or 112). Ask for an ambulance if anyone is hurt, including the other person, and the police. If you have the skills, give first aid to anyone who needs it, provided it is safe to do so.

### Dealing with the police

The police will want to know what happened. You have the right to legal advice, free of charge, at the police station. It is generally sensible to take that advice before giving a detailed account, particularly if you may be suspected of an offence. When you do give an account, be honest. Explain what you saw, what you believed and why you acted as you did. If you recognised pre-attack indicators, say so. If you were frightened, say so.

### Witnesses and evidence

If there were witnesses, try to get their contact details or ask the police to do so. Note the locations of any cameras. Write down your own recollection as soon as you can, because memory fades and is distorted by stress.

### The emotional aftermath

Even a brief violent incident can have a lasting effect. It is very common to experience shaking, nausea, sleeplessness, intrusive memories, anxiety, irritability, guilt or anger in the days and weeks afterwards. These are normal responses to an abnormal event.

Talk to people you trust. Return to normal routines as soon as you can. Be patient with yourself. If the effects persist beyond a few weeks, or are severe, speak to your GP or seek support from organisations that help victims of crime. There is no weakness in this. Many experienced martial artists, police officers and soldiers have needed help after violent incidents.

Every Goju-Ryu kata begins and ends with a bow, *rei*. It is easy to treat the bow as a formality. But it carries a reminder that is especially relevant to this chapter: the techniques between the two bows are serious, and they are framed by respect, restraint and responsibility. ::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "I find it hard to walk away. It feels cowardly."

**Solution:** That feeling is very common, especially among people who train in martial arts. Remind yourself what you are protecting: your health, your freedom, your family, your future. Walking away from someone who wants a fight costs you nothing of value. Staying could cost you everything. Courage is doing the right thing when it is hard, and the right thing is usually to leave.

**Problem:** "I'm worried I'll freeze if something happens."

**Solution:** Almost everyone freezes, at least briefly, the first time. Scenario training (Chapter 17), the surprise grab drill (Drill 15.2) and the fence drill (Drill 18.1) all reduce the freeze by making realistic pressure familiar. Having one simple, automatic first response, such as raising the fence and stepping off the line, also helps, because the body can act while the mind catches up.

**Problem:** "How do I know whether my force would be considered reasonable?"

**Solution:** No one can know for certain in advance, because every case depends on its facts. As a guide: use force only when you genuinely believe you or someone else is in danger, use only as much as you need to stop the threat, stop as soon as the threat stops, and leave as soon as you safely can. If you can honestly say you did those things, you are on much firmer ground.

**Problem:** "I've been in an incident and I can't stop thinking about it."

**Solution:** That is a normal response. Talk to someone you trust, keep to your routines, and be patient. If it does not ease within a few weeks, or if it is affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please speak to your GP or a support organisation. Getting help is a sign of good sense, not weakness.
:::

## Bringing It Together

The real world is where all your training is ultimately tested, but not in the way most people imagine. The test is rarely a fight. It is far more often a moment of awareness, a decision to leave, a calm word, a well-judged distance. Physical technique is the last layer of protection, used only when everything else has failed.

When it is needed, keep it simple, decisive and brief. Use it to stop the threat and escape. Stop when the threat stops. Know the law well enough to act with confidence and restraint. And look after yourself afterwards.

That is the end of the technical part of this book. In the pages that follow you will find a twelve-week training programme, a map linking the techniques to the kata, answers to common questions and a glossary. I hope they help you put everything into practice.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Real violence is sudden, chaotic, frightening, unfair and legally consequential. The goal is to get home safely.
- Protection works in layers: awareness, avoidance, de-escalation, escape and, only last, physical defence.
- Learn to recognise the build-up and the pre-attack indicators.
- De-escalate with calm, acknowledgement, a way out and a safe distance.
- The fence controls distance, protects you, looks non-aggressive and prepares every close-range technique.
- If you must act, act decisively, keep it simple, aim to escape, and stop when the threat stops.
- In the UK, you may use reasonable force based on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be. You need not wait to be hit, but grossly disproportionate force is not reasonable.
- After an incident: get safe, call for help, take legal advice, note evidence, and look after your mental health.
:::

# Bringing It to Sparring: Progressive Close-Range Kumite

::: chapter-intro
Drills build skills. Sparring tests them. Without sparring, close-range skill remains a set of cooperative patterns that may or may not survive contact with a resisting person. But close-range sparring is also where people get hurt, where egos flare, and where training partners are lost. This chapter sets out a progressive, safe method for bringing everything in this book into live practice, so that you can pressure-test your skills without losing your partners or your health.
:::

I have seen close-range sparring done very badly. In one club I visited, the instructor's idea of close-range training was to put two people in a corner and tell them to "sort it out". The result was a red-faced brawl, a bloody nose, a twisted knee and two people who did not speak to each other for the rest of the evening. Nobody learned anything, except perhaps not to come back.

I have also seen it done very well. In the best clubs, close-range sparring is a structured, progressive, intelligent process. Partners start with very limited goals and very light contact, and gradually add techniques, intensity and freedom as their skill and trust develop. People come out of these sessions tired, sweaty, grinning and better than they were an hour before.

The difference is not the techniques. It is the method. This chapter gives you the method.

## The Problem: Pressure Without Injury

The problem with sparring is that it must provide realistic pressure without causing injury. Too little pressure, and the sparring becomes a cooperative drill that teaches nothing new. Too much, and people get hurt, become frightened, and stop learning.

At close range, this problem is particularly acute. The techniques of close combat, elbows, knees, throws, joint controls, headbutts, are among the most dangerous in martial arts. They cannot be used at full power on a training partner. Yet if they are never tested against resistance, you will never know whether you can use them.

The solution is to *limit* sparring in carefully chosen ways, so that each kind of sparring tests a particular skill under realistic pressure while removing the most dangerous elements.

## The Principle: Constrain, Then Free

The key idea behind progressive sparring is that every sparring game has *constraints*: limits on what techniques are allowed, how hard they can be applied, what the goals are, how long a round lasts, and so on. By choosing the constraints carefully, you can create a game that tests a specific skill with realistic pressure and acceptable risk.

As skill develops, the constraints are relaxed one at a time. A game that allows only pushing becomes a game that allows pushing and light strikes. A game with one target becomes a game with three. A game at quarter intensity becomes a game at half intensity.

This approach has several advantages. It keeps risk low, because dangerous techniques are introduced only when people are ready for them. It focuses learning, because each game tests a particular skill. And it builds trust, because partners know what to expect.

## The Agreement

Before any close-range sparring, partners must agree on the following. I insist on this in my classes, even between people who have trained together for years.

*Allowed techniques.* Exactly which techniques may be used.

*Targets.* Exactly which targets may be struck.

*Contact level.* How hard strikes may land, usually described as a percentage: touch, light (around a quarter of full power), medium (around half). Full contact is never used in close-range sparring in my classes.

*Protection.* What equipment is worn: gloves, head guards, mouthguards, groin guards, shin guards, body protectors.

*The tap.* A clear signal for stopping, usually a tap on the partner or the floor, or the word "stop". On the tap, both partners stop immediately.

*The goal.* What each person is trying to achieve.

*The round length.* Usually between thirty seconds and two minutes.

If either partner is unhappy with any part of the agreement, it is changed. If either partner breaks the agreement, for example by hitting harder than agreed, the round stops immediately and the agreement is restated.

::: fault
**Fault: escalation**

The most common problem in sparring is escalation. One partner hits slightly harder than agreed, the other responds slightly harder still, and within a minute both are hitting much harder than either intended. Nobody decides to escalate; it just happens.

*Correction:* Stop the round as soon as you notice it, restate the agreement, and start again. If it keeps happening with a particular partner, train with someone else for a while. Experienced partners can help by deliberately keeping their contact slightly *below* the agreed level.
:::

## The Progression

Here is the progression I use. Each stage is a family of games, and you should spend weeks or months at each before moving on. You will also return to earlier stages regularly, as warm-ups and to sharpen particular skills.

### Stage one: positional games

At the first stage, there is no striking at all. The games are about position: structure, balance, inside control and angles.

*The pushing square* (Drill 2.2): push your partner out of a square.

*The shoulder game* (Drill 4.2): touch your partner's shoulder in kakie.

*Owning the outside* (Drill 3.2): gain the outside of your partner's lead arm.

*Clinch position* (Chapter 9): gain inside control and break your partner's posture.

*Off the wall* (Drill 9.2): escape from a wall or keep your partner pinned.

These games are safe, very tiring and extremely instructive. They develop the foundations without the stress of being hit.

### Stage two: touch games

At the second stage, strikes are introduced, but only as touches. A touch scores; a strike that lands harder than a touch is a foul.

*Touch kakie*: free kakie in which an open-hand touch to the face or body scores.

*Touch knees* (Drill 8.3): touch your partner's thigh or body with your knee from the clinch.

*Trap and touch*: trap your partner's arm and touch them with the free hand.

Touch games teach timing and targeting without fear. They also teach control, which is essential for everything that follows.

### Stage three: light contact with limited techniques

At the third stage, strikes are allowed at light contact, but the range of techniques is limited. Each game focuses on a particular group of techniques.

*Hands only*: short punches, palm heels and back fists to the body and head guard, at light contact.

*Hands and knees*: as above, plus knees to the thigh with thigh protection.

*Traps and strikes*: strikes that score only if preceded by a trap.

*Clinch and knees*: clinch control and knees to the thigh and body.

### Stage four: throws and takedowns

At the fourth stage, throws are introduced, on mats, with competent breakfalls. Strikes may or may not be included.

*Throwing games*: start in a clinch and try to throw your partner. No strikes. A throw scores; you then reset.

*Sweep games*: try to sweep your partner's foot. No strikes, no other throws.

*Strike to throw*: light strikes are allowed, and a throw that follows a strike scores extra.

### Stage five: integrated close-range sparring

At the fifth stage, most of the techniques in this book are allowed, at light to medium contact, with full protection. The goal is to integrate structure, sensitivity, trapping, short strikes, clinch work and throws into a continuous exchange.

Even at this stage, some techniques are never used at speed or force: elbows to the head (allowed only as touches to the head guard, if at all), knees to the head, headbutts, stamps to the knee, strikes to the groin, joint controls applied quickly, and neck attacks of any kind.

### Stage six: scenario training

At the sixth stage, sparring is placed in realistic scenarios: an aggressive person approaching, a grab from behind, a push against a wall, two attackers, a confined space. The goal is to test your skills under the stress of a realistic situation, and to practise the awareness, de-escalation and escape skills of Chapter 18.

Scenario training must be very carefully controlled, with clear rules, plenty of protection and close supervision, because the stress of the scenario makes people more likely to lose control.

::: drill
**Drill 17.1: The ten-minute ladder**

A short session that climbs the ladder. Two minutes of the pushing square. Two minutes of touch kakie. Two minutes of hands-only light sparring at close range. Two minutes of clinch and touch knees. Two minutes of throwing games on mats.

Rest for thirty seconds between each section. This drill is an excellent end to a session, because it revisits every stage in a short time and shows you where your skills are strongest and weakest.
:::

## Starting Positions

One problem with close-range sparring is that, if both partners start at a distance, much of the round is spent getting to close range, and some people will avoid it altogether. Using close-range starting positions solves this.

*Kakie start*: both partners begin with forearms in contact, in the kakie position.

*Clinch start*: both partners begin in a collar-and-elbow clinch or a double underhook position.

*Grab start*: one partner begins holding the other's wrist, lapel or shoulder.

*Wall start*: one partner begins with their back to a padded wall, the other pressing.

*Fence start*: one partner holds a fence (hands up, open, in front), the other at close range in front of them. This is particularly useful for self-defence scenarios.

By choosing the starting position, you can focus the sparring on the skills you want to develop.

## Equipment

For close-range sparring, appropriate protective equipment is essential.

*Gloves.* MMA-style open-fingered gloves allow grabbing and trapping while offering some protection to the hands and the partner's face. Boxing gloves offer more protection but make trapping and grabbing difficult.

*Head guard.* A good head guard, ideally with cheek protection, reduces the risk of cuts and some impacts, though it does not prevent concussion.

*Mouthguard.* Always. A properly fitted mouthguard protects the teeth and reduces the risk of jaw injuries.

*Groin guard.* Always, for both men and women.

*Shin and thigh protection.* For any game involving knees or low kicks.

*Body protector.* For games involving knees or elbows to the body.

*Mats.* For any game involving throws, sweeps or takedowns.

## Partners

Your choice of training partners matters enormously. Good partners make you better; bad partners make you worse, or injured.

*Train with a variety of partners*: big and small, strong and fast, experienced and new, men and women. Each will teach you something different.

*Train with people you trust.* At close range, you are putting your safety in your partner's hands. Choose people who keep to the agreement and look after you.

*Be a good partner yourself.* Keep to the agreement, control your contact, tap and release promptly, and give your partner the chance to succeed sometimes. The best training happens when both partners are working at the edge of their ability, not when one is crushing the other.

*Match intensity to experience.* Experienced practitioners working with beginners should keep their intensity well below their maximum and give the beginner room to learn.

## Learning from Sparring

Sparring is only useful if you learn from it. Here are some ways to make sure you do.

*Have a focus.* Before each round, choose one thing to work on: a particular trap, a counter, a position. You will learn far more from a focused round than from a round in which you try everything.

*Review afterwards.* After each round, take a moment to ask yourself what worked, what did not, and why. Talk to your partner about it.

*Film yourself.* Video is brutally honest. You will see habits you never knew you had.

*Go back to drills.* When sparring reveals a weakness, go back to the drills in this book that address it. Sparring tells you what to practise; drills are how you practise it.

*Keep a training diary.* Write down what you worked on, what you noticed and what you want to improve. Over months, the diary will show you patterns.

::: drill
**Drill 17.2: Focused rounds**

Before each round of light close-range sparring, each partner writes down one specific goal on a card, such as "land a palm pin followed by a strike" or "gain the outside line three times". After the round, each partner reports whether they achieved it, and the partner gives feedback.

Swap goals and partners regularly. This simple structure makes sparring far more productive than open, unfocused rounds.
:::

## Sport Rules and Close Range

If you compete in sport karate, close-range sparring will help you even though many rule sets limit close-range work. You will be better at handling opponents who rush in, more comfortable when the exchange closes, and more able to use the few close-range techniques that are allowed, such as sweeps, short punches and quick traps.

In rule sets that allow more close-range work, such as knockdown karate, some traditional formats and mixed rules, the skills in this book become much more directly useful. Adapt the progression above to your rule set: if knees are allowed, include them at an appropriate stage; if throws are restricted, focus on the ones that are permitted.

## Mental Skills in Sparring

Close-range sparring is psychologically demanding. Being close to someone who is trying to hit, throw or control you is stressful, and stress affects performance. Sparring is an opportunity to train the mental skills that will serve you in real encounters.

*Breathing.* Use the Sanchin breathing from Chapter 2 to stay calm. Breathe out on effort and keep breathing throughout.

*Relaxation.* Notice tension in your shoulders, jaw and hands, and release it. Tension slows you down and tires you out.

*Attention.* Keep your attention on your partner and on the contact between you, not on your own thoughts or fears.

*Acceptance.* You will be hit, thrown and controlled. That is part of learning. Accept it, learn from it and move on.

*Enjoyment.* Close-range sparring, done well, is enormously enjoyable. The intimacy, the problem-solving, the flow between partners can be a genuine pleasure. If you are not enjoying it at least some of the time, something needs to change.

::: street
**Street note: sparring is not fighting**

Sparring, however realistic, is not a real fight. There are rules, protection, a cooperative partner and no real intention to harm. A real encounter has none of these. Sparring builds skills and confidence, but it does not fully prepare you for the shock, chaos and fear of real violence. Scenario training helps bridge the gap, but the best preparation for real violence is the awareness and avoidance skills in the next chapter.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My partner keeps hitting harder than we agreed."

**Solution:** Stop the round immediately and restate the agreement. If it happens again, stop again. If it continues, stop training with that partner and speak to your instructor. A partner who cannot keep to an agreement is a danger to you.

**Problem:** "I freeze up in sparring and can't do anything I've practised."

**Solution:** You have moved up the ladder too quickly. Go back to the positional and touch games, where the stress is lower, and build your confidence there. Use the breathing and relaxation techniques. And make sure each round has a single, simple focus, so that you are not trying to think of everything at once.

**Problem:** "Close-range sparring always turns into wrestling."

**Solution:** That is very common when the rules are vague. Use specific constraints: for example, a rule that the clinch can be held for only five seconds before the partners separate, or a rule that a throw must be attempted within ten seconds of clinching. Also make sure both partners have a clear goal for the round.

**Problem:** "I'm much smaller than most of my partners and I always lose."

**Solution:** You may lose more often, but you are learning more. Use the constraints to give yourself a chance: for example, a game in which you score by touching your partner and they score only by pushing you out of a square. Ask larger partners to work at a lower intensity with you. And notice what does work for you; those techniques are your strengths.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Sparring is where close-range skill is tested and refined. Done well, it is safe, progressive and enjoyable, and it transforms cooperative patterns into genuine ability. Done badly, it is dangerous and teaches very little.

The method is simple: constrain, then free. Begin with positional games, move to touch games, then light contact with limited techniques, then throws, then integrated sparring, and finally scenarios. Agree the rules before every round. Wear the right equipment. Choose good partners and be a good partner. And learn from every round.

The final chapter steps outside the dojo, into the real world, where there are no agreements, no protection and no rules.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Sparring must provide realistic pressure without injury. Constraints make this possible.
- Agree techniques, targets, contact, protection, the tap, the goal and the round length before every round.
- Climb the ladder: positional games, touch games, light contact with limited techniques, throws, integrated sparring, scenarios.
- Use close-range starting positions to focus sparring on the skills you want.
- Never use dangerous techniques at speed or force in sparring: elbows and knees to the head, headbutts, stamps to the knee, groin strikes, fast joint controls or neck attacks.
- Choose trustworthy partners of all kinds, and be a good partner yourself.
- Learn from sparring with focused rounds, review, video and a training diary.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Write out a sparring agreement for your next session, covering all seven elements. Discuss it with your partner before you start.
2. Which rung of the ladder are you currently on? What would you need to be able to do before moving to the next?
3. After a month of focused rounds (Drill 17.2), review your cards. Which goals did you achieve most often? Which least? What does that tell you?
:::

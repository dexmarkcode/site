# Hiza-geri: Knees and the Low-Line Weapons

::: chapter-intro
If the elbow is the close-range weapon of the upper body, the knee is its partner below. It is the heaviest strike most people can throw, it needs almost no room, and at close range it is very hard to see coming. Alongside the knee, Goju-Ryu uses a family of low kicks, stamps and sweeps that attack the legs from distances where a high kick would be impossible. This chapter covers them all.
:::

Goju-Ryu has a reputation as a style without much kicking. People who know only competition karate sometimes look at the kata, see the absence of high kicks, and conclude that Goju-Ryu practitioners cannot kick. They are wrong, but in an interesting way.

Goju-Ryu does kick. It just kicks low and close. The front kicks in the kata are aimed at the groin, the knee and the shin. There are stamping kicks, knee strikes, and sweeping and hooking movements of the feet. These are not the kicks of the sport fighter, who needs distance and flexibility. They are the kicks of someone who expects the other person to be close, who wants to keep their own balance, and who knows that a strike to the leg can end a fight as surely as a strike to the head.

Of all these leg techniques, the knee is the most important at close range, and it is where we begin.

## The Problem: The Lower Body Is Unguarded

When people fight at close range, they instinctively protect their heads. Their hands come up, their chins tuck in, their elbows close in front of their faces. What they rarely protect is everything below the chest: the abdomen, the groin, the thighs, the knees and the shins.

This is partly instinct and partly training. Most people who have done any boxing or karate are used to defending against punches to the head, but not against knees and low kicks. And at close range the lower body is simply hard to see. Your eyes are on the other person's face and hands, and their legs are below your field of view, hidden by the bodies between you.

At the same time, your own legs are free. While your hands are busy trapping, covering and striking, your legs are doing nothing except holding you up. A close-range fighter who can use their legs as well as their arms has twice as many weapons as one who cannot.

## The Principle: The Hips Are the Engine

Just as elbow power comes from the body rather than the arm, knee power comes from the hips rather than the leg. A knee thrown by lifting the leg is weak. A knee thrown by driving the hips forward, so that the whole weight of the body goes into the strike, is extremely powerful.

To feel this, stand with your hands on a partner's shoulders. Lift your knee towards a pad they hold against their thigh, using only your leg. Then try again, this time pulling yourself towards your partner with your hands as you drive your hips forward and up, rising onto the ball of your standing foot. The second knee will be many times stronger.

This is why the knee works so well with the clinch. When you have hold of the other person, you can pull them onto your knee while you drive your hips into it. The pull and the drive combine.

## The Knee Strikes

There are four main knee strikes, each suited to different positions and targets.

### The straight knee (mae hiza-geri)

The straight knee drives forward and upward into the target, usually the abdomen, the groin, the thigh or, if the head has been pulled down, the face. The point of the knee, just above the kneecap, is the striking surface.

To throw it, step or shift so that your striking leg is behind you. Drive the knee forward and up while pushing your hips forward and rising onto the ball of your standing foot. Pull the other person towards the knee with your hands if you have hold of them. Lean back very slightly to keep your balance, but not so much that you lose the forward drive.

### The rising knee to the head

When the other person's head has been pulled down, a straight knee becomes a rising knee to the face. This is one of the most devastating strikes in close combat, and one of the most dangerous. It is never used in karate sparring, and it should be practised only on pads.

### The curved knee (mawashi hiza-geri)

The curved knee travels in an arc from the side, like a roundhouse kick folded up. It is usually aimed at the ribs, the side of the abdomen or the outside of the thigh.

To throw it, lift your knee out to the side, turn your hips over and drive the knee across into the target. The striking surface is the inside of the knee or the point. The curved knee goes around the other person's guard, so it is useful when they are covering their front.

### The knee to the thigh

A straight or slightly curved knee into the front or outside of the thigh, aimed at the large muscles, causes intense pain and a temporary loss of strength in the leg, known in British playgrounds as a "dead leg". It is safe enough to practise with control and with thigh protection, and it is one of the most practical knees for self-defence because it is very effective but unlikely to cause lasting injury.

::: drill
**Drill 8.1: Hip drive knees**

Your partner holds a Thai pad or kick shield firmly against their abdomen. Place both hands on their shoulders. Throw ten straight knees with your right leg, concentrating entirely on driving your hips forward and pulling your partner onto the knee. Then ten with the left. Then alternate legs for twenty knees, switching your stance with a small hop between each.

Your partner should feel the difference between a knee that lifts and a knee that drives. Ask them to tell you when the knee feels heavy and when it feels light.
:::

## Targets for the Knee

As with the elbow, the choice of target matters, both for effectiveness and for safety.

The *thigh* is the safest and one of the most effective targets. Knees to the outside of the thigh (the peroneal area) and the front of the thigh cause pain and weakness without lasting harm in most cases.

The *abdomen and solar plexus* are effective targets for straight knees, causing winding and doubling over. They are reasonably safe in training with body protection.

The *ribs* are a target for curved knees. They can be broken, so control is needed in training.

The *groin* is a very effective self-defence target and is never struck in training except on pads or a groin-guard target used with great care.

The *face and head* are targets only when the head has been pulled down. Knees to the head can cause severe injury and are used only on pads.

## Low Kicks and Stamps

Beyond the knee, Goju-Ryu uses a range of low kicks that work at close and middle range. These are simple, direct and hard to see.

### The low front kick (mae geri gedan)

The low front kick is a snapping or thrusting kick with the ball of the foot or the toe of the shoe, aimed at the groin, the shin or the knee. In the kata, front kicks are usually low, and at close range they are often thrown with very little chambering, the leg simply snapping up from the floor.

### The stamping kick (kansetsu geri or fumikomi)

The stamping kick drives the heel or the sole of the foot downwards and forwards into the knee, the shin or the instep. Against the knee, it is extremely dangerous: a stamp to the front of the knee can hyperextend it and tear the ligaments. Against the shin or instep, it is painful and disruptive.

Stamping movements appear throughout the Goju-Ryu kata, and they are often overlooked because they look like steps. Any time you see a heavy, deliberate step forward in a kata, it is worth asking whether it might be a stamp on the other person's foot or knee.

### The shin kick

A short kick with the shin into the outside or inside of the other person's thigh or calf, similar to a Muay Thai low kick but thrown from closer range and with less wind-up. It is very effective at disrupting balance and causing pain, and it can be practised safely with shin guards.

### The heel kick and back stamp

When someone is behind you or at your side, a stamp backwards with the heel onto their foot or shin, or a short back kick into their knee, can break a hold or create space.

::: kata
**Kata link: the stamps of Seisan and Suparinpei**

Seisan and Suparinpei both contain heavy stepping and stamping movements that are often performed as simple stance changes. Read as stamps, they become powerful leg attacks: the step lands on the other person's foot or knee as your hands pull or strike their upper body. This combination, upper body controlled while the leg is attacked, is one of the most effective in all of close combat, because the person cannot easily move their leg away while you have hold of their upper body.
:::

## Combining Hands and Legs

The real power of knees and low kicks at close range comes from combining them with hand techniques. While the other person's attention is on your hands, your legs can attack freely, and vice versa.

### High–low

The simplest combination is high–low: strike or threaten the head, and as the other person covers, attack the legs or body. A back fist to the face, followed instantly by a knee to the thigh, is a classic close-range combination. The back fist brings their hands and attention up; the knee lands below.

### Low–high

The reverse also works: attack the legs or body first, and as the other person drops their hands or bends forward, attack the head. A knee to the abdomen bends them forward, and a downward elbow or a rising knee to the head follows. A stamp on the foot pins them in place, and a strike to the head lands while they cannot step back.

### Pull and knee

The strongest knee combinations involve pulling. When you have hold of the other person's arm, collar, neck or shoulders, you pull them onto the knee as you drive it. This combines their forward movement with yours, and it also stops them stepping away.

### Trap and kick

A trap that ties up the other person's arms also ties up their attention. While they are struggling to free their arm, a low kick to the shin or a knee to the thigh is very hard for them to notice or defend.

::: drill
**Drill 8.2: High–low at the pads**

Your partner wears focus mitts on their hands and a thigh pad strapped to the outside of one thigh. They hold the mitts in front of their face like a guard. You throw a back fist to one mitt, then immediately drive a knee into the thigh pad. Then a palm heel to the other mitt and a shin kick to the thigh pad. Then a knee to the thigh pad and a hook to a mitt.

Twenty combinations, alternating sides. The aim is to make the hand and the leg feel like a single action, with no pause between them.
:::

## Defending Against Knees and Low Kicks

If knees and low kicks are so effective, you need to be able to defend against them too. Fortunately, the defences are relatively simple, and they are worth practising alongside the attacks.

### Distance and posture

The best defence against a knee is to keep your hips back slightly and your elbows down, so that there is a little space between your body and the other person's. A knee needs the other person's hips to be close to yours. If you keep a slight gap and a forearm frame, their knees have little room to develop.

### The elbow block

As mentioned in Chapter 7, dropping your elbow onto an incoming knee protects your ribs and abdomen and can injure the attacker's leg. Keep your elbows tight to your body in the clinch, and drop them sharply as a knee comes in.

### The leg check

Against a low kick or a curved knee to the thigh, lifting your own knee so that your shin meets their kick is the standard defence. It hurts them as much as it hurts you, and it stops the kick reaching your thigh. At close range, a small lift of the knee is usually enough.

### The jam

Against a straight knee, placing your hand or forearm on the other person's hip as they start to drive it in can stop the knee before it develops, because the hip is the engine. Push the hip back and the knee goes nowhere.

### Moving the target

Turning your hips so that a knee meets the side of your thigh rather than your groin or abdomen, or stepping slightly to the side so it misses, are simple and effective defences. Your Sanchin stance, with its slightly inward-turned knees, naturally protects the groin.

## Balance and the Knee

There is one problem with knees and kicks that every close-range fighter must reckon with: whenever you strike with your leg, you are standing on one leg. That makes you vulnerable to being pushed, pulled or swept.

At long range this is less of a problem, because the other person cannot reach you. At close range it is a real danger. If you throw a knee and the other person pushes you as your leg is in the air, you will fall over.

There are three ways to manage this.

First, *hold onto them*. When you throw a knee from the clinch, your hands are holding the other person, and their body helps support you. They cannot easily push you because you are attached to them.

Second, *keep it short*. A knee or low kick at close range should be fast and should return quickly to the floor. The longer your leg is in the air, the longer you are vulnerable.

Third, *strike when they cannot respond*. Throw your knees when the other person's arms are trapped, when they are off balance, or when they are reacting to a hand strike. If they are busy, they cannot push you.

::: fault
**Fault: leaning back on the knee**

Many people, when they throw a knee, lean back to keep their balance. This takes all the power out of the strike, because the hips go back instead of forward, and it also puts them off balance.

*Correction:* Keep your upper body upright or leaning very slightly forward, and drive your hips into the knee. Use your hands on your partner to stabilise you. If you find yourself falling backwards, you are leaning back.
:::

## Knees and Low Kicks in Sparring

Most karate rule sets prohibit knees and low kicks, although some allow foot sweeps and low kicks to the thigh. If your club allows close-range sparring, knees to the thigh with thigh protection, and low kicks with shin guards, are relatively safe to include. Knees to the body should be very light and used only with body protection. Knees to the head, stamps to the knee and kicks to the groin should never be used in sparring.

A useful sparring game is to allow only *touch* knees: a knee that touches the opponent's thigh or body scores, but must not strike. This teaches the timing and positioning of knees without the risk of injury.

::: drill
**Drill 8.3: Touch-knee clinch rounds**

Partners start in a light clinch, each with one hand behind the other's neck and one hand on the other's arm. On the signal, each tries to touch the other's thigh with their knee while preventing their partner doing the same. Touches only, no strikes. One-minute rounds.

You will quickly discover the value of posture, hip position and control of the other person's upper body, and you will learn to feel when a knee is coming.
:::

::: street
**Street note**

On the street, the knee to the thigh is one of the most useful techniques you can have. It is very effective, it is hard to see, and it is unlikely to cause the kind of lasting injury that would be difficult to justify legally. A couple of hard knees to the thigh can take the fight out of the other person's legs, making it hard for them to chase you as you leave. Knees to the groin and head, and stamps to the knee, are much more dangerous and should be reserved for situations where you genuinely fear serious harm.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My knees have no power."

**Solution:** You are lifting your leg rather than driving your hips. Practise Drill 8.1 with your hands on your partner's shoulders, focusing entirely on the hips. Rise onto the ball of your standing foot. Pull your partner towards you as you drive. If you still feel weak, practise standing knee drives against a wall: hands on the wall, drive your knee up and your hips forward towards the wall, twenty times each side.

**Problem:** "I keep losing my balance when I throw knees."

**Solution:** You are leaning back or throwing from too far away. Get closer, hold onto your partner, and keep your upper body upright. Also check your standing foot: it should be turned slightly outward and planted firmly, with the heel lifting only as you drive.

**Problem:** "My partner keeps catching my low kicks."

**Solution:** Your kicks are too slow or too high, or you are throwing them without setting them up. Keep low kicks very low, below the knee or into the lower thigh, so they are hard to catch. Throw them immediately after a hand technique, when your partner's attention is high. And return your leg to the floor as fast as you threw it.

**Problem:** "I can never see the knees coming in the clinch."

**Solution:** You will rarely see them. Learn to feel them instead. When the other person's hips move towards you, a knee is coming. Keep a hand or forearm near their hip so you can feel the movement and jam it. Drill 8.3 develops exactly this sensitivity.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Knees and low kicks give you a second set of weapons at close range, aimed at targets the other person is rarely protecting. Combined with hand techniques, they create combinations that are very hard to defend, because the other person cannot protect high and low at once.

Like the elbow, the knee gets its power from the body, specifically the drive of the hips. Like the elbow, it is most effective after a trap or from the clinch. And like the elbow, it must be treated with respect in training, because it can cause serious injury.

The next chapter brings elbows, knees and the hands together in the clinch, where the two bodies are locked together and the fight is decided by control of the head, the arms and the posture.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- At close range, the lower body is usually unguarded and hard to see. Attack it.
- Knee power comes from driving the hips, not lifting the leg. Pull the other person onto the knee.
- The four main knees are the straight knee, the rising knee to the head, the curved knee and the knee to the thigh.
- Goju-Ryu low kicks include the low front kick, the stamp, the shin kick and the back stamp. Stamps are hidden throughout the kata.
- Combine hands and legs: high–low, low–high, pull and knee, trap and kick.
- Defend knees with posture and distance, the elbow drop, the leg check, the hip jam and moving the target.
- Manage balance by holding on, keeping strikes short, and striking when the other person is busy.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Look through the kata you know for every step that could be a stamp. How many did you find? Which seem most plausible?
2. Throw ten knees to a pad with your hands at your sides, then ten with your hands on your partner's shoulders. What difference do the hands make?
3. During kakie or clinch work, notice how often your partner's thigh is within range of your knee. What are you usually doing with your legs at those moments?
:::

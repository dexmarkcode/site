# Wrist, Elbow and Shoulder Controls

::: chapter-intro
With the principles of Chapter 13 in place, we can look at specific controls. This chapter teaches six joint controls that I have found reliable under pressure, along with the restraint and escort positions that follow from them. Each is shown from a realistic starting point, usually a grab, a push or a punch, because that is where joint controls actually happen.
:::

When I began studying joint controls, I collected them the way some people collect stamps. I learned dozens of wrist locks, elbow locks and shoulder locks, each with its own name and its own precise sequence of movements. Most of them worked beautifully in the dojo, against partners who stood still and offered their wrists.

Then I started working with people who did not cooperate: police officers on a self-defence course, security staff, and a few very strong judo players who thought my wrist locks were faintly ridiculous. Almost all of my collection failed. Only a handful survived, and those were the ones I have kept teaching ever since. They have three things in common. They use large movements rather than small ones. They work from realistic starting positions. And they flow naturally from or into strikes, traps and throws.

Those are the controls in this chapter.

## The Problem: From Contact to Control

The starting point for every control in this chapter is contact. The other person has grabbed you, pushed you, thrown a punch that you have blocked, or been trapped by you. Their arm is in contact with yours, and you want to turn that contact into control.

The difficulty is that the contact lasts only a moment. A grab may be released, a push withdrawn, a punch retracted. You must move from contact to control very quickly, before the other person has time to react, and you must do it without letting them hit you with their other hand.

The solution is to use the movement that the contact already contains. A grab is pulling you; go with the pull. A push is pushing you; turn and let it go past. A punch is extending; pull it further. Joint controls applied in the direction of the other person's own movement are much faster and much harder to resist than controls applied against it.

## Control One: The Outward Wrist Turn

The outward wrist turn, known in many arts as *kote gaeshi*, is one of the most famous joint controls in the world. It rotates the wrist outward and backward, turning the other person's arm and body and, if continued, throwing them to the floor.

### From a wrist grab

The other person grabs your left wrist with their right hand.

*Entry.* Circle your left hand outward and upward, turning it so that your palm faces up and your fingers point towards their elbow. This loosens their grip and brings their hand to the outside. As you do, place your right hand over the back of their right hand, with your thumb on the back of their hand between the knuckles of the ring and little fingers and your fingers wrapping around the base of their thumb.

*Control.* Free your left hand and add it to the control, so that both your hands hold their right hand. Your thumbs are on the back of their hand. Turn their hand outward, rotating it away from them and bending the wrist back towards their forearm, as if you were turning a large key.

*Takedown.* Step back with your left foot, turning your body to your left, and continue the rotation. Their arm and body follow the rotation, and they fall backward and to the side. In training, they should breakfall to the side as they feel the turn.

The key detail is that the rotation must be in a large circle, driven by your body turning, not a small twist of the wrist driven by your hands. A small twist is easy to resist; a large turn of the whole body is not.

![**Figure 14.1** The outward wrist turn. (1) From a wrist grab, your hand circles to bring theirs to the outside. (2) Both thumbs on the back of their hand. (3) Turn their hand outward in a large circle as you step back and turn; they fall to the side.](figures/fig14_1.png)

### From a punch

The outward wrist turn can also be applied to a punch, by catching the punching hand as it withdraws and applying the turn. This is harder, because the hand is moving fast, but it is a classic application of the hooking and pulling hand movements in kata.

## Control Two: The Inward Wrist Bend

The inward wrist bend, related to *nikyo* in aikido, bends the wrist forward and rotates it inward, creating intense pain at the wrist. It is a powerful pain-compliance control and a good basis for escorting someone.

### From a lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing at chest height with their right hand.

*Entry.* Trap their right hand against your chest with your right hand, palm flat on the back of their hand. Their hand is now fixed: they cannot release it without pulling it out from under your palm.

*Control.* Bring your left forearm over their right forearm, close to their wrist, and press down. At the same time, bow slightly forward from the hips. Their wrist is bent forward between your chest and your forearm, and the pressure drives them downward.

*Finish.* Continue the pressure until they go down to their knees, or until they tap in training. From here you can release and leave, or move into an escort position.

The beauty of this control is that the grab provides the fulcrum. Their hand is pinned against your chest by your hand, and all you have to do is press their forearm down. It requires very little strength and is very hard to escape once it is on.

![**Figure 14.2** The inward wrist bend from a lapel grab. (1) Pin the grabbing hand flat against your chest. (2) Bring your forearm over their wrist and bow slightly forward. Their wrist bends forward and they are driven down.](figures/fig14_2.png)

::: kata
**Kata link: the opening of Seipai and Shisochin**

Several Goju-Ryu kata contain a movement in which one hand is drawn to the chest while the other forearm presses forward or down. Read as the inward wrist bend, the hand at the chest pins the grabbing hand, and the pressing forearm drives the wrist down. This is one of the clearest examples of a joint control hidden in an apparent block.
:::

## Control Three: The Straight-Arm Bar

The straight-arm bar controls the elbow by pressing on the back of a straight arm. We saw it as a takedown in Chapter 11; here we look at it as a control.

### From a push

The other person pushes you with their right hand on your left shoulder or chest.

*Entry.* Let the push turn you slightly to your left, so that their arm slides past. Take their right wrist with your right hand and draw it forward, straightening the arm. Step forward with your left foot to their right side.

*Control.* Place your left forearm on the back of their right arm, just above the elbow. Pull their wrist slightly upward with your right hand while pressing down on the elbow with your left forearm. Their arm is straight and locked, and the pressure on the elbow drives their shoulder down.

*Takedown or escort.* Continue pressing and circle forward to take them to the floor, as in Chapter 11. Or hold the control and walk them in the direction you want, keeping the arm straight.

The straight-arm bar is one of the most reliable controls because the elbow has a very clear limit. However, it is also one of the most dangerous, because the elbow can be hyperextended and injured very quickly. In training, apply it very slowly.

## Control Four: The Bent-Arm Lock

The bent-arm lock, sometimes called the *figure-four* or *key lock*, controls the shoulder by bending the arm and rotating it, either upward (an outward rotation) or behind the back (an inward rotation). It is very powerful and forms the basis of many restraint positions.

### The rear version (behind the back)

The other person reaches for you with their right hand.

*Entry.* Take their right wrist with your right hand. Step to their right side and slide your left arm under their right arm from the front, between their arm and their body, so that your left forearm is in the crook of their elbow.

*Control.* Bend their right arm by pushing their wrist back and down with your right hand while lifting under their elbow with your left forearm. Their arm folds behind their back. Grip your own right wrist with your left hand, forming a figure four around their arm.

*Finish.* Lift their elbow while keeping their hand low, rotating their shoulder inward. This creates strong pressure on the shoulder and drives them forward and onto their toes.

This lock is the basis of the classic "hammerlock" escort, and it is very effective. It is also very dangerous to the shoulder. Apply it slowly and release on the tap.

## Control Five: The Elbow Lift and Turn

The elbow lift and turn is less a lock than a way of steering someone by controlling their elbow. It is very useful for moving people without hurting them, and it is the gentlest control in this chapter.

### From any contact

You have contact with the other person's right arm, near the elbow.

*Control.* Place your left palm under their right elbow and your right hand on their right wrist or forearm. Lift the elbow slightly, which raises their shoulder and breaks their posture. At the same time, turn their forearm so that their palm faces up.

*Steering.* With their elbow lifted and their arm turned, you can steer them by turning their elbow in the direction you want them to go. Their body follows the elbow. Walk them where you want them, keeping the elbow lifted.

This is a very good control for dealing with someone who is a nuisance but not a serious threat, such as a friend who has had too much to drink. It is uncomfortable but not painful, and it gives you control of their movement without escalating the situation.

## Control Six: The Wrist Lock Escort

The wrist lock escort, sometimes called the *gooseneck*, bends the other person's wrist forward and holds it against your body, allowing you to walk them with very little effort.

### From the elbow lift

You have lifted the other person's right elbow and turned their arm, as in control five.

*Entry.* Bend their right arm so that their forearm is vertical and their hand is near your chest. Hold their elbow against your body with your left arm.

*Control.* With your right hand, bend their wrist forward so that their fingers point down and their palm faces them, like the neck of a goose. Press their fingers towards their forearm.

*Escort.* With their elbow trapped against your body and their wrist bent, you can walk them forward. If they resist, a small increase in the wrist pressure brings them up onto their toes.

This is the control used by many security staff and police officers for escorting a non-compliant but not violent person. It is very effective, but like all wrist locks it depends on pain compliance, and it may not work on someone who is very intoxicated or very determined.

## Combining Controls

Joint controls rarely work perfectly the first time. The other person will move, resist and try to escape. The skill is to flow from one control to the next as they escape, so that each escape leads them into another control.

The classic pattern is that each control has a natural escape, and each escape leads naturally into another control.

*Outward wrist turn and inward bend.* If the other person resists an outward wrist turn by pulling their hand back towards themselves, their wrist bends forward, which is the inward bend. Follow their resistance into it.

*Straight-arm bar and bent-arm lock.* If they resist a straight-arm bar by bending their arm, the arm is now bent and can be folded behind their back into the bent-arm lock.

*Elbow lift and wrist escort.* The elbow lift leads naturally into the wrist escort if more control is needed.

This idea of following resistance from one control to another is the joint-control version of the action–reaction principle that runs through this book. It is also where sensitivity becomes essential, because you must feel the resistance as it begins and move with it.

::: drill
**Drill 14.1: Control chains**

With a cooperative partner, apply each control in turn. Your partner offers a gentle, realistic resistance to each one, for example bending the arm against the straight-arm bar. You follow their resistance into the next control. Begin with pairs of controls, then build chains of three.

Go slowly, and stop on the tap. The aim is to learn the transitions, not to win.
:::

## Restraint and Release

Once you have someone under control, what then?

In most real situations, the answer is to *release them and leave*. A joint control has done its job if it has stopped an attack, moved someone out of your way, or given you a moment to escape. Holding someone in a lock for longer than necessary increases the risk of injury to them, legal risk to you, and the danger that their friends will intervene.

In some situations, such as a security role or a situation where you must protect someone else, you may need to hold someone for a time. If so, keep the following in mind.

*Use the lowest effective level.* Apply only as much pressure as is needed to maintain control.

*Watch their breathing.* Never put pressure on someone's neck, chest or back that restricts their breathing. People have died from being held face down with weight on their backs. This is called positional asphyxia, and it is a real and serious danger.

*Talk to them.* Tell them calmly what you want them to do. Many people stop resisting once they understand that resistance is pointless and that you are not trying to hurt them.

*Get help.* Do not try to hold someone on your own for a long time. Call the police or ask others to help.

*Release safely.* When you let go, step away to a safe distance at an angle, so that they cannot immediately attack you as they get up.

::: street
**Street note: restraint is risky**

Holding someone down is far more dangerous than many people realise. Restraint deaths, usually through positional asphyxia, have occurred when people were held face down with weight on their backs, sometimes for only a few minutes. If you ever have to restrain someone on the ground, keep your weight off their back and chest, watch their breathing and colour, and release the moment they stop resisting. If they say they cannot breathe, take it seriously.
:::

::: fault
**Fault: small and quick**

The most common fault in joint controls is trying to make them small and quick, using the hands alone, with a sharp twist. This rarely works against a resisting person, and it is very dangerous in training.

*Correction:* Make controls large and slow. Use your whole body, turn your hips, step, and drop your weight. A big, smooth movement of the whole body is much harder to resist than a small, quick twist of the hands, and it is far safer for your partner.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My partner just pulls their hand away before I can apply the wrist turn."

**Solution:** You are going for the lock too directly. Distract them first, with a strike or a pull, or use the moment their grab is fixed. Also make sure your first contact traps their hand: in the inward bend, for example, pin the hand against your chest before you try to lock it.

**Problem:** "The straight-arm bar just turns into a wrestling match."

**Solution:** You are probably not controlling the wrist well enough, so they can bend the arm. Draw the wrist forward to straighten the arm before you apply pressure, and keep it drawn forward throughout. If they bend the arm anyway, follow it into the bent-arm lock.

**Problem:** "Wrist locks don't seem to hurt my partner."

**Solution:** Some people have very flexible wrists, and wrist locks are less effective on them. Use elbow and shoulder controls instead, which are less dependent on individual flexibility. Also check your fulcrum: a wrist lock without a fixed fulcrum just moves the arm.

**Problem:** "I can apply the controls but I don't know what to do next."

**Solution:** In most real situations, the next thing is to release and leave. If you need to keep control, practise the escort positions until you can walk a partner around the room comfortably. And practise the transitions between controls, so that if one fails you flow into another.
:::

## Bringing It Together

These six controls, the outward wrist turn, the inward wrist bend, the straight-arm bar, the bent-arm lock, the elbow lift and turn, and the wrist lock escort, give you a practical set of options for controlling, moving and restraining another person.

Each works best from a realistic starting point, uses the whole body rather than the hands, and flows naturally into the others. Each must be trained slowly and safely, with immediate release on the tap. And each should be used, in real life, only as much and as long as necessary.

The final chapter of Part Five turns the tables: what to do when someone else has hold of you.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Use the movement the contact already contains: go with grabs, turn with pushes, pull punches further.
- The outward wrist turn rotates the wrist outward in a large circle driven by the body.
- The inward wrist bend uses a grab as its fulcrum, pinning the hand to your chest.
- The straight-arm bar and bent-arm lock control the elbow and shoulder; both are powerful and must be trained very carefully.
- The elbow lift and turn steers someone with minimal pain, ideal for low-level situations.
- The wrist lock escort lets you walk someone with very little effort.
- When a control fails, follow the resistance into another control.
- In real life, release and leave as soon as you can. If you must restrain, never restrict breathing.
:::

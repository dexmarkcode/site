# Preface

This book began as a set of handwritten notes for my own students. Every few weeks, someone would ask me a question that I could not answer properly in the few minutes at the end of a class. What is this movement in Seipai actually for? Why does my kakie fall apart when my partner speeds up? Why do I keep getting thrown when I try to clinch? How much force am I allowed to use if someone grabs me outside a pub? I began writing the answers down, drawing little diagrams in the margins, and before long the notes had become a folder, and the folder had become a box.

What you are holding is that box, sorted, tested and rewritten many times over.

It is not a history of Goju-Ryu, although there is a little history in it. It is not a kata manual, although the kata run through every chapter. And it is not a book of tricks. It is a practical guide to one particular part of the art, the close range, written for people who want to understand that range and function well within it, whether in the dojo, in sparring or, if it ever comes to it, in the street.

I have tried to write the book I wish I had been given twenty-five years ago. That means plain language rather than mystique. It means starting from problems that real people face rather than from techniques for their own sake. It means being honest about what works under pressure and what does not, about the dangers of close-range training, and about the law. And it means a great many diagrams, because a picture of where the elbow goes is worth a page of description.

Nothing in these pages is entirely my own. I have learned from teachers, training partners, students, judoka, boxers, wrestlers, police officers and door staff, and from a fair number of people who were trying very hard to hit me at the time. Where a method has proved its worth, I have kept it. Where it has not, I have left it out, however traditional it might be.

A word on language. I write in British English, so you will find *defence*, *centre* and *practise* (as a verb) throughout. I use Japanese terms where they are standard in Goju-Ryu dojo, always with an explanation, and there is a glossary at the back. I refer to the person applying a technique as *you* and to the training partner or opponent as *the other person* or *your partner*, which I find clearer than the traditional *tori* and *uke*.

Finally, please train safely. Close combat is the most intimate and the most dangerous part of martial arts, and the skills in this book can cause real harm. Find a good teacher, train with partners you trust, tap early, release quickly and look after each other. The art is meant to make you safer, not to give you injuries you will carry for the rest of your life.

*Dr Beverly J. Stivers*

# Kansetsu-waza: The Principles of Joint Control

::: chapter-intro
Joint controls let you move, steer and restrain another person by applying pressure to their joints in directions the joints do not want to go. Done well, they need surprisingly little strength, and they offer an option between doing nothing and striking. Done badly, they fail under pressure or cause serious injury. This chapter sets out the principles that make joint controls work, and the safety rules that make them trainable.
:::

There is a common belief, encouraged by a great many films and demonstrations, that joint locks are magical. A slight figure takes hold of a large attacker's wrist, makes a small twisting movement, and the attacker drops to his knees in agony. It looks effortless, and it suggests that anyone who learns the secret can control anyone else with a flick of the wrist.

The reality is more complicated. Joint controls can be extremely effective, and they can indeed allow a smaller person to control a larger one. But they work only when certain conditions are met: when the joint is isolated, when the other person's structure is broken, when the lever is long and the pressure is in the right direction, and above all when the other person is distracted, off balance or hurt. Against a calm, balanced, resisting person who is expecting it, a wrist lock is extremely hard to apply.

The old Okinawan teachers knew this well. The joint-manipulation methods they practised, sometimes called *tuidi* or *tuite*, were usually applied after a strike, during a grab, or as part of a throw, not as a standalone technique against a ready opponent. In this chapter and the next, we will learn them the same way.

## The Problem: When Striking Is Not the Answer

Most of this book has been about striking, trapping and throwing. These are effective tools, but they are not always the right ones.

Sometimes you need to *control* someone rather than hurt them. You may be dealing with a drunk friend, a confused or distressed person, a family member, or someone who is a nuisance rather than a serious threat. Striking them may be out of proportion, and the law expects you to use only as much force as is reasonable.

Sometimes you need to *move* someone: out of a doorway, away from another person, or towards an exit. A strike will not do that; a joint control can.

Sometimes you need to *hold* someone until help arrives. Again, a strike will not help.

And sometimes you are already in contact with someone, perhaps because they have grabbed you, and a joint control is simply the most natural response to the position you are in.

Joint controls give you options in all these situations. They form a middle ground between doing nothing and doing something drastic.

## The Principle: Lever, Fulcrum, Direction

Every joint control is an application of simple mechanics. To understand them, you need three ideas.

### The lever

A lever is a rigid bar that turns around a fixed point. The longer the lever, the less force is needed to turn it. In joint controls, the other person's limb is the lever. The forearm is a lever for the wrist; the whole arm is a lever for the shoulder; the forearm and hand together are a lever for the elbow.

The practical lesson is to *apply force as far from the joint as possible*. To control the elbow, push or pull near the wrist, not near the elbow itself. To control the shoulder, use the whole arm, not just the upper arm.

### The fulcrum

The fulcrum is the fixed point around which the lever turns. In joint controls, the fulcrum is usually the joint itself, or a point just beside it that you stabilise with your own body.

For the lever to work, the fulcrum must not move. If you push on someone's wrist to bend their elbow and their whole arm simply moves, there is no fulcrum and no control. You must fix one end of the lever, usually by pinning the joint with your hand, forearm or body, while you apply force at the other end.

### The direction

Every joint has directions in which it moves freely and directions in which it does not. The elbow bends and straightens freely, but it does not bend backwards or sideways. The wrist bends forwards and backwards and moves a little from side to side, and it rotates with the forearm, but it does not rotate beyond a certain point. The shoulder moves in many directions, but each has a limit.

Joint controls work by moving a joint *towards the limit of its range* in a direction it cannot escape from easily. The pressure must be in exactly the right direction. A few degrees off, and the other person will simply move with the pressure and escape.

## Isolating the Joint

A joint can be controlled only if it is *isolated*: if the other person cannot relieve the pressure by moving another part of their body.

Consider a straight-arm lock on the elbow. If you push on the back of someone's elbow while their arm is straight, they can relieve the pressure by bending the arm, by turning their shoulder, by stepping forward, or by turning their whole body. Each of these movements takes the elbow away from the limit of its range. For the lock to work, you must stop all of them.

This is why most effective joint controls involve more than one point of contact. One hand holds the wrist to stop it rotating; the other hand or forearm presses on the elbow; your body position stops their shoulder turning; and your footwork stops them stepping.

In practice, you cannot always isolate a joint completely, but you can make it very difficult to escape by combining the lock with balance-breaking. A person who is off balance cannot step or turn to relieve the pressure, because they need all their movement to stay on their feet.

## Structure: Yours and Theirs

Just as in striking and throwing, structure is the key to joint control.

*Breaking their structure.* A person with good structure, with their elbows down and their shoulders connected to their back, is very hard to lock, because their whole body supports each joint. A person with broken structure, with their elbow raised and their shoulder disconnected, is much easier. Many joint controls begin by raising the other person's elbow away from their body, which breaks the connection between the arm and the trunk.

*Keeping your own structure.* When you apply a joint control, use your whole body, not just your arms. Keep your elbows close to your body, turn your hips, and drop your weight into the control. A lock applied with arm strength alone is weak and can be resisted by a stronger person. A lock applied with your body weight behind it is much harder to fight.

## Pain, Compliance and Damage

Joint controls work in three different ways, and it is important to understand the differences.

### Pain compliance

At the first level, the joint is moved near the limit of its range, causing pain. The other person moves to relieve the pain, and in doing so goes where you want them to go. This is the level at which most joint controls are used for restraint and escort. It relies on the person feeling the pain and responding to it.

The weakness of pain compliance is that it does not work on everyone. People who are drunk, on drugs, highly agitated or simply very tough may not respond to pain at all. If pain compliance fails, you must be ready to do something else.

### Mechanical control

At the second level, the joint is held at the limit of its range so that the other person *cannot* move in certain directions, regardless of pain. Their skeleton is locked, and their body must follow the lock. This is more reliable than pain compliance, because it does not depend on the other person feeling or responding to pain.

Mechanical control usually requires a combination of a joint lock and a body position, such as an arm held straight with pressure on the shoulder while the person is face down on the floor.

### Damage

At the third level, the joint is forced beyond the limit of its range, causing a sprain, dislocation or fracture. This disables the limb. It is the most reliable level, but also the most serious, and it can only be justified in situations of grave danger.

In training, you work only at the first level, and very gently. You should learn where the second and third levels are, but you must never go there with a training partner.

::: street
**Street note: proportionate control**

In the United Kingdom, any force you use must be reasonable in the circumstances. Joint controls are often a more proportionate response than strikes in situations involving a nuisance or a low-level threat. But a joint control that dislocates an elbow or breaks a wrist is serious violence and must be justified as such. Apply only as much pressure as is needed to achieve control, and release the control as soon as the danger has passed.
:::

## The Safety Rules

Joint controls are the techniques in this book most likely to injure a training partner, because the line between pain and damage can be very thin, and because different people have very different ranges of movement. A wrist that can bend ninety degrees on one person may be injured at sixty degrees on another.

These are the rules I use in my own classes, and I strongly encourage you to adopt them.

*Tap and release.* The person receiving the lock taps clearly on their partner or on themselves, or says "tap" or "stop", as soon as they feel pain. The person applying the lock releases *immediately*. This rule is absolute.

*Slow application.* All joint controls are applied slowly in training, over two or three seconds, so that the receiver has time to tap before the pressure becomes dangerous. Never snap a lock on.

*No jerking.* Joint controls are never applied with a sudden jerk or twist in training. Sudden force does not give the joint time to signal pain before damage occurs.

*Know your partner.* Ask about previous injuries before you start. People with old wrist, elbow or shoulder injuries need particular care.

*Tap early.* As a receiver, tap as soon as you feel genuine discomfort. There is no prize for enduring a lock, and joint injuries can take months or years to heal.

*Supervision.* Joint controls should be learned under the supervision of an experienced instructor.

::: fault
**Fault: the late tap**

Pride makes many people tap late. They want to show they can take the pain, or they think they can escape. Then something goes wrong and a ligament tears.

*Correction:* Tap early, every time. You learn nothing by enduring a lock. You learn a great deal by feeling where the lock begins, and then working out how to escape before it gets there.
:::

## The Joints

Let us look briefly at the main joints and how they can be controlled. Specific techniques follow in Chapter 14.

### The fingers

The fingers are small and weak, and they can be controlled very easily by bending them backwards or sideways. A single finger bent back can move a large person. However, finger locks are easy to slip out of, and fingers break very easily. They are most useful for breaking a grip.

### The wrist

The wrist can be controlled by bending it forwards (flexion), bending it backwards (extension), turning it outwards or inwards beyond its normal range, or a combination. Wrist locks are the classic joint controls of aikido and jujutsu, and some of the most effective are found in Goju-Ryu kata. They work best when the elbow is bent and the arm is close to the body, because then the forearm forms a strong lever.

### The elbow

The elbow can be controlled by straightening it beyond its normal range (hyperextension), usually by pressing on the back of the elbow while the arm is held straight, or by bending it and rotating the forearm. Elbow controls are among the most reliable, because the elbow is a simple hinge with a clear limit.

### The shoulder

The shoulder can be controlled by rotating the arm inwards or outwards beyond its normal range, or by raising the arm behind the back. Shoulder controls are very powerful because the shoulder controls the whole upper body, and they are often the basis of restraint holds.

### The neck

The neck is technically a set of joints, and it can be controlled by twisting or bending the head. Neck controls are extremely dangerous and can cause paralysis or death. In this book I discuss neck control only in the context of head control in the clinch (Chapter 9) and turning takedowns (Chapter 11), and escapes from neck holds (Chapter 15). I do not teach neck locks.

## Setting Up Joint Controls

As I said at the beginning of this chapter, joint controls are very hard to apply against a calm, balanced, resisting person. They work best when they are set up. Here are the main ways to do that.

### After a strike

A strike causes pain, distraction and a flinch. In the moment of distraction, the other person's arm is often in a position where it can be locked, and their mind is elsewhere. Many traditional applications begin with a strike, often to the face or a nerve point, followed immediately by a lock.

### During a grab

When someone grabs you, their hand is fixed in place, and their arm is extended towards you. This is the classic setup for wrist and elbow locks, because the grab provides the fulcrum for you. You simply have to supply the lever.

### During a push or punch

When someone pushes or punches, their arm is extended, and their weight is moving towards you. A pull along the line of the push or punch, combined with a lock, uses their own force to apply the control.

### From a trap

A trap in which you have hold of the other person's arm is a natural setup for a joint control. If you have pinned their arm against their body or pulled it across their body, it is already partly controlled.

### From a throw

Many throws, particularly the arm-bar takedown in Chapter 11, naturally lead into a joint control on the ground.

::: kata
**Kata link: Shisochin and Kururunfa**

Shisochin contains movements in which one hand presses down while the other pulls up, sometimes with a turn of the body. These are often read as elbow or shoulder controls applied after a grab. Kururunfa, sometimes described as a kata of "holding on long and striking suddenly", contains several sequences of grabbing, turning and pressing that suit joint control applications very well. Both kata reward close study with a careful partner.
:::

## Joint Controls and Sensitivity

Finally, joint controls depend heavily on sensitivity. To apply a lock effectively, you must feel where the other person's structure is weak, which direction their joint is resisting, and when they are about to move. The best people at joint controls are almost always people with excellent sensitivity, developed through kakie or a similar practice.

This is another reason why the order of this book matters. If you jump straight to joint controls without the sensitivity of Part Two, you will find them very difficult. If you have spent months in kakie, you will find that you can feel the lock developing and adjust it moment by moment.

::: drill
**Drill 13.1: Finding the end of range**

Work with a cooperative partner. Take their wrist gently and move it slowly through its full range in each direction: forward, back, side to side and rotation. Feel the point where the movement starts to resist. Do not go beyond it.

Then do the same for the elbow and shoulder. Your aim is to learn what the end of range feels like, so that you can recognise it in a lock. Swap roles, and notice how different people's ranges are.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 13.2: Lock on the grab**

Your partner grabs your wrist firmly. Without trying any particular technique, explore the grab: turn your hand, move your elbow, step around. Find the positions in which you can feel pressure developing on their wrist, elbow or shoulder. When you find one, apply it slowly until they tap.

This exploratory drill teaches you that joint controls are not fixed techniques but natural consequences of positions. Once you understand that, the specific techniques in the next chapter will make much more sense.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My locks never work on resisting partners."

**Solution:** That is normal. Locks rarely work on calm, balanced, resisting people. Set them up with a strike, a grab, a trap or a balance-break, and apply them while the other person is distracted or moving. Also check that you are isolating the joint and that your direction is precise.

**Problem:** "My partner says it doesn't hurt."

**Solution:** Your direction is slightly off, or there is no fulcrum, so the other person is simply moving with the pressure. Slow down, find the fulcrum, and adjust the angle by small amounts until your partner feels it. Different people feel locks at different points, so be patient.

**Problem:** "I'm worried about hurting my partner."

**Solution:** That is a good worry to have. Follow the safety rules strictly: slow application, no jerking, immediate release on tap. Ask your partner about old injuries. And remember that most of the learning in joint controls happens at very low pressure.

**Problem:** "I'm much smaller than my partner. Can I really lock someone that big?"

**Solution:** Yes, if you use leverage and set the lock up well. A long lever and a well-fixed fulcrum multiply your force enormously. But do not expect locks to work on a large, calm person who is resisting. Set them up, and combine them with balance-breaking, so that the other person's size and strength are working against them.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Joint controls are a valuable option between doing nothing and striking. They let you move, steer and hold another person, often with relatively little force.

They rest on simple mechanics: a lever, a fulcrum and a precise direction. They require the joint to be isolated and the other person's structure to be broken. They work at three levels, pain compliance, mechanical control and damage, and in training you must stay firmly at the first. And they work best when set up by a strike, grab, push, trap or throw.

The next chapter applies these principles to specific controls of the wrist, elbow and shoulder.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Joint controls work by moving a joint to the end of its range in a direction it cannot escape.
- Every control needs a lever (as long as possible), a fulcrum (fixed) and a precise direction.
- Isolate the joint by stopping the other person relieving the pressure through other movements.
- Break their structure, keep yours, and apply locks with the body, not the arms.
- Controls work at three levels: pain compliance, mechanical control and damage. Train only at the first.
- Follow the safety rules absolutely: tap early, release immediately, apply slowly, never jerk.
- Set controls up with strikes, grabs, pushes, traps and throws. Against a calm, resisting person, they rarely work alone.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Using Drill 13.1, compare the wrist range of three different partners. How much does it vary? What does that tell you about safety?
2. Choose one movement from Shisochin or Kururunfa and work out, with a partner, whether it could be a joint control. Where is the lever, the fulcrum and the direction?
3. Think of three real situations in which a joint control would be more appropriate than a strike. What would make it the better choice in each?

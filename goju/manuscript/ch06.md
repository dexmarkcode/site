# From Contact to Finish: Trap-and-Strike Combinations

::: chapter-intro
A single trap opens a door. A single strike may or may not go through it. What makes close-range fighting effective is the flow from one action to the next: the strike that provokes a reaction, the trap that catches the reaction, the second strike that lands over the trap, and the change of position that makes the next strike possible. This chapter is about building that flow, so that your close-range work stops being a series of separate techniques and becomes a continuous attack.
:::

When I watch beginners at close range, I see the same pattern again and again. They do one thing, then stop, then look to see what happened, then try to think of the next thing. The pause might last only half a second, but at close range half a second is an eternity. In that pause, the other person recovers, adjusts, and very often hits back.

When I watch experienced close-range fighters, I see something completely different. Their actions run into each other. A strike becomes a trap, which becomes a second strike, which becomes a pull, which becomes a knee. There is no pause in which the other person can recover. Each action creates the conditions for the next one.

This is not because experienced fighters have memorised long sequences. It is because they have learned the *connections* between actions: what each action does to the other person, and what that opens up. Once you understand the connections, you can link actions together on the fly, adapting to whatever the other person does.

## The Problem: The Gap Between Actions

The gap between one action and the next is where you are most vulnerable. During an action, you are doing something to the other person, and they are usually busy dealing with it. Between actions, you are doing nothing, and they are free to act.

There are three common causes of the gap.

The first is *waiting to see what happened*. You strike, then pause to see whether it landed. This is natural, but it is fatal at close range. By the time you have seen the result, the moment has passed.

The second is *resetting*. After each action, you return to a neutral position, such as a guard, before starting the next. This feels safe, but it means each action starts from scratch, and the other person has time to reset too.

The third is *not knowing what comes next*. You have a strike and a trap in your repertoire, but you have never practised moving from one to the other, so when the moment comes you have to think about it.

The solution to all three is the same: practise the connections until they become automatic.

## The Principle: Action and Reaction

Every action you take at close range causes a reaction in the other person. If you understand the reaction, you can prepare for it, and your next action can be aimed not at where they are now, but at where your first action will put them.

This is the central idea of combination work, and it is worth looking at in detail.

### Strikes cause flinches

When you strike at someone's face, even if you miss, they flinch. Their head moves back, their hands come up towards their face, and their eyes close or narrow. That flinch is predictable, and it opens other targets. The body and ribs are exposed when the hands come up. The legs are exposed when the weight shifts back. And the arms, now up in front of the face, can be trapped.

### Traps cause resistance

When you pin or pull an arm, the other person resists. They pull the arm back, push it forward or try to twist it free. That resistance is also predictable. If you pull their arm towards you, they pull it back towards themselves, and you can push it into their body. If you push their arm into their body, they push back, and you can pull it towards you.

### Pulls cause posture changes

When you pull someone forward, they lean back to resist. When you push them back, they lean forward. When you pull their head down, they try to lift it. Each of these posture changes opens new targets and new opportunities for throws.

### Pain causes withdrawal

When you strike something that hurts, the other person tends to withdraw that part of the body. A strike to the thigh makes them lift or move the leg. A strike to the ribs makes them bring the elbow down. A strike to the face makes them turn the head away.

Once you start looking at close-range fighting in terms of action and reaction, combinations become much easier to understand. You are not trying to remember a sequence. You are reading the reaction your last action caused, and choosing the action that takes advantage of it.

## Three Basic Combinations

Rather than give you dozens of combinations, I want to teach you three in detail. Each illustrates a different principle, and between them they form the backbone of close-range combination work. Once you understand them, you will find that most other combinations are variations on these three.

### Combination one: strike, pin, strike

This is the simplest and most useful combination in close combat.

You throw a short strike at the face with your lead hand, such as a back fist, a palm heel or a jab. The other person flinches and raises their hands. As their lead arm comes up, you pin it against their body with your lead hand, using the palm pin from Chapter 5. Their lead arm is now briefly out of action, and you strike over it with your rear hand.

The beauty of this combination is that the first strike sets up the trap, and the trap sets up the second strike. You do not need to find their arm; the flinch delivers it to you.

The key detail is timing. The pin must come at the moment their arm rises, not after it has settled into a guard. That means the pin must follow the first strike instantly. In practice, your lead hand strikes and then, without withdrawing, drops onto their arm.

### Combination two: pull, strike, pull

The second combination uses the hooking pull.

You hook the other person's lead wrist or forearm and pull it towards your hip. This drags their lead shoulder forward and turns their head towards you. As they come forward, you strike their face or neck with your rear hand. They will instinctively pull back against your pull. As they do, you release the hook and immediately hook their other arm, or the same arm at a different point, and pull again, drawing them onto another strike.

This combination teaches the rhythm of pull and release. Each pull draws them onto a strike; each release lets their own resistance carry them into a new position.

### Combination three: strike, change line, strike

The third combination uses a change of position rather than a trap.

You strike from the inside line, for example with a short straight punch to the face. The other person blocks or covers with their lead arm. As they block, you use the contact with their arm to pass it, moving to the outside line with a small step or pivot. Now their blocking arm is between you and their rear hand, and you strike from the outside with a hook or an elbow to the side of the head.

This combination teaches you to use the other person's defence as a bridge to a better position. Their block gives you contact, and the contact gives you the information and leverage to move around their arm.

![**Figure 6.2** Strike, change line, strike. (1) A short straight punch from the inside is blocked. (2) Using the contact, you pass the blocking arm and pivot to the outside line. (3) From the outside, an elbow or hook lands on the side of the head.](figures/fig6_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill 6.1: The three combinations**

Your partner wears focus mitts and holds them close to their face, as if they were a guard. Work through each of the three combinations slowly, with your partner reacting as described: flinching on the first strike, resisting the pull, blocking the straight punch. Your partner moves a mitt into position for each strike.

Do ten repetitions of each combination on each side, at half speed. Then do them at three-quarter speed, with your partner varying the reaction slightly each time. The aim is not to memorise the combination but to feel how each action leads to the next.
:::

## Keeping the Arms Busy

The principle that ties all close-range combinations together is this: *keep the other person's arms busy*. As long as their arms are occupied, defending, resisting traps or recovering from pulls, they cannot be used to hit you. The moment their arms are free, you are in danger.

This means that the best combinations are those that give the other person's arms something to do at every moment. A strike that they have to block keeps an arm busy. A trap keeps an arm busy. A pull keeps an arm busy, because they are using it to resist. Even a strike to the body keeps their arms busy, because they drop them to protect themselves.

What does *not* keep their arms busy is a gap. If you strike and then pause, their arms are free. If you trap and then hold the trap without doing anything, they will free the arm or use the other one.

::: fault
**Fault: the one-handed fighter**

Many people, when they start working at close range, use only one hand. They trap with one hand and then strike with the same hand, or they strike repeatedly with their dominant hand while the other hangs uselessly. This leaves one of the other person's arms completely free.

*Correction:* Work both hands in every combination. One hand controls, the other strikes, and then they swap. If you find yourself using only one hand, deliberately start your next combination with the other one.
:::

## Short Strikes for Close Range

Combinations at close range use short strikes. Long, straight punches with a full step need more room than you have. Here are the main strikes we use in this chapter, and which we will develop further in later chapters.

The *back fist* (*uraken*) is a snapping strike with the back of the knuckles, usually to the face or the side of the head. It is fast, needs very little room, and is often the first strike in a combination because it causes a sharp flinch.

The *palm heel* (*shotei*) is a strike with the heel of the open hand, usually to the chin, the nose or the side of the jaw. It is safer for your hand than a closed fist and very effective at close range.

The *hammer fist* (*tettsui*) is a strike with the bottom of the closed fist, like hitting a table with your fist. It can be thrown downwards, sideways or backwards, and it is very powerful at close range.

The *short hook* is a curved punch to the side of the head or body, thrown with the elbow bent at about ninety degrees. It goes around the other person's guard rather than through it.

The *short uppercut* is a rising punch, usually to the chin or the solar plexus, thrown from close range with the elbow bent. It comes up through the gap between the other person's arms.

The *forearm strike* uses the bony edge of the forearm, often to the side of the neck or the jaw. It is very strong and very hard to stop at close range.

The *elbow* is so important at close range that it has its own chapter, Chapter 7. For now, note that many combinations end with an elbow, because once you have trapped an arm and closed the distance, the elbow is often the most natural strike.

## Combinations from Kata

The Goju-Ryu kata are full of combinations, and they illustrate the principles of this chapter beautifully once you know how to look.

::: kata
**Kata link: Saifa**

After the opening release, Saifa moves through a series of actions that can be read as a single close-range combination: a pull with one hand while the other covers, a back fist to the face, a hammer fist downward, and a step that takes you around the other person. Read as a sequence of blocks and strikes against several attackers, it looks disjointed. Read as a combination against one person at close range, it flows: release, pull them onto the back fist, strike down as they double over, and move to their side.
:::

::: kata
**Kata link: Seiyunchin**

Seiyunchin is almost entirely performed in a low straddle stance, and it contains no kicks. Many of its movements involve both hands working together: one hand pulling or pinning while the other strikes or presses. The repeated pattern of a pulling hand returning to the hip while the other hand strikes is a lesson in combination work: control with one hand, strike with the other, and use the lowered stance to break the other person's posture as you pull.
:::

When you practise kata, try to identify the combinations within them. Look for places where one hand pulls while the other strikes, where a block is followed immediately by a strike from the same arm, or where a turn or step changes the angle. Each of these is a combination principle in action.

## Building Combinations Under Pressure

Combinations in drills are one thing. Combinations against a resisting partner are quite another. Here is a progression for building them up.

### Stage one: fixed combination, fixed reaction

You perform a specific combination, and your partner reacts in a specific way. This is where you learn the mechanics. Drill 6.1 is a stage-one drill.

### Stage two: fixed combination, varied reaction

You perform a specific combination, but your partner varies their reaction. Sometimes they flinch, sometimes they block, sometimes they step back. You have to adapt the rest of the combination to their reaction.

### Stage three: free combination, cooperative partner

You strike, trap and move freely, and your partner reacts naturally but does not counter-attack. You build combinations on the fly based on what you feel.

### Stage four: free combination, resisting partner

Your partner reacts and resists, and may counter with light strikes when you leave a gap. This is close-range sparring, which we cover in detail in Chapter 17.

::: drill
**Drill 6.2: Three actions**

Your partner wears gloves and a head guard and stands in a guard position at close range. You must perform exactly three actions, any combination of strikes, traps, pulls and changes of position, without a pause between them. Your partner reacts naturally but does not counter. Strikes are light and controlled.

After each set of three actions, reset. Do ten sets, then swap. The rule of exactly three forces you to link actions rather than stopping after one, while keeping the drill short enough to stay controlled.

As you improve, increase the number of actions to four, then five. Most people find that beyond five, the quality drops sharply. That is fine: in real exchanges, most decisive combinations are short.
:::

## When the Combination Breaks Down

Every combination breaks down eventually. The other person does something you did not expect, or your trap fails, or your strike misses. What matters is what you do next.

The worst thing you can do is to stop. A broken combination leaves you close to the other person with no plan, and that is exactly when you are most vulnerable.

The second-worst thing is to try to force the combination through, for example by grabbing harder for an arm that has escaped. This usually leaves you overextended and off balance.

The best thing is to *flow into whatever is available*. If your trap fails and their arm escapes, their arm is now somewhere else, and that may open a different target. If your strike misses because they moved their head, their head has moved, and that may set up a different strike or a pull. If they push you away, you can use the push to pivot to the outside.

This is where kakie becomes so important. A person who has done a lot of kakie is used to the contact changing constantly and to adapting moment by moment. A combination that breaks down is just another change of contact.

When there is genuinely nothing available, the fallback is simple: *cover, frame and reset*. Bring your forearms up to protect your head, put a forearm or palm on the other person to create a frame, and use the frame to create space or move to a better angle. Then start again.

::: street
**Street note: the short combination**

On the street, the ideal combination is short: two or three actions that create enough damage or enough space to escape. A long exchange of traps and strikes increases the chance of something going wrong, of you being hurt, of friends joining in, and of the incident escalating. Train long combinations in the dojo for skill. On the street, aim to strike, create an opening and leave.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "I always pause after my first strike."

**Solution:** This is the most common problem of all. Use Drill 6.2 with the rule of exactly three actions. Also practise striking without looking at the result: hit a pad and immediately follow with a second action, without checking whether the first one landed cleanly. Over time, your body learns to move on without waiting for confirmation.

**Problem:** "My partner doesn't react the way the combination expects."

**Solution:** Good. That means your partner is behaving like a real person. Move to stage-two training, where your partner varies their reaction deliberately, and practise adapting. Remember that combinations are not fixed sequences; they are chains of action and reaction. If the reaction changes, the next action changes with it.

**Problem:** "I run out of breath after a few seconds of combinations."

**Solution:** You are probably holding your breath or tensing throughout. Breathe out on every strike, keep your shoulders relaxed between actions, and use the pulse of tension and relaxation from Chapter 2. Also build your conditioning with rounds of pad work at close range: thirty seconds of continuous combinations, thirty seconds of rest, repeated six to eight times.

**Problem:** "Whenever I get close, my partner just grabs me and we end up wrestling."

**Solution:** That means your combinations are leaving gaps that they can fill with a clinch. Keep your strikes shorter and your traps tighter. Also learn the clinch skills in Chapter 9 and the throws in Part Four, because some exchanges will inevitably become grappling, and you need to be comfortable there too.
:::

## Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Two. We have moved from the quiet listening of kakie, through the specific traps that grow out of it, to the combinations that turn traps into finished exchanges.

The thread running through all three chapters is sensitivity: feeling what the other person is doing and responding to it. Kakie develops the feeling. Trapping uses it to control the arms. Combinations use it to link actions together, so that each action is aimed at the reaction the previous one caused.

In Part Three, we turn to the short weapons: elbows and knees. These are the strikes that close-range combinations most often end with, and they deserve detailed study in their own right.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- The gap between actions is where you are most vulnerable. Close it by practising the connections between actions.
- Every action causes a reaction. Strikes cause flinches, traps cause resistance, pulls cause posture changes, and pain causes withdrawal.
- The three core combinations are strike–pin–strike, pull–strike–pull, and strike–change line–strike.
- Keep the other person's arms busy at every moment. A gap gives them a free hand.
- Use short strikes at close range: back fist, palm heel, hammer fist, short hook, short uppercut, forearm and elbow.
- Build combinations from fixed to free, and from cooperative to resisting.
- When a combination breaks down, flow into whatever is available, or cover, frame and reset.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Choose one strike and ask your partner to react to it in five different ways. For each reaction, find one follow-up action. Write them down.
2. Perform Saifa slowly and identify every place where one hand controls while the other strikes. How many did you find?
3. Film yourself doing Drill 6.2. Count the pauses. Where do they occur? What do you think you were doing during each pause?
:::

# Trapping: Controlling the Arms and Clearing the Line

::: chapter-intro
A trap is any action that stops the other person using one of their arms, even for a moment. It might be a pin against their body, a pull that drags the arm out of the way, or a jam that stops it before it starts. At close range, where both people's hands are within easy reach of each other's heads, a single half-second in which one of their arms is out of action can decide the whole exchange. This chapter teaches the main trapping methods found in Goju-Ryu and explains how to make them work against someone who does not want to be trapped.
:::

Trapping has a mixed reputation. Some people think of it as the most sophisticated skill in close-range fighting. Others think of it as a cooperative fantasy that never works against a resisting opponent. Both views have some truth in them.

The truth is that trapping, as it is often taught, does not work well. Long, elaborate sequences in which one person pins the other's arm, then the other arm, then switches hands and pins again, rely on a partner who leaves their arms out to be trapped and does not move. Under real pressure, nobody does that. Their arms are moving, pulling back, punching, grabbing and pushing, and by the time you have trapped one arm the situation has already changed.

But trapping as a *principle* works very well indeed. The idea that you should control the other person's arms while yours are free is one of the oldest and most effective ideas in close combat. The trick is to keep traps short, simple and opportunistic, and to use them as a bridge to something else, usually a strike, a throw or an escape. Think of a trap as a door you hold open for half a second rather than a room you intend to live in.

## The Problem: Two Arms in the Way

At close range, the other person has two arms between you and their head and body. Even an untrained person will instinctively raise them when threatened. Those arms block your strikes, grab your clothing, push you away and, of course, hit you.

You have three basic choices.

You can *go around* the arms, using angles and hooking strikes to reach targets the arms are not protecting. We looked at angles in Chapter 3.

You can *go through* the arms, hitting so hard or so suddenly that the guard is overwhelmed. This works sometimes, particularly against untrained people, but it is unreliable against a good guard and a bigger opponent.

Or you can *move the arms out of the way*. That is trapping.

The great advantage of trapping is that it deals with both halves of the problem at once. When you trap an arm, you remove it as a shield and as a weapon at the same moment. And if you trap it in the right place, it may also block the other arm, as we saw with the outside line in Chapter 3.

## The Principle: Control the Elbow, Clear the Line

There are many trapping techniques, but they rest on a small number of principles.

### Control the elbow, not the wrist

The wrist is the part of the arm that moves fastest and most freely. It is very hard to catch and very easy to pull free. The elbow, on the other hand, is close to the body, moves relatively slowly, and controls the whole arm. If you control the elbow, the wrist and hand have nowhere to go.

This is why many of the most effective traps in Goju-Ryu involve pressing on or pulling at the area just above or below the elbow rather than grabbing the wrist. A grab at the wrist is easy to break; a press on the elbow is much harder to escape.

### Pin against something

An arm that is pinned against the other person's own body, or against their other arm, is much easier to control than an arm in open space. When you press an arm into their chest, they have to push against you and against their own body to free it. When you press one arm across the other, both are tied up at once.

### Clear the line, then use it

A trap is only useful if it opens a line for something else. The question to ask whenever you trap an arm is: *what does this let me do?* If the answer is "nothing", the trap is wasted effort. Usually the answer should be a strike, a change of position or a throw, and it should follow immediately.

### Short duration

A trap rarely lasts more than half a second against a resisting opponent. Plan for that. Do not try to hold a trap while you think of what to do next. Trap and strike should feel like a single action.

### Feel, do not force

Traps work best when they follow the other person's movement rather than fighting it. If they are pulling their arm back, a trap that helps them pull it further, into their own body, works far better than one that tries to pull it the other way. This is where the sensitivity from kakie becomes essential.

## The Main Traps

Here are the traps I find most useful. Each is described in its basic form, followed by notes on how to make it work under pressure.

### The palm pin (osae)

The palm pin is the simplest and most useful trap of all. You place your palm on the other person's forearm or upper arm, near the elbow, and press it into their body. That arm is now briefly pinned and cannot be used to strike or block.

The pin works best when the other person's arm is already near their body, for example when they are holding a guard, have just pulled back a punch, or are pushing against you with a bent arm. You simply add a little pressure in the direction it is already going.

The most common use is to pin the lead arm with your lead hand and then strike over the top of it with your rear hand. Because their lead arm is pinned across or against their body, it cannot rise to block the strike.

![**Figure 5.1** The palm pin. (1) Your lead hand meets the opponent's lead forearm near the elbow. (2) You press the arm into their body, closing their guard. (3) Your rear hand strikes over the pinned arm.](figures/fig5_1.png)

*Under pressure:* The palm pin fails when you reach for the arm, because the arm moves before you get there. Make it from contact. If your forearm is already touching theirs, as it is after a bridge or in kakie, you can slide your hand into position without reaching. And press towards their centre, not straight back, so that the arm is pinned across their own body.

### The hooking pull (hikite)

The hooking pull uses an open hand, shaped like a hook, to catch the other person's wrist or forearm and pull it towards your own hip. As the arm comes towards you, it drags their shoulder forward and turns their body, opening the side of the head and the ribs.

This is the true meaning of the *hikite*, the pulling hand that returns to the hip in nearly every basic karate technique. In kihon it often looks like a gesture for balance. In application it is a hand that has caught something and is pulling it.

The hooking pull is especially effective against a straight punch or a grab. As the arm comes towards you, you hook it and pull it along its own line of travel, which unbalances the other person and draws them onto your strike.

![**Figure 5.2** The hooking pull. Your lead hand hooks the opponent's wrist and pulls it back towards your hip (the hikite), drawing their shoulder forward and turning them onto your rear-hand strike.](figures/fig5_2.png)

*Under pressure:* Do not try to grip the wrist tightly. A tight grip is slow to make and easy to feel. A hook, with the fingers together and the thumb alongside them, catches the arm without needing a full grip, and can be released instantly. And pull with your body, not just your arm: turn your hips and step back slightly as you pull.

### The forearm jam

The forearm jam stops an arm before it can be used. You place your forearm against the other person's arm, usually across the inside of the elbow or the bicep, as they start to punch or pull back. The arm is jammed and cannot extend.

This is particularly useful against hooking punches and against someone who is trying to draw their arm back to punch. Instead of waiting for the punch to come, you stop it before it starts.

*Under pressure:* The jam must be early. Once the punch is moving fast, a jam will be overpowered. That means you need to read the preparation: the shoulder dipping, the elbow drawing back, the weight shifting. This is the kind of reading that kakie trains.

### The cross trap

The cross trap uses one of the other person's arms to block the other. You take their lead arm and press or pull it across their body so that it lies over their rear arm. Now neither arm can be used, at least for a moment.

The cross trap is particularly effective from the outside position. From there, a pull on the lead arm across their body naturally brings it over their rear arm, and you can strike over the top of both.

![**Figure 5.3** The cross trap, seen from above. From the outside line, your lead hand pushes the opponent's lead arm across their body so that it lies over their rear arm. Both arms are briefly tied up and the head is open.](figures/fig5_3.png)

### The elbow lift

The elbow lift raises the other person's arm, usually by lifting under the elbow with your forearm or palm, so that it can no longer protect their body. It also tends to lift them slightly onto their toes and break their posture.

This is useful against someone who is holding a high guard, gripping your collar, or pushing at your face. Lifting the elbow exposes the ribs, the armpit and the side of the body, and sets up knees and short strikes.

### The pass

The pass is less a trap than a way of clearing an arm out of the line and moving past it. You take contact with the other person's arm and guide it past your body as you move to the side, so that the arm goes one way and you go the other. Once the arm has passed, you are on the outside line, with their arm behind you.

The pass is very common in the kata, often as a circular sweeping movement of the open hand that looks like a block. It is one of the safest and most reliable ways to get to the outside.

::: kata
**Kata link: kake uke and mawashi uke**

The hooking block, *kake uke*, appears throughout the Goju-Ryu kata. Read as a block, it is a slightly odd movement: the hand rises, turns over and pulls back. Read as a trap, it makes perfect sense. The hand meets the incoming arm, hooks over it and pulls it into a position of control.

The circular block, *mawashi uke*, or *tora guchi* (the "tiger's mouth"), is similarly rich. It contains a pass, a pin and a double-handed push or strike, all in one movement. At the end of Sanchin, Tensho and several other kata, mawashi uke is the culminating movement, and it can be understood as a complete summary of trapping: take the arm, turn it aside, pin it, and strike.
:::

## The Free Hand

I have mentioned the free hand several times already, and I want to emphasise it now. In almost every trap, one of your hands is controlling one of the other person's arms. That leaves you with one hand free, and that free hand is usually your weapon.

But it is not only your weapon. It is also your protection against their free hand. Remember that if you trap one of their arms, they still have the other one. A trap that leaves you open to their other hand is a bad trap.

There are three ways to deal with this.

First, you can trap in a way that ties up *both* arms, as with the cross trap.

Second, you can trap from the *outside line*, so that their trapped arm is between you and their free hand.

Third, you can use your free hand to *cover* rather than strike, at least for a moment, and strike with an elbow, a knee or your head instead.

Most of the time, the second option is the best. This is why the outside line keeps coming up. A trap from the inside line, between their two arms, leaves you exposed to the free hand. A trap from the outside, beyond their elbow, puts their own arm in the way.

## Trapping from Different Contacts

Traps almost never begin from a clean, neutral position. They begin from whatever contact happens to arise. Here are the common contacts and the traps that flow naturally from them.

### From a bridge

You have closed in with a cover entry and your forearm is in contact with their forearm. From here, the palm pin and the hooking pull are the most natural traps. If your forearm is on the outside of their arm, you can slide your hand down to their elbow and press it across their body. If it is on the inside, you can turn your hand over, hook their wrist and pull.

### From a block

You have blocked a punch and your forearm is in contact with their punching arm as it starts to withdraw. This is one of the best moments to trap, because their arm is moving back towards them anyway. Follow it with a palm pin, pressing it into their body as it retracts, and strike with your other hand.

### From a grab

They have grabbed your clothing, wrist or arm. A grabbing arm is a gift, because it is fixed in place and its owner has committed one hand to holding you. You can pin the grabbing hand against your own body, trapping it there, while you strike with your free hand. Or you can pass the grabbing arm and move to the outside line. We look at releases from grabs in more detail in Chapter 15.

### From a push

They push you with one or both hands. A pushing arm is extended and committed, and its owner's weight is moving towards you. You can pivot, as we practised in Chapter 3, and pass the pushing arm as it goes by, or you can pin the pushing arm against your chest with your hand and turn, so that their own push takes them off balance.

### From a guard

They are holding a high guard, hands up in front of their face. This is the hardest situation to trap from, because they are not committing anything. Here, the best traps tend to be quick, light pulls and pins that move the guard just enough to open a line for a short strike, often combined with a feint to make them move their hands first.

::: drill
**Drill 5.1: Trap from any contact**

Your partner wears focus mitts. They create a contact of their choosing, such as a punch that you block, a grab on your wrist, a push on your chest or a high guard that you touch. As soon as contact is made, you apply the most natural trap you can feel, followed immediately by a single strike to a mitt that your partner presents.

Begin slowly and cooperatively, with your partner telling you which contact they will use. Progress to random contacts. The aim is to learn to recognise the trap that each contact offers, rather than trying to force a favourite trap into every situation.
:::

## Making Traps Work Against Resistance

All of this is well and good when your partner cooperates. Against resistance, traps become much harder. Here are the principles I have found most useful for making them work against someone who is trying to stop you.

### Use their reaction

The best time to trap an arm is when the other person is moving it for their own reasons. When they are drawing back to punch, the arm is coming towards their body and can be pinned there. When they are pushing forward, the arm is extended and can be pulled or passed. When they are reacting to a strike, the arm is moving to protect themselves and can be trapped in that position.

This is why traps and strikes work together so well. Strike first, and the other person reacts. Their reaction moves their arms, and you trap the arms in the position the reaction has put them in. Then strike again over the trapped arm.

### Short, sharp and simple

Under pressure, complicated traps fall apart. The traps that survive are the simple ones: a single pin, a single pull, a single pass. Learn a small number of simple traps really well rather than a large number of complicated ones.

### Use body weight, not arm strength

A trap made with arm strength alone is easy to overpower. A trap made with body weight behind it is not. When you pin an arm, lean slightly into it, so that the pressure comes from your whole body. When you pull an arm, turn your hips and shift your weight back, so that the pull comes from your legs.

### Combine with off-balancing

An arm is much easier to trap when its owner is off balance, because they have to use it to recover. Combine traps with small pulls and pushes that break the other person's posture, and their arms will often end up exactly where you want them.

### Accept failure gracefully

Many traps will fail. The other person will pull their arm free, move away or strike first. That is fine. A failed trap that leaves you in a good position is not a disaster; it is just one more step in the exchange. What you must avoid is a failed trap that leaves you exposed, for example reaching too far for an arm and leaving your head open. Keep your structure, keep your free hand ready, and if the trap does not work, move on.

::: fault
**Fault: chasing the arm**

The most common error in trapping practice is reaching for the other person's arm. You see it, you want it, and you stretch towards it. By the time your hand arrives, the arm has moved, and you are now off balance with your arm extended and your head forward.

*Correction:* Never reach. Trap from contact, or wait for the arm to come to you. If you cannot trap from where you are, change your position first.
:::

## Trapping in Sparring

Most sport karate rules do not allow much trapping. Grabbing is often prohibited or allowed only briefly, and many rule sets stop the action as soon as the fighters are in contact. Nevertheless, simple traps can be very effective within the rules.

The most useful sparring trap is a quick palm pin or slap on the opponent's lead hand, followed instantly by a reverse punch. If the pin is quick and light, it is usually within the rules, and it creates a moment in which the opponent's lead hand cannot block. Another useful trap is a brief hooking pull on the opponent's lead hand as you step in, which draws them onto your punch.

In rule sets that allow more contact and grappling, such as some knockdown and mixed rules, the full range of traps becomes available, and they can be devastatingly effective, particularly when combined with elbows, knees and throws.

::: drill
**Drill 5.2: Pin and punch**

Both partners wear sparring gloves. Partner A holds a guard and moves around freely, but does not strike. Partner B tries to land light punches to A's head and body. B may use a quick palm pin or hooking pull on A's lead hand to open a line, but must strike within half a second of the trap.

Rounds of one minute. A scores a point every time B's trap is held for more than half a second without a strike following. This teaches B to keep traps short and connected to strikes.
:::

::: street
**Street note**

In a real confrontation, a trap is almost always a means to an end. You are not trying to hold someone's arm; you are trying to create a moment in which you can strike, escape or both. The most useful street traps are the pin against your own body when someone grabs you, the pass when someone pushes or swings at you, and the hooking pull when someone throws a big punch. Keep them simple, and follow them immediately with a strike or with leaving.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My traps work in drills but never in sparring."

**Solution:** In drills your partner leaves their arms in place. In sparring they do not. Work on trapping from reactions: strike first, then trap the arm where it ends up. Use Drill 5.1 with random contacts and Drill 5.2 with a resisting partner. And check that you are trapping from contact rather than reaching.

**Problem:** "When I trap one arm, I get hit by the other."

**Solution:** You are trapping from the inside line. Move to the outside before or as you trap, so that their trapped arm is between you and their free hand. If you cannot get to the outside, use your free hand to cover rather than strike, and strike with an elbow or knee instead.

**Problem:** "My partner is much stronger than me and just pulls their arm free."

**Solution:** Do not try to hold the arm with strength. Use your body weight, keep the trap short, and pin the arm against something, either their body or their other arm. Also use their pulling: if they are pulling their arm back, go with it and pin it into their own body. A strong person pulling against you is giving you energy you can use.

**Problem:** "I can never remember which trap to use."

**Solution:** That is because you are thinking about techniques rather than feeling contacts. Go back to kakie and Drill 5.1. With enough practice, the trap that each contact offers becomes obvious, and you stop needing to choose. In the meantime, limit yourself to the palm pin and the hooking pull. Between them, they cover most situations.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Trapping is the art of making the other person's arms busy while yours are free. Done well, it creates moments of opportunity that would never exist otherwise. Done badly, it becomes a cooperative dance that falls apart the instant someone resists.

The key is simplicity. Learn a few traps well. Make them from contact, not by reaching. Control the elbow rather than the wrist. Pin against something. Use the other person's own movement. And always follow the trap immediately with something else, usually a strike.

The next chapter takes that "something else" and makes it the focus: how to link traps to strikes, how to string several actions together into fluent combinations, and how to keep the other person's arms tied up long enough to finish the exchange.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Trapping is a principle, not a set of fixed sequences. Keep traps short, simple and opportunistic.
- Control the elbow, not the wrist. Pin the arm against the body or against the other arm.
- Every trap should open a line for something else, usually a strike that follows immediately.
- The main traps are the palm pin, the hooking pull, the forearm jam, the cross trap, the elbow lift and the pass.
- Kake uke and mawashi uke in the kata are full of trapping applications.
- Trap from the outside line wherever possible, so that their trapped arm blocks their free hand.
- Use the other person's reactions and movements to set up your traps. Never reach for an arm.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. In kakie, try to apply a palm pin from each of the four positions of the cycle. Which position makes the pin easiest? Why?
2. Watch a video of a boxing or MMA bout and look for moments when one fighter briefly traps or controls the other's arm. How long does each trap last? What follows it?
3. Take one kata you know well and find three movements that could be traps. Test each with a partner using the four bunkai tests from Chapter 1.
:::

# Kuzushi: The Art of Breaking Balance

::: chapter-intro
No throw works on a person who is balanced, rooted and ready. Every successful throw begins with a moment in which the other person's balance has been broken, however slightly, so that they cannot resist. The Japanese call this kuzushi. This chapter explains where balance comes from, how to take it away, and how the pushes, pulls and turns hidden in the Goju-Ryu kata do exactly that.
:::

When I first started trying to throw people, I made the mistake almost everyone makes. I treated the throw as a feat of strength. I grabbed hold, turned, heaved and hoped. Against smaller or less experienced partners it sometimes worked, in an ugly sort of way. Against anyone bigger or more experienced, it failed completely. They simply stood there, solid as a tree, while I strained against them.

The person who finally showed me what I was doing wrong was a judo coach in his sixties who weighed perhaps sixty-five kilograms. He let me grab hold of him and try my best throw. Nothing happened. Then he took hold of me, and without any apparent effort, I was on my back looking at the ceiling. "You tried to throw me while I was standing," he said. "I threw you while you were falling. You just didn't know you were falling yet."

That is kuzushi. The throw itself is almost an afterthought. The real work is done in the moment before, when the other person's balance is taken away.

## The Problem: People Resist

The fundamental problem with throwing someone is that they do not want to be thrown. If you try to throw a balanced person, they will resist with their whole body, and unless you are much stronger than them, you will fail.

Worse, a failed throw at close range is dangerous. You are tangled up with the other person, often with your back partly turned to them, and your own balance is compromised by the effort. A good opponent will use your failed throw to counter-throw you.

The answer is not to try harder. It is to throw at the moment when they *cannot* resist, because their balance has been broken and their body is busy trying to recover it.

## The Principle: The Base and the Centre

To understand how balance is broken, you need two simple ideas: the base and the centre of gravity.

### The base

Your base is the area on the floor enclosed by your feet. If you stand with your feet together, your base is small. If you stand with your feet apart, it is larger. The base is not just the area under your feet; it is the whole area between them.

### The centre of gravity

Your centre of gravity is the point at which your body's weight is concentrated. For most people standing upright, it lies roughly behind the navel, in the lower abdomen. When you move, bend or lean, your centre of gravity moves too.

### Balance and its loss

You are balanced when your centre of gravity is directly above your base. The further it is from the edges of your base, the more stable you are. When your centre of gravity moves outside your base, you begin to fall, and you must either step to create a new base under it or go down.

Breaking someone's balance means moving their centre of gravity towards the edge of their base, or moving their base out from under their centre of gravity. Either way, they are forced to react, and while they are reacting they cannot resist your throw.

## The Eight Directions

Judo teaches that a person's balance can be broken in eight directions: forward, backward, left, right and the four diagonals between them. This is a useful framework for karateka too.

The important insight is that a person in a particular stance is strong in some directions and weak in others. This is the triangle idea from Chapter 3. A person standing with one foot forward is strong along the line of their feet, forwards and backwards along that line. They are weak across that line, towards the third corner of the triangle.

In practice, for a person in a typical fighting stance with the left foot forward, the weakest directions are usually *diagonally forward to their right*, past their lead foot, and *diagonally backward to their left*, behind their lead foot. These are the directions in which there is no foot to catch them.

## How to Break Balance

There are four basic ways to break someone's balance. Most real throws use a combination of them.

### Pulling

Pulling the other person towards you moves their centre of gravity forward. If you pull them in a direction where there is no foot to catch them, they will lose balance forward.

Pulls are most effective when they are sudden and when they follow a push. If someone is pushing towards you, a pull in the same direction adds to their own momentum and draws them off balance very easily.

The key to pulling is to use your body, not your arms. Step back or turn as you pull, so that your weight goes into it. A pull with the arms alone is weak and easily resisted.

### Pushing

Pushing the other person away from you moves their centre of gravity backward. Like pulling, pushing is most effective when it is sudden, when it follows a pull, and when it is directed towards a weak direction.

A push directly into someone's chest along the line of their feet will usually be resisted. A push diagonally across their stance, towards the point behind their lead foot, is much harder to resist.

### Turning

Turning the other person, by twisting their shoulders or head, moves their centre of gravity sideways and disrupts their base, because their feet do not turn as quickly as their upper body. A turn of the head is particularly effective, because where the head goes the body follows.

### Lifting and lowering

Lifting the other person, even slightly, takes weight off their feet and makes them much easier to move. Lowering them, by pulling down on their head or arms, bends them forward and puts their centre of gravity in front of their feet.

Both lifting and lowering work well with pulling and pushing. A pull that also lifts is much more effective than a pull alone. A push that also lowers can collapse someone's posture completely.

## Action and Reaction Again

In Chapter 6 we saw how every action causes a reaction, and how combinations exploit those reactions. The same principle is at the heart of kuzushi.

When you push someone, they push back. When you pull them, they pull back. When you push their head down, they lift it. These reactions are almost automatic, and they are the key to breaking balance against a resisting opponent.

The classic method is the *push–pull*: push the other person, wait for them to push back, and then pull suddenly in the same direction as their push. Their own force, combined with your pull, takes them off balance far more easily than any pull on its own could.

The reverse, *pull–push*, works just as well. Pull them towards you, feel them resist by leaning back, and then push suddenly in the direction they are already leaning.

::: drill
**Drill 10.1: Push–pull**

Stand facing a partner, each with hands on the other's shoulders or upper arms. Push your partner gently backwards. Ask them to resist by pushing back. The moment you feel them push back, step back and turn slightly, pulling them forward in the direction they are pushing. They should stumble forward.

Then reverse: pull them gently, feel them lean back, and push suddenly as they lean. They should stumble backward.

Do this slowly at first, with your partner cooperating. Over several sessions, ask your partner to resist more naturally, and try to feel the exact moment their reaction begins. That moment is the moment to act.
:::

## Kuzushi Through the Arms and Head

At close range, you usually break balance through the points of contact you have with the other person: their arms, their head, their shoulders or their clothing.

### Through the arms

Pulling on the other person's arm, particularly at the wrist or forearm, draws their shoulder forward and turns their body. If you pull down as well as towards you, their posture bends forward. This is the hooking pull from Chapter 5, used now for balance rather than for opening a target.

Pushing on the other person's elbow, particularly upwards and across their body, turns them away from you and lifts their weight onto one foot. This is the elbow lift from Chapter 5.

### Through the head

The head is the most powerful handle on the body. Turning someone's head turns their whole body. Pulling it down bends them forward. Pushing it back bends them backward.

A palm on the chin, pushing up and back, is one of the most effective balance-breaks in close combat. It tips the head back, which arches the spine and moves the centre of gravity behind the heels. Combined with a leg sweep or a step behind the other person, it becomes a powerful throw.

A hand on the back of the head, pulling down and towards you, breaks the posture forward. Combined with a knee to the body or face, or with a step and turn, it is equally powerful.

### Through the shoulders

Pushing one shoulder while pulling the other turns the other person, as if you were turning a steering wheel. This is very effective for moving them in a circle, which disrupts their footwork and often leaves their feet crossed or in a line.

## Kuzushi Through the Legs

Balance can also be broken by attacking the base directly: moving, blocking or striking the other person's legs.

*Blocking a foot* stops the other person from stepping to recover their balance. If you have pulled them forward and they try to step forward to catch themselves, a foot placed in front of theirs means they cannot step, and they fall.

*Sweeping a foot* moves their base out from under them. A sweep is most effective when the foot being swept is carrying little weight, for example as they step, or when their weight has been shifted to the other foot by a pull or push.

*Striking a leg*, for example with a knee to the thigh or a low kick, causes the leg to buckle or withdraw, which takes away part of their base.

*Stepping behind* the other person, with your leg behind theirs, means that if they move backward they will trip over your leg. This is the basis of many of the most effective throws.

## Timing: The Moment of Weakness

The best moment to break someone's balance is when they are already moving. A person standing still with their weight evenly distributed is at their most stable. A person in the middle of a step, a strike or a reaction is much less stable.

There are several moments of weakness worth looking for.

*Mid-step.* When the other person steps, their weight is briefly on one foot. A push or pull at that moment, towards the foot that is moving, is very hard to resist.

*Mid-strike.* When they throw a punch or kick, their weight is committed in the direction of the strike. A pull in that direction carries them past their own balance point.

*During a reaction.* When they react to one of your actions, by flinching, pushing back or pulling away, their balance is committed to that reaction.

*During a recovery.* When they have been slightly unbalanced and are trying to recover, they are vulnerable to being unbalanced again in a different direction.

Learning to feel these moments is a matter of sensitivity, and once again kakie is the foundation. In kakie you are constantly feeling your partner's pressure and timing. The same sense, applied to the whole body, tells you when their balance is vulnerable.

::: kata
**Kata link: the turns of Seipai and Shisochin**

Many Goju-Ryu kata contain turns of ninety or one hundred and eighty degrees, often combined with grabbing or pulling hand movements. These turns are often taught as turning to face a new attacker. Read as close-range applications, they are frequently balance-breaks: you have hold of the other person, and as you turn, your body weight pulls them in a circle, breaking their balance and swinging them off their feet. Shisochin's opening movements and the turning sequences of Seipai are good places to explore this.
:::

## Kuzushi and Striking

Balance-breaking is not only for throws. It is equally useful for strikes.

A person whose balance is broken cannot strike effectively, because they need their legs for recovery rather than for generating power. They also cannot defend well, because their arms are often reaching out to catch themselves. A strike landed on someone who is off balance is much more effective than one landed on someone who is balanced, because they cannot absorb it with their structure.

This is why so many of the combinations in this book involve pulling or pushing before striking. The pull breaks the balance; the strike lands on someone who cannot defend or absorb it.

::: drill
**Drill 10.2: Pull and strike**

Your partner holds a focus mitt near their face with one hand and offers you their other arm. Hook their arm and pull suddenly, stepping back and turning as you do. As they stumble forward, strike the mitt with your free hand.

Then the same with a push: push their shoulder suddenly, and as they stumble backward, follow and strike the mitt.

Do ten each way, on both sides. Notice how much harder you can strike when your partner is off balance, and how little they can do to stop it.
:::

## Protecting Your Own Balance

If you can break the other person's balance, they can break yours. Here are the principles for protecting it.

*Keep your base wide enough.* A stance that is too narrow, or with the feet in a line, is easy to break. Sanchin dachi, with its slightly staggered feet and moderate width, is a good balance between stability and mobility.

*Keep your centre low.* Bent knees lower your centre of gravity and make you harder to move.

*Keep your posture upright.* A bent spine or a forward head moves your centre of gravity towards the edge of your base.

*Do not over-commit.* Every time you push, pull or strike hard, you commit your balance in that direction. If the other person is skilled, they will use that commitment against you. Keep your actions short and controlled.

*Move your feet.* When your balance is threatened, the simplest answer is to step and create a new base under your centre of gravity. Small, quick steps are better than big ones.

*Do not fight the force; redirect it.* If someone is pulling you strongly, do not pull back. Step towards them, turn, and redirect their pull past you. This is the essence of the soft side of Goju-Ryu.

::: fault
**Fault: resisting with stiffness**

When their balance is threatened, many people go stiff, trying to resist with muscle. This makes them easier to throw, not harder, because a stiff body moves as a single block, and a single block is easy to tip over.

*Correction:* When you feel your balance going, relax and move your feet. A relaxed body can adapt; a stiff one cannot.
:::

::: street
**Street note: uneven ground**

On the street, balance is affected by the ground: wet pavements, ice, gravel, kerbs, steps and debris. A small push near a kerb can send someone sprawling into the road. A pull on ice can bring two people down together. Be very aware of the ground beneath you, and take it into account both for your own balance and for anyone you might need to unbalance. Throwing someone onto concrete can cause catastrophic head injuries, which is one reason we will discuss control and responsibility so carefully in the next chapter.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My partner just stands there and I can't move them."

**Solution:** You are trying to move a balanced person directly. Use the push–pull or pull–push principle: provoke a reaction first, then use it. Also check the direction of your force. If you are pushing or pulling along the line of their feet, you are working against their strongest direction. Go across it.

**Problem:** "When I pull, my partner just pulls back harder and I end up being pulled myself."

**Solution:** You are pulling with your arms and not committing your body. Step back and turn as you pull, so that your weight goes into it. And if they pull back hard, use it: go with their pull, step in, and push them in the direction they are leaning.

**Problem:** "I can feel when my partner is off balance but by the time I act they've recovered."

**Solution:** Your timing is slightly late, which is very normal at this stage. Work on Drill 10.1 slowly, and try to act at the very beginning of your partner's reaction rather than when it is fully developed. Over time, you will start to feel the reaction before it happens.

**Problem:** "I'm always the one who gets thrown."

**Solution:** Look at your own posture and base. Most people who are thrown frequently have their feet too close together, their knees too straight, or their head too far forward. Practise the protection principles above, and spend time in the pushing square from Chapter 2.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Kuzushi is the hidden foundation of every throw and many strikes. Without it, throws become contests of strength, which the stronger person usually wins. With it, a smaller person can throw a larger one with surprising ease.

The principles are simple. Balance depends on the relationship between the centre of gravity and the base. You can break it by pulling, pushing, turning, lifting or lowering, through the arms, the head, the shoulders or the legs. You do it best by using the other person's own reactions and by acting at the moment when they are already moving.

The next chapter uses these principles to build the throws themselves, drawing on the rich supply of throwing techniques hidden in the Goju-Ryu kata.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Every successful throw begins with kuzushi: breaking the other person's balance.
- Balance depends on keeping the centre of gravity above the base. Break it by moving one away from the other.
- A person in a stance is strong along the line of their feet and weak across it.
- Break balance by pulling, pushing, turning, lifting and lowering, using the body rather than the arms.
- Use reactions: push–pull and pull–push are far more effective than a single pull or push.
- The head is the most powerful handle on the body. Where the head goes, the body follows.
- The best moments to break balance are mid-step, mid-strike, during a reaction and during a recovery.
- Protect your own balance with a sound base, a low centre, an upright posture and relaxed, quick feet.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Stand on one leg and ask a partner to push you very gently in eight directions. Which directions are hardest to resist? Now stand in sanchin dachi and repeat. What changed?
2. In kakie, try to feel the moment your partner's balance shifts. Describe what you feel in a few words.
3. Find three turns in the kata you know that could be read as balance-breaks. Test them with a partner.
:::

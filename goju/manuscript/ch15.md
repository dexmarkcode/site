# Releases: Escaping Grabs, Holds and Neck Attacks

::: chapter-intro
Much of the real violence people face does not start with a punch. It starts with a grab: a hand on the wrist, a fist in the collar, an arm around the neck, a pair of arms around the body from behind. This chapter turns the joint-control and close-range principles of the book to defensive use, and shows how to escape the grabs and holds that people most often encounter.
:::

When I teach self-defence courses for people with no martial arts background, the techniques they ask about most are not strikes or throws. They are escapes. "What do I do if someone grabs my wrist?" "What if someone grabs me from behind?" "What if someone puts their hands round my neck?"

These are good questions, because grabs and holds are common, frightening and often the first stage of something worse. A grab is a way of controlling you, holding you still while you are hit, moving you somewhere you do not want to go, or stopping you leaving. Being able to escape quickly and confidently is one of the most practical skills in close combat.

It is also, as it happens, the context for which many of the Goju-Ryu kata seem to have been designed. Saifa begins with an escape from a wrist grab. Several kata contain movements that make most sense as escapes from holds around the body or neck. The old teachers clearly expected their students to be grabbed.

## The Problem: Being Held

Being held is dangerous for three reasons.

First, it *fixes you in place*, so that you can be struck more easily. A person holding your collar with one hand can hit you with the other, and you cannot step back out of range.

Second, it *lets you be moved*. A person holding your arm or clothing can drag you, push you against a wall, pull you off balance or take you somewhere more private.

Third, and often overlooked, it is *frightening*. Being grabbed triggers a powerful freeze response in most people. The mind goes blank, the body stiffens, and valuable seconds are lost.

## The Principle: Weak Point, Leverage, Action

Escapes from grabs rest on three principles.

### The weak point of a grip

Every grip has a weak point, and for a hand grip it is almost always the gap between the thumb and the fingers. Four fingers are much stronger than one thumb. If you pull against the fingers, you are fighting four; if you pull towards the thumb, you are fighting one. So the basic rule for escaping a hand grip is: *move towards the thumb*.

### Leverage, not strength

Do not try to pull straight out of a grip with strength. Use leverage: rotate your arm, use your elbow as a lever, use your whole body. A small rotation of the forearm against the thumb is far more effective than a large pull against the fingers.

### Act, do not just escape

An escape on its own often leaves you exactly where you started: facing someone who has just grabbed you and who will probably try again. Combine the escape with an action: a strike, a step to the outside, a trap or simply leaving. The escape is the beginning, not the end.

### The order of things

In practice, the sequence is often: *distract, escape, act*. A strike or a sudden movement distracts the other person, the escape is made while they are distracted, and then you act, usually by moving away or following up. Against a strong person, the distraction is often what makes the escape possible.

## Wrist Grabs

Wrist grabs are the simplest grabs and the best place to start.

### The same-side wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with their left hand, so that their thumb is on top of your wrist.

*Escape.* Find their thumb. Rotate your forearm so that the narrow edge of your wrist turns towards the gap between their thumb and fingers. Bend your elbow and drive it towards their forearm, while turning your hand up and out in a small circle. Your wrist slides out through the gap.

*Follow-up.* As your hand comes free, it is already close to their face. Strike with a back fist or palm heel, or step to the outside of their arm.

### The cross wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with their right hand, so their thumb is on the inside of your wrist.

*Escape.* Rotate your forearm towards their thumb and bring your elbow up and across towards them. Your wrist turns out through the gap. This is the movement at the start of Saifa: the free hand covers the trapped hand, and the body steps and pulls.

*Follow-up.* The step that releases the grip also takes you to their side. From there, a hammer fist or back fist, as in the kata, follows naturally.

### The two-handed wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with both hands.

*Escape.* Reach between their arms with your left hand and grasp your own right fist. Pull your right arm up sharply between their thumbs, using both your arms and the drive of your legs. Their two thumbs are the weak point; your two arms working together are stronger than their two thumbs.

*Follow-up.* Your arms finish high, close to their face, ready for a downward elbow or hammer fist.

::: kata
**Kata link: Saifa**

The opening of Saifa is the classic wrist-grab escape. The free hand covers the gripped hand, the body steps out at an angle, and both hands are pulled sharply in a tearing movement. The grip is broken against the thumb, your body is moved off the line, and the next movements, a back fist and a hammer fist, are the follow-up. It is a complete escape, distraction and counter in a few seconds.
:::

## Clothing Grabs

Clothing grabs, at the collar, lapel, sleeve or shoulder, are very common in real violence, and they are often a prelude to a punch.

### The single lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing at chest height with their right hand and draws back their left to punch.

*Immediate action.* The punch is the danger, not the grab. Raise your left forearm to cover your head on the punching side, and step to the outside of their grabbing arm, as described in Chapter 3. Their grabbing arm is now between you and their punch.

*Escape and control.* Pin their grabbing hand against your chest with your right hand, and apply the inward wrist bend from Chapter 14 by bringing your forearm over their wrist. Or strike over the grabbing arm with your left elbow into their face.

### The double lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing with both hands, often pushing you back against a wall.

*Escape.* Both their hands are committed, which leaves their head and body open. Strike first, with a knee to the thigh or groin, or a palm heel to the chin between their arms. Then bring both your arms up between theirs and drive them outward, or turn and pass one of their arms to escape to the side.

### The rear collar grab

The other person grabs your collar or hood from behind.

*Escape.* Turn quickly towards the grabbing hand, so that you face them and their grip twists. As you turn, raise your arm on that side to cover your head, and strike with an elbow as you come round.

## Holds from the Front

### The front bear hug over the arms

The other person wraps their arms around your body from the front, pinning your arms to your sides.

*Escape.* You still have your head, your hips and your legs. Drop your weight suddenly by bending your knees, which loosens the hold. Drive your hips back to create space. Strike with a knee to the groin or thigh, or a headbutt if justified. As the hold loosens, bring your hands up to their hips or chest and push away, stepping back at an angle.

### The front bear hug under the arms

The other person wraps their arms around your body under your arms, leaving your arms free.

*Escape.* Your arms are free, so use them. Push their head back with a palm under the chin, or strike their face with elbows and palm heels. As their head goes back, their posture breaks, and you can push them away or use the outer reap from Chapter 11.

## Holds from Behind

Attacks from behind are among the most frightening, because you cannot see them coming and your weapons all face the wrong way.

### The rear bear hug over the arms

The other person wraps their arms around you from behind, pinning your arms.

*Escape.* Drop your weight immediately, bending your knees and widening your stance. This makes you harder to lift and loosens the hold. Strike backwards: stamp on their foot with your heel, drive a backward elbow into their ribs if your arm is free enough, or strike with the back of your head if their face is close. As the hold loosens, step to one side and turn, bringing your elbow round into their head or body.

### The rear bear hug under the arms

The other person holds you from behind under your arms.

*Escape.* Your arms are free. Strike backward with elbows into their head and ribs. Attack their hands: peel back a finger or strike the back of the hand with your knuckles. Drop your weight and stamp on their foot. As the hold loosens, turn into them and strike, or step away.

## Neck Attacks

Attacks on the neck are the most dangerous holds of all, because they can cause unconsciousness within seconds and death within minutes. Every martial artist should know how to escape them, and should treat any attack on the neck as an emergency requiring immediate and decisive action.

I want to stress something here. In this section, I describe *escapes* from neck attacks. I do not teach how to apply neck restraints, chokes or strangles, because they are among the most dangerous techniques that exist, and they need to be learned, if at all, under close supervision from a qualified instructor.

### The front strangle with two hands

The other person grips your throat with both hands from the front.

*Immediate action.* Tuck your chin hard to protect your windpipe. Your priority is to break the grip before you lose consciousness.

*Escape.* Bring one arm up between their arms and over the top, turning your body sharply as you do, so that your arm and shoulder break the grip of one of their hands. Step to the side as you turn. Alternatively, strike immediately and hard to the groin, eyes or throat to make them release. Then step away and escape.

Many self-defence instructors also teach plucking at the thumbs to loosen the grip, but under real pressure, a turn of the whole body breaking the grip is usually more reliable.

### The rear forearm around the neck

The other person wraps their forearm around your neck from behind. This is one of the most dangerous attacks there is, because it can render you unconscious very quickly.

*Immediate action.* Tuck your chin into the crook of their elbow, turning your head towards their elbow if you can, to protect your windpipe and arteries. Grab their forearm with both hands and pull down hard to create space at your neck.

*Escape.* Drop your weight and turn your body towards their elbow, so that your head slides out through the gap between their arm and body. As you turn, strike their groin or ribs with your elbow, or stamp on their foot. Once your head is free, turn to face them and get away.

This escape must be practised slowly and carefully, with a partner who releases the hold the instant you tap. Never apply pressure to a partner's neck in training.

![**Figure 15.3** Escaping a rear neck hold. (1) Tuck your chin into the crook of their elbow and pull down on their forearm with both hands. (2) Drop your weight and turn towards their elbow. (3) Your head slides out through the gap as your elbow strikes back.](figures/fig15_3.png)

::: street
**Street note: neck attacks are an emergency**

An attack on the neck can kill. If someone is strangling you or holding you round the neck, you are entitled to use a high level of force to make them stop, because you are in genuine danger of serious harm or death. Do not hesitate. Strike hard to vulnerable targets, break the hold, and escape. Equally, never apply a neck restraint to someone unless you are in genuine fear for your life, because the risk of killing them is real.
:::

## Training Escapes Safely and Realistically

Escapes are only useful if they work under pressure, and pressure is hard to create safely. Here is a progression I use.

*Stage one: cooperative.* Your partner applies the grab or hold with moderate pressure. You perform the escape slowly, learning the mechanics.

*Stage two: committed.* Your partner applies the grab or hold firmly, as they would in real life, but does not resist the escape actively. You must make the escape work against a real grip.

*Stage three: resisting.* Your partner applies the grab firmly and tries to maintain it, adjusting as you try to escape. You must use distraction, timing and follow-up to get free. Strikes are light and controlled.

*Stage four: scenario.* Your partner approaches with a realistic attack, such as a shove and a grab, a grab and a punch, or a grab from behind as you walk. You must respond without knowing exactly what is coming.

Never progress to resisting or scenario training with neck holds. Neck escapes are always trained at stage one or two, with immediate release on the tap.

::: drill
**Drill 15.1: Grab circuit**

Your partner works through a sequence of grabs: same-side wrist, cross wrist, two-handed wrist, single lapel, double lapel, front bear hug, rear bear hug. For each, you escape and follow up with one light strike to a pad your partner holds, then step away at an angle. Then swap roles.

Do the circuit three times. The first time is cooperative, the second committed, the third with light resistance. Keep the neck holds out of the resisting round.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 15.2: The surprise grab**

You walk slowly around the mat. At a random moment, your partner, approaching from any direction, grabs you in one of the ways described in this chapter. You must escape and move away within three seconds.

This drill trains the most important skill of all: overcoming the freeze. The first few times, you will probably hesitate. After a few sessions, your response becomes faster and more automatic.
:::

::: fault
**Fault: pulling against the fingers**

The instinctive response to a wrist grab is to pull straight back, against the fingers. Against a strong person, this simply does not work, and it wastes energy and time.

*Correction:* Always find the thumb. Turn the narrow edge of your wrist towards it and use your elbow as a lever. If you find yourself pulling straight back, stop, find the thumb, and rotate.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My partner is much stronger than me and I can't break their grip."

**Solution:** Strength is not the answer. Use the weak point (the thumb), use leverage (rotate and use the elbow), and above all use distraction. A sharp strike to the face or a stamp on the foot makes almost anyone loosen their grip for a moment. Escape in that moment.

**Problem:** "When I'm grabbed from behind I panic."

**Solution:** That is entirely normal, and the answer is repetition. Practise Drill 15.2 regularly, starting with gentle grabs and gradually making them firmer and more sudden. The freeze response reduces with familiarity. Also practise dropping your weight as your very first response to any rear grab; having one simple, automatic first move makes the rest easier.

**Problem:** "I escape the grab but then my partner just grabs me again."

**Solution:** You are escaping without acting. Every escape should be followed immediately by a strike, a step to the outside, or leaving. Practise escapes with a follow-up every time, never on their own.

**Problem:** "I'm worried about practising neck escapes."

**Solution:** Your caution is wise. Practise neck escapes only at the cooperative and committed stages, never with resistance. Your partner should apply very light pressure and release instantly when you tap or say stop. Most of the learning is in the movements, not in the pressure.
:::

## Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Five. We have studied the principles of joint control, the specific controls of the wrist, elbow and shoulder, and now the escapes from grabs, holds and neck attacks.

The thread running through all three chapters is leverage: using the weak points of a joint or a grip, the long lever of a limb, and the strength of the whole body rather than the arms alone. Joint controls and escapes are two sides of the same coin. Once you understand how to control a joint, you understand how to escape from someone controlling yours.

In Part Six, the final part of the book, we bring everything together: timing and counter-attacking, sparring at close range, and the realities of real-world encounters.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Grabs and holds are among the most common forms of real violence, and often a prelude to something worse.
- The weak point of a hand grip is the gap between the thumb and the fingers. Move towards the thumb.
- Use leverage and rotation, not strength. Use the elbow as a lever.
- Distract, escape, act. An escape without a follow-up leaves you where you started.
- Against a lapel grab and punch, deal with the punch first by moving to the outside.
- Against rear holds, drop your weight first, then strike and turn.
- Neck attacks are emergencies. Tuck the chin, break the hold decisively and escape.
- Train escapes progressively, but never with resistance on neck holds.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Ask three partners of different sizes to grab your wrist firmly. Which grip is hardest to escape? What makes it hard?
2. Perform the opening of Saifa slowly against a real wrist grab. Does the kata movement work as an escape? What adjustments do you need?
3. After a session of Drill 15.2, write down how long you think you hesitated on each grab. How does this change over a month of practice?
:::

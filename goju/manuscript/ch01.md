# The Close-Range Heart of Goju-Ryu

::: chapter-intro
Most people who walk into a Goju-Ryu dojo for the first time expect to learn to kick and punch from a respectable distance. Within a few weeks they discover something odd. The kata keep pulling them in. The hands keep grabbing. The feet keep stepping into the other person's space rather than away from it. This chapter explains why, and sets out how the rest of the book will help you make sense of it.
:::

Every martial art has a home range, a distance at which its techniques feel most natural and its training makes most sense. For some it is the long range of the sport fighter, bouncing in and out, scoring with a fast reverse punch and retreating before the other person can reply. For others it is the floor, where wrestlers and judoka feel at home. Goju-Ryu's home range is the space in between: the distance at which your forearm touches the other person's forearm, your forehead could touch theirs, and your knee is a few inches from their thigh.

I will be honest with you. For years I did not understand this. I trained in a club that treated Goju-Ryu like any other sport karate style. We performed the kata for grading and competitions, and then we put on our mitts and sparred at a distance that the kata never seemed to address. When I asked my first instructor what the opening movements of Seiyunchin were for, he told me they were blocks. When I asked why we stood so low and so square to block a punch, he told me it was for leg strength. Both answers were partly true and both missed the point.

It was only when I began training with people who had studied on Okinawa, and later when I started to pressure-test the movements with judo players, boxers and doormen, that the picture came into focus. The kata were not describing a duel at a polite distance. They were describing what to do when someone has already grabbed you, pushed you against a wall, or swung a heavy right hand at your head from close enough to touch. In other words, they were describing the situations that real violence actually produces.

This book is about that range. It is written for the karateka who wants to understand what the kata are really teaching, for the sparring competitor who keeps getting smothered when the exchange closes up, and for the ordinary person who wants to be able to look after themselves if the worst ever happens. We will cover trapping, elbows, knees, throws, joint controls and counter-attacks, and we will do it the way I teach in my own classes: by starting with problems rather than techniques.

## Where Goju-Ryu Came From

You do not need to be a historian to train well, but a little history explains a great deal about why Goju-Ryu looks the way it does.

The style grew out of the work of Kanryo Higaonna, a nineteenth-century Okinawan from Naha who travelled to Fuzhou in southern China and spent many years studying Chinese boxing there. Accounts differ about exactly whom he studied with and for how long, and you will find people arguing about the details with great passion. What matters for us is the character of the material he brought home. Southern Chinese boxing systems from that region, often associated with crane and other animal styles, tended to favour a stable, rooted posture, short powerful hand techniques, a great deal of grabbing and pulling, and a training culture built around conditioning the body to absorb and deliver force at close quarters.

Higaonna taught in Naha, and his most dedicated student was Chojun Miyagi. Miyagi trained under him for many years, made his own journeys to China, and gradually organised what he had learned into a coherent system. Around 1930, when one of his students was asked at a demonstration in mainland Japan what the art was called, there was no good answer. Miyagi chose the name Goju-Ryu, the "hard-soft school", from a line in the old Chinese manual known as the Bubishi, which speaks of the way of breathing being both hard and soft.

Miyagi also did something that shaped the art for the next century. He added two kata of his own, Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni, designed as introductory forms, and the breathing and tension kata Tensho, which sits beside the much older Sanchin as one of the art's two great training forms. He placed a heavy emphasis on supplementary training with traditional tools such as the chi'ishi (a stone weight on a stick) and the nigiri game (gripping jars), and on partner conditioning of the arms and body.

When you put these threads together, a clear picture appears. This was an art designed by and for people who expected to be grabbed, to grab back, and to need strong hands, strong grips and strong, stable bodies to survive. It was civilian self-protection with a Chinese accent and an Okinawan practicality.

## The Kata as a Close-Combat Manual

Goju-Ryu is usually taught with twelve core kata, plus Sanchin and Tensho. Different organisations order them slightly differently, but a typical list runs: Gekisai Dai Ichi, Gekisai Dai Ni, Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseru, Seipai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei, with Sanchin and Tensho underpinning the whole syllabus.

If you look at the kata with fresh eyes, several things stand out.

First, there are very few kicks, and almost all of them are low. You will find front kicks to the groin and knee height, stamping kicks, and knee strikes, but nothing that would win a points competition by tapping someone on the head.

Second, there is a great deal of grabbing. Hands open, circle, hook and pull back to the hip. The pulling hand, the hikite, is not decoration. It is the hand that has hold of something.

Third, the stances are short and strong. Sanchin dachi, with its inward-gripping knees and narrow base, makes little sense as a stance for long-range fighting. It makes a great deal of sense as a stance for staying upright while someone is pushing, pulling and twisting you.

Fourth, there are turns, drops and changes of level that look strange as blocks but look very sensible as ways of breaking someone's balance or getting out from under a grip.

Fifth, many of the strikes are short: elbows, hammer fists, palm heels, back fists and short uppercut-style punches. These are tools for the distance where a long reverse punch has no room to work.

Put simply, the kata describe a fight that has already closed. The person who wrote them assumed that the attacker would be close, would be grabbing or striking heavily, and would not politely stay at the end of your fist.

::: kata
**Kata link: Saifa**

The opening of Saifa is a good example. In most dojo it is taught as a release from a wrist grab, with the free hand covering the trapped one, a step to the side and a pulling, tearing movement. It is followed by a back fist and a hammer fist. Even the most conservative interpretation accepts that the kata begins with someone having hold of you. From the very first move, the form tells you what range it is concerned with.
:::

### A word about bunkai

The analysis of kata is called bunkai, and its practical application oyo. You will find almost as many approaches to bunkai as there are instructors, and some of them are frankly fanciful. Over the years I have developed a simple set of tests that I apply to any interpretation before I teach it.

The first is the *pressure test*. Does the application still work when the training partner is resisting at a sensible level, or does it rely on them standing still with their arm out like a mannequin?

The second is the *context test*. Does the application solve a problem that people really face? Defences against a deep, lunging, single punch that stays out at full extension may be useful for teaching distance, but they are not what happens when a drunk man grabs your collar and swings.

The third is the *consistency test*. Does the interpretation use the movement as it actually appears in the kata, or has it needed so much alteration that the kata is no longer recognisable?

The fourth is the *follow-through test*. If the application works, what happens next? A technique that leaves you tangled up, off balance or with your back to the other person is not finished.

Throughout this book, every application I give you has passed those four tests in my own training and teaching. That does not mean they are the only correct readings of the kata. It means they work.

## What We Mean by Close Combat

It helps to be precise about distance, because a lot of confusion in martial arts comes from people using the same words to mean different things.

I divide the fighting space into four broad ranges, measured by what the two people can do to each other without moving their feet.

The *kicking range* is the distance at which only a kick or a long lunging punch can reach. Most people spend very little time here in a real confrontation. It is a transitional distance.

The *punching range* is where a straight punch can land with the rear foot planted. Boxers live here, and so do most sport karate competitors.

The *close range*, sometimes called trapping range, begins at the moment the arms touch. Forearm meets forearm, a hand lands on a shoulder, or a grab is made. At this range elbows, knees, short punches, head control and trapping all become available. This is the heart of this book.

The *clinch and grappling range* is where the bodies are joined: chest to chest, head to head, arms wrapped. Throws, sweeps, joint controls and knees dominate here, and the fight can go to the floor at any moment.

![**Figure 1.2** The four ranges seen from the side. Close combat in this book means the last two: from the moment the forearms touch to the moment the bodies lock together.](figures/fig1_2.png)

The ranges are not fixed zones. A fight flows between them very quickly, and much of the skill of close combat lies in managing that flow: closing when it suits you, opening when it does not, and never being surprised by the change. A person who is comfortable only at one range is in trouble the moment the fight moves away from it. A boxer who has never been clinched will tire himself out in thirty seconds of tying up. A grappler who has never been struck hard will drop his head at exactly the wrong moment. Goju-Ryu, at its best, prepares you to be at ease in the space where the other two meet.

## Hard and Soft at Close Range

The name of the style is not just poetry. *Go* means hard, *ju* means soft, and the idea of combining the two runs through every technique in this book.

At close range the hard and soft qualities take very specific forms.

Hardness means *structure*. It is the ability to take a push without folding, to deliver a short strike without your own wrist or elbow collapsing, to hold a grip without your arm being peeled away. It comes from correct alignment, sound breathing and conditioning. Sanchin, which we study in the next chapter, is above all a school of hardness.

Softness means *sensitivity and adaptability*. It is the ability to feel a change in pressure through your forearm and respond before you consciously see it, to yield to a strong push and redirect it rather than meeting it head on, to slip a grip, turn around a heavy arm and arrive in a better position. Tensho and the partner drill kakie are the great schools of softness.

Neither quality on its own is enough. Pure hardness becomes stiff, slow and predictable. A stiff person is easy to throw, because the moment they push back you can use their own effort against them. Pure softness becomes floppy and weak. A person who only yields eventually runs out of space and gets crushed against a wall.

What we want is something the old teachers sometimes described with the Okinawan word *muchimi*: a heavy, sticky, rubbery quality in the arms, soft on the surface and very hard underneath. Imagine trying to push away an arm made of wet rope wrapped around a steel bar. It gives, it clings, and then suddenly there is nothing to push against and you find yourself being pulled. That is the feeling we are chasing.

::: drill
**Drill 1.1: Feeling hard and soft**

Stand facing a partner, both in a natural stance, and place your right forearm against their right forearm at chest height, wrists touching. Ask them to push steadily towards your chest.

First, resist with pure tension. Lock your shoulder and arm and push back. Notice how quickly you become tired, how your partner can feel exactly where your effort is going, and how easily they could pull you forward simply by relaxing.

Next, go completely soft and let your arm fold. Notice how quickly your partner's hand reaches your chest.

Finally, keep your elbow down and your forearm in place, but let the push travel through your structure into your legs. Breathe out slowly and turn your waist a few degrees so the pressure slides off to the side. You have just felt, in a very small way, the difference between go, ju and goju.
:::

## Why the Close Range Matters

There are three good reasons to study close combat seriously, and they apply whether your interest is sport, self-protection or simply better karate.

### The fight almost always closes

Watch any footage of real violence and the pattern is depressingly consistent. Very few incidents begin at kicking range and stay there. Most begin with someone already close: talking, shouting, pointing, pushing. The first blow is often thrown from a distance at which a textbook reverse punch has no room. Then, within a second or two, the people involved are grabbing each other, falling against cars, walls and furniture, and usually ending up on the ground.

Sport sparring also closes, more often than people like to admit. Many karate rule sets stop the action the moment the fighters clinch, which hides the problem rather than solving it. If you watch competitors who also train in other combat sports, you see how quickly someone who is comfortable in close is able to control the exchange.

### Close range is where size and strength matter most, unless you have skill

At long range, a smaller, faster person can stay out of trouble. At close range, size, weight and strength become enormous advantages for whoever has them. A heavier person leaning on you will wear you out. A stronger person who gets a grip on your collar can throw you around.

The irony is that this is exactly why the smaller person must learn the close range. You cannot always choose your distance, and if you cannot function when someone bigger gets hold of you, everything else you know is fragile. Skill at close range is the great equaliser, because it is mostly built on leverage, timing, structure and sensitivity rather than raw power.

### The close range teaches you to feel

Training at close range develops a quality that long-range sparring rarely gives you: the ability to read another person through touch. Your eyes can be fooled by feints, poor light, adrenaline and fatigue. Your forearms, once trained, are much harder to fool. A good close-range fighter often knows what the other person is going to do before they do it, because they have felt the shift of weight, the tightening of a shoulder or the drop of an elbow.

This sensitivity, once developed, changes the way you do everything else. Your blocks become lighter and more accurate. Your counters become better timed. Your kata stop being a series of shapes and start being a series of conversations.

## Sparring and the Street: Two Different Problems

Throughout this book I will keep two very different contexts in view, and I want to be clear about the difference from the start.

*Sparring* is a cooperative game with competitive elements. You and your partner agree to rules, levels of contact and targets, and you agree to look after each other. The purpose is to learn. In sparring, a technique that would injure your partner is not a good technique, however effective it might be. You will find, throughout the book, versions of the close-range methods adapted for safe sparring, with controlled contact, safer targets and clear tapping rules.

*Real-world encounters* are not games. There are no rules, no referee and no agreement. The other person may be bigger, stronger, drunk, armed or accompanied by friends. They may not stop when you expect them to. The environment may be cluttered, slippery, dark or crowded. Your aim is not to win but to get home safely, and very often the best technique is the one that never needs to be used because you saw the trouble coming and left.

The techniques overlap a great deal, but the priorities are different. In sparring you might work a joint lock for thirty seconds against a resisting partner, because that is how you learn it. On the street, a joint lock that takes thirty seconds is a liability, and you should be thinking about strikes, escapes and leaving. I will flag these differences as we go.

::: street
**Street note**

The law in the United Kingdom allows you to use reasonable force to protect yourself or others. What counts as reasonable depends on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be at the time. We cover this properly in Chapter 18, but it is worth saying at the outset: the techniques in this book can cause serious injury. Knowing how to use them carries a responsibility to use them only when you genuinely must, and only as much as you need to.
:::

## The Problem-Solving Approach

Most martial arts books are organised by technique. Here is an elbow strike, here is another elbow strike, here is a throw. That approach has its place, but it leaves the reader with a long list of answers and no clear idea of which question each one answers.

This book is organised around problems. Real fights, and good sparring, are a series of problems that one person sets and the other person has to solve. Someone grabs your lead wrist. Someone pushes you towards a wall. Someone throws a looping punch from close range. Someone is bigger than you and is leaning their weight on you in the clinch. Each problem has a small number of good solutions and a large number of bad ones.

For every major area of close combat, I will take you through the same five-stage process.

1. **The problem.** What exactly is happening? What is the other person doing, and why is it dangerous?
2. **The principle.** What general idea solves this kind of problem? Usually it is something to do with structure, angle, timing or leverage.
3. **The technique.** What specific movement applies that principle here? This is where the diagrams come in.
4. **The drill.** How do you practise the technique so that it becomes reliable, starting cooperatively and gradually adding resistance?
5. **Pressure.** How do you test it against a partner who is genuinely trying to stop you, within safe limits?

![**Figure 1.3** The problem-solving loop used throughout this book. Pressure testing feeds back into the problem: if a technique fails under pressure, you go back and ask what problem it was really solving.](figures/fig1_3.png)

The last stage is the one that most traditional training leaves out, and it is the one that matters most. A technique you have never tried against resistance is a theory, not a skill. I will give you safe ways to add pressure to everything in this book, and I urge you to use them.

Each chapter ends with a *Problem-Solving Clinic*: a set of common difficulties students run into, with the solutions I have found work best. If something in your training is not working, look there first.

## How to Read the Diagrams

This book contains a large number of diagrams, and they all follow the same conventions, so it is worth taking a moment to learn them.

The person drawn in *solid black* is you, the person applying the technique. The person drawn in *white with a black outline* is your training partner or the opponent. Limbs on the far side of each body, the ones that would be partly hidden from the viewer, are drawn in grey. This helps you tell the near arm from the far arm when the bodies overlap.

Solid arrows show the main movement. Dashed arrows show a preparatory or secondary movement, or a path the other person's limb follows. A small starburst marks the point of impact for a strike. Numbered black circles show the order of events where several things happen in one picture.

Where the action needs a bird's-eye view, usually for footwork, I use footprint diagrams seen from above. Your feet are solid black, the other person's are white, and arrows show steps and turns.

![**Figure 1.4** The drawing conventions used in this book: you in solid black, the opponent in white, far-side limbs in grey, solid arrows for the main action, dashed arrows for preparation, and a starburst for impact.](figures/fig1_4.png)

The diagrams are deliberately simple. They are not trying to be beautiful illustrations of the human body. They are trying to show, as clearly as possible, where the limbs go, which direction the force travels and in what order things happen. Read the text and the diagram together, then go and try it slowly with a partner.

## What You Need to Train

You need surprisingly little. A training partner is essential for nearly everything in this book, and ideally more than one, of different sizes and temperaments. A mat or a soft floor is necessary for throws and falls. A pair of focus mitts, a kick shield and a heavy bag are very useful for developing power in the short strikes. Mouthguards and groin guards are sensible for any pressure testing, and light MMA-style gloves or sparring mitts for the striking drills.

You also need a particular attitude. Close-range training is intimate and sometimes uncomfortable. You will be pushed, pulled, squashed and occasionally hurt. Your partner will be too. The training only works if both of you look after each other, communicate honestly and leave your egos at the door. I have seen more injuries caused by pride than by any technique. If you are tapped, let go at once. If you are caught, tap early. There is no shame in either.

Finally, you need patience. Sensitivity in particular develops slowly. In the early months of kakie practice most people feel nothing but sore forearms and frustration. Then one day something shifts and you start to feel your partner's intention a fraction before they move. It is one of the great pleasures of the art, and it cannot be rushed.

## How the Book Is Organised

The book is divided into six parts of three chapters each, and they build on one another.

*Part One*, which you are reading now, lays the foundations: the context of the art, the Sanchin structure that holds everything together, and the geometry of distance and angles at close range.

*Part Two* deals with sensitivity and trapping. We study kakie, the sticky-hands exercise at the centre of Goju-Ryu close-range training, then the trapping methods that grow out of it, and finally the combinations that take you from a trap to a finishing strike.

*Part Three* covers the short weapons: elbows, knees and the way they combine with head control in the clinch.

*Part Four* deals with throws and takedowns: breaking balance, the throws found in the kata, and the skills of falling safely and getting back to your feet.

*Part Five* covers joint controls and restraint: the principles of leverage, the main wrist, elbow and shoulder controls, and the escapes from grabs and holds.

*Part Six* brings it all together with timing and counter-attacking, a progressive method for sparring at close range, and a frank discussion of real-world encounters, the law and the aftermath.

At the back of the book you will find a twelve-week training programme that takes you through the material in a sensible order, a map linking the techniques to the kata in which they appear, answers to the questions I am most often asked, and a glossary of the Japanese and Okinawan terms.

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "I've trained Goju-Ryu for years but I panic whenever someone gets close in sparring. I just cover up and wait."

**Solution:** This is very common, and it is not a character flaw. It is a gap in training. Your nervous system has never been taught that the close range is a place where you can function. Begin with Drill 1.1 and then the kakie work in Chapter 4, at very low intensity, until being in contact with another person feels normal. Only then add striking. Familiarity first, then skill, then pressure.

**Problem:** "My club only does long-range points sparring. How can I practise this material?"

**Solution:** Use the cooperative drills in this book as a warm-up or as a separate section of class, with permission from your instructor. Most instructors are glad to see students taking an interest in bunkai. If you are an instructor yourself, try introducing kakie for ten minutes at the start of each session for a month. You will see the change in your students' sparring.

**Problem:** "I'm small and light. Surely close range is the worst place for me?"

**Solution:** It is the most dangerous place for you, which is exactly why you must be good there. You cannot always choose the distance. The methods in this book rely on angles, timing and leverage rather than size, and many of the best close-range fighters I have trained with have been the smallest people in the room. Pay special attention to Chapters 3, 10 and 13.
:::

## Before You Turn the Page

I want to finish this first chapter with a thought that has stayed with me since I first heard it from an old Okinawan teacher, translated through a student who had lived on the island for years. Someone asked him what the secret of Goju-Ryu was. He thought for a while and said something like: "Stand properly. Breathe properly. Do not be afraid to be close."

That is really all this book is about. The next chapter deals with the first two. The rest of the book deals with the third.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- Goju-Ryu's home range is close: from the moment forearms touch to the moment bodies lock together.
- The kata were designed for a fight that has already closed. They contain grabs, short strikes, low kicks, knees, throws and joint controls.
- Test every bunkai interpretation for pressure, context, consistency and follow-through.
- Go is structure; ju is sensitivity and adaptability. At close range you need both, blended into a heavy, sticky quality the Okinawans called muchimi.
- Sparring and the street are different problems with overlapping solutions. Know which one you are training for.
- This book works from problem to principle to technique to drill to pressure. The last stage is the one that turns theory into skill.
:::

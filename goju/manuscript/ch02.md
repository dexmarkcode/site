# Sanchin Structure: A Body That Holds Its Ground

::: chapter-intro
You cannot trap, elbow, knee or throw from a body that collapses the moment someone leans on it. Before anything else, close combat demands structure: the ability to take force without folding and to deliver force without leaking it. Goju-Ryu builds that structure through Sanchin. This chapter is about what Sanchin actually trains, why it matters at close range, and how to carry its lessons out of the kata and into a moving, messy encounter.
:::

The first time I watched a senior Goju-Ryu teacher test a student's Sanchin, I thought it looked like a strange kind of ritual. The student stood in that narrow, pigeon-toed stance, breathing loudly, arms held out in front. The teacher walked around them slapping their thighs, shoulders and back, pushing on their arms, jabbing fingers into their abdomen. It seemed theatrical, and frankly a little odd.

Years later, after I had been pushed around a few training halls by people much bigger than me, I understood. Every slap and push was a question: *is this part of the body connected to the ground, or is it floating?* When the answer was "floating", the push moved the student. When the answer was "connected", the push went straight through them and disappeared into the floor.

That connection is what close combat lives on. At long range you can get away with poor structure, because nobody is touching you long enough to find the weakness. At close range, the other person is touching you all the time, and your weaknesses are discovered within seconds.

## The Problem: Being Moved

Let us start, as always, with the problem.

When someone is close to you, they will push, pull and twist you, sometimes deliberately and sometimes simply because two bodies in contact are always exchanging force. The danger is not the push itself. The danger is what the push does to your *ability to act*.

If a push rocks you onto your heels, you cannot strike with any power until you have recovered. If a pull brings your head forward over your feet, you are a few inches from being thrown or kneed in the face. If a twist turns your shoulders away from the other person, you have lost your guard and exposed your back. In each case, a small loss of structure leads to a big loss of options.

In sport sparring, this shows up as the competitor who gets bundled backwards out of the area every time an opponent rushes in. In the street, it shows up as the person who gets pushed against a wall, pinned and hit, or who goes down the moment someone grabs their jacket.

The solution is not strength. A strong person with poor structure can still be moved, because they are using muscle to fight the problem rather than skeleton and ground. The solution is to organise your body so that force has a clear, short path to the floor, and so that your muscles are free to move rather than being tied up holding you together.

## The Principle: Alignment, Tension and Breath

Sanchin teaches structure through three ingredients, and it is worth understanding each before we look at the stance itself.

### Alignment

Your skeleton is the strongest thing you own. Bones in good alignment can bear enormous loads with very little muscular effort. Bones out of alignment rely on muscles and ligaments to stop them buckling, and muscles get tired.

At close range, alignment means a few practical things. Your head is over your spine, not poking forward. Your shoulders are down, not hunched up around your ears. Your elbows point down towards the floor, not out to the sides, so that a push on your forearm travels along the bones of your arm into your shoulder and down your back. Your pelvis is tucked slightly, so that your lower back is not arched and your spine forms a single strong column. Your knees track over your feet, so that force through your legs goes straight into the ground.

### Tension

Sanchin is famous, and sometimes infamous, for the tension it develops. People who see it for the first time often think it is about making the whole body as rigid as possible. That is a misunderstanding, and practising it that way is both exhausting and bad for your blood pressure.

The tension in Sanchin is *selective and connecting*. You are learning to engage the muscles that link the parts of the body together, especially the large muscles of the back, the abdomen, the buttocks and the inner thighs, so that a force arriving at one point is shared by the whole body. Think of it as joining up the parts rather than freezing them.

In close combat, this connecting tension appears in short bursts. You do not walk around tense. You become connected at the moment of contact, of impact, or of resisting a push, and then you relax again. Learning to switch between the two quickly is one of the core skills of the art.

### Breath

Sanchin breathing is deep, controlled and linked to movement. You breathe in through the nose as the arm draws back, and out through the mouth as it extends, with the out-breath often audible. The breathing is driven from the lower abdomen, the area the Japanese call the *tanden*, a little below the navel.

The practical value of this at close range is enormous. Breathing out on effort stiffens the trunk and protects the organs when you are struck. Breathing in a controlled way keeps you calm when adrenaline wants you to gasp and freeze. And breathing that stays low in the belly keeps your centre of gravity down rather than lifting it into your chest.

::: fault
**Common fault: holding the breath**

Under pressure most people stop breathing. You will see it in sparring: a student closes the distance, starts working in the clinch and holds their breath for five or six seconds. They then have to break off, gasping. Train yourself to breathe out on every strike, every push and every effort. If you hear yourself going silent, you are probably holding your breath.
:::

## The Sanchin Stance

The stance itself, sanchin dachi, looks odd at first but makes perfect sense once you understand what it is for.

Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart side to side. Step one foot forward so that the heel of the front foot is roughly level with the toes of the back foot. Turn both feet slightly inwards so that the toes point a little towards each other. Bend the knees slightly and press them gently inwards, as if you were trying to hold a ball between them. Tuck the pelvis, so that the buttocks are drawn under rather than sticking out. Grip the floor lightly with the toes.

![**Figure 2.1** Sanchin dachi from above and from the side. The feet are turned slightly in, the knees press gently inwards, and the pelvis is tucked so that the spine forms a single column from head to tailbone.](figures/fig2_1.png)

When you stand like this, several things happen. The inward pressure of the knees locks the legs into a stable triangle with the floor. The tucked pelvis connects the legs to the spine. The slightly forward-and-back foot position means you can absorb a push from the front without tipping, and the narrow side-to-side width means you are not split open by a push from the side.

People often ask why the stance is so short. Surely a longer stance would be more stable? Against a single push in one direction, yes. But at close range the forces come from every direction and change constantly. A long, deep stance is very stable against a push along its length and very unstable against a push across it. It is also slow to move from. Sanchin dachi gives up a little stability in any single direction in exchange for being reasonably stable in all directions and ready to step in any of them. That is exactly the trade-off you want when someone is grabbing and pushing you.

::: kata
**Kata link: Sanchin**

In the kata, you step forward in sanchin dachi, each step following a crescent path in which the moving foot travels in towards the standing foot and then out again. That crescent step is not decoration. It keeps the knees protected and the groin covered as you move, and it teaches you to keep your weight centred while you travel. When you step into an opponent at close range, you are using the same movement.
:::

## The Arms in Sanchin

The arms in Sanchin are held with the elbows down and roughly a fist's width from the ribs, the forearms angled slightly upwards, and the fists at about shoulder height. As the kata progresses, you punch slowly with full tension, open the hands, circle them, and draw them back with a pulling motion.

The detail that matters most for close combat is the elbow. An elbow that stays down and close to the body is strong. A push on the forearm travels along the arm and into the back. An elbow that flares out to the side is weak. A push on the forearm simply folds the arm and opens a path to the body.

Try this with a partner. Hold your forearm up in front of you, elbow down, and ask your partner to push steadily on your wrist towards your chest. Now let your elbow drift outwards and ask them to push again. Most people are astonished by the difference. With the elbow down, they can resist a strong push with little effort. With the elbow out, their arm collapses almost immediately.

![**Figure 2.2** Elbow alignment. Left: elbow down, the push travels along the forearm and upper arm into the back and legs. Right: elbow flared, the same push folds the arm and reaches the body.](figures/fig2_2.png)

This single principle, *keep the elbow down and in*, will come up again and again throughout this book. It is the foundation of good blocking, of good kakie, of strong trapping and of resisting a grab. It is also the reason so many people's guards collapse in sparring: they hold their hands up, but their elbows float away from the body, and the first push or heavy punch smashes straight through.

## Testing Structure: Shime

The traditional method of testing Sanchin is called *shime*, which means something like "tightening" or "checking". The teacher or a partner pushes, presses and strikes the person performing the kata to see whether their structure holds.

Done badly, shime becomes a test of how hard someone can hit and how much punishment the student can take. That is not the point, and it is not a sensible way to train for most people. Done well, shime is a feedback tool. The partner is asking questions of the structure, and the person performing is learning to answer them.

Here is a progressive way to use shime for close-combat training.

::: drill
**Drill 2.1: Progressive shime**

*Stage one: static pushes.* Stand in sanchin dachi with the arms in the guard position. Your partner pushes steadily on the front of your shoulders, then on your forearms, then on your back between the shoulder blades, then on the sides of your hips. Each push builds slowly over three seconds and is held for three seconds. Your job is to stay still and breathe, finding the alignment that sends the push into the floor.

*Stage two: varying direction.* Your partner pushes from changing directions without warning, still slowly. Notice which directions find you out. Most people are weakest to a push diagonally backwards across the line between their feet.

*Stage three: sudden pushes.* Your partner now gives short, sharp pushes rather than slow ones. You will have to connect quickly at the moment of contact rather than holding tension all the time.

*Stage four: moving shime.* You step forward and back through the kata pattern while your partner pushes. This is the bridge between static structure and moving structure, and it is where most people's Sanchin falls apart for the first time.
:::

![**Figure 2.3** Shime testing points. Your partner checks the shoulders, the forearms, the upper back, the abdomen and the hips. The arrows show the direction of each test push.](figures/fig2_3.png)

The fourth stage is the most important for close combat, because nobody fights standing still. If your structure only works when you are stationary, it will not help you when someone rushes you.

## From Static Structure to Moving Structure

This is where many traditional practitioners get stuck. They develop a beautiful, solid Sanchin in the kata, and then they go to spar and their structure disappears. They stand up tall, lean back, flare their elbows and get pushed around like everyone else.

The reason is simple. In the kata, structure is the *only* thing they are thinking about. In sparring, they are thinking about a hundred other things: the punch coming at them, the punch they want to throw, the score, their breathing, their fear. Structure drops to the bottom of the list.

The answer is not to think harder about structure. It is to train structure under progressively more distracting conditions until it becomes automatic. Here are the stages I use.

First, structure while *being pushed* in the kata, as in Drill 2.1.

Second, structure while *pushing*. Two partners face each other in sanchin dachi, forearms touching, and push against each other slowly. The aim is to make your partner adjust their stance without adjusting your own.

Third, structure while *pushing and moving*. The same exercise, but now each person may step to gain an advantage. This begins to look like the pushing games found in many Chinese styles and in sumo training.

Fourth, structure while *striking*. Short punches and elbows on a pad held firmly against your partner's chest, with your partner leaning forward into you. You must strike without being pushed backwards.

Fifth, structure while *being struck*. Light body shots on the abdomen and ribs while you maintain your stance and breathing. This is the classic conditioning element of Sanchin, and it should be built up very gradually.

Sixth, structure in *kakie and clinch work*, which we will cover in later chapters.

::: drill
**Drill 2.2: The pushing square**

Mark out a square about three metres across. Two partners start in the middle, hands on each other's shoulders, in a natural fighting posture. On the signal, each tries to push the other out of the square. Strikes are not allowed, and neither are throws for now. Rounds last thirty seconds.

You will learn more about structure in five rounds of this game than in a month of standing in sanchin dachi, but only if you have done the standing first. Watch for the moment your hips go back and your head goes forward. That is the moment you lose.
:::

## The Hips and the Ground

Everything I have said so far comes down to one idea: force needs a path to the ground, and the hips are the junction where the upper body meets the legs. If the hips are in the right place, force from the arms and shoulders flows into the legs and down. If they are not, force stops at the hips and bends you in half.

There are three common hip errors at close range.

The *hips back* error. The person leans forward from the waist with their backside sticking out. This is very common when someone is trying to push forward or resist a push. It feels strong, but it is not. The hips are behind the feet, the head is in front of them, and a simple pull or a step to the side will make the person fall forward.

The *hips forward* error. The person leans back with their hips pushed forward, usually because they are trying to get their head away from a strike. Now the hips are in front of the feet and the head behind, and a push on the chest or a sweep of the legs will put them on their back.

The *hips square* error. The person's hips face directly at the opponent and stay there. This is not always a mistake, but at close range it means they cannot turn to generate power for strikes, and they offer the whole front of their body as a target.

Good close-range structure keeps the hips *under* the shoulders, the head *over* the hips, and the hips *free to turn*. Sanchin teaches the first two. Tensho and the turning movements of the other kata teach the third.

## Tension and Relaxation: The Pulse

I mentioned earlier that close-combat tension comes in bursts. I want to explain this in more detail, because it is one of the most misunderstood aspects of Goju-Ryu.

Watch a skilled close-range fighter and you will see that they are soft most of the time. Their arms are relaxed, their shoulders are loose, their breathing is easy. Then, at the moment of contact, of striking or of being pushed, their whole body connects for a fraction of a second. Then they relax again.

I call this the *pulse*. It is like a heartbeat of tension and release, and it is what makes their movement both fast and powerful. Fast, because relaxed muscles move quickly. Powerful, because the body connects at the moment it matters.

Sanchin, practised slowly with full tension throughout, develops the *capacity* for connection. It teaches you which muscles to engage and how it feels when the body is joined up. But on its own it can produce a person who is tense all the time. To develop the pulse, you need to practise the kata, and close-range drills, with the tension applied only at the end of each movement.

::: drill
**Drill 2.3: Pulse punching**

Stand in sanchin dachi in front of a heavy bag or a pad held firmly by a partner. Rest your fist lightly on the target, arm relaxed. Without drawing back, connect your whole body in one sharp pulse: grip the floor, tighten the abdomen, turn the hips a few degrees and breathe out sharply. The fist should drive into the target with surprising force.

Then relax completely. Repeat ten times, then ten more with the other hand. The aim is maximum connection for the shortest possible time, followed by complete relaxation. This is the root of every short strike in this book.
:::

## Conditioning: Building a Body That Can Take Contact

No discussion of structure in Goju-Ryu would be complete without mentioning conditioning. Close combat involves contact, and the body needs to be prepared for it.

The traditional methods of Goju-Ryu conditioning, known collectively as *hojo undo*, include the chi'ishi, the nigiri game, the makiwara and a range of partner exercises such as *kote kitae*, in which partners strike their forearms together in a set pattern. These methods develop grip strength, wrist and forearm toughness, shoulder stability and the ability to absorb impact.

I want to add a word of caution here. Conditioning is valuable, but it is easy to overdo, and the damage can last a lifetime. Hand conditioning on a makiwara, done badly, can lead to arthritis in later life. Body conditioning, done too hard too soon, can cause internal injuries. Forearm conditioning, done to show off, produces nothing but bruises and a lot of ego.

The principle is the same as for any physical training: progressive overload, adequate recovery and attention to warning signs. Start lightly. Build gradually over months, not days. Stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling. And remember that the purpose is to make you more capable, not to prove how tough you are.

### A simple conditioning routine

For most readers, the following routine, done two or three times a week, will provide everything you need for the close-combat material in this book.

- **Grip.** Squeezing a stress ball or gripper, farmer's walks with heavy weights, or the traditional nigiri game. Three sets of thirty to sixty seconds.
- **Wrist and forearm.** Chi'ishi or a weighted stick, with slow circles, figures of eight and lifts. Three sets of ten in each direction.
- **Forearm contact.** Kote kitae with a partner, starting at very low intensity. Two minutes.
- **Core.** Planks, side planks and slow sit-ups with a controlled out-breath. Three sets of thirty to sixty seconds.
- **Body contact.** Light partner slaps and pushes on the abdomen, ribs, shoulders and thighs while you hold Sanchin structure and breathe. Two minutes, building very gradually.
- **Neck.** Gentle isometric presses in four directions using your own hand for resistance. Five presses of ten seconds each way.

The neck work is often forgotten and it should not be. A strong neck reduces the effect of blows to the head and makes you far harder to control in the clinch, where the other person will try to pull your head down.

## Structure in the Real World

It is worth saying a few words about how structure translates outside the dojo, because the real world presents some particular challenges.

You may be wearing shoes that grip badly, or standing on a wet pavement, an icy car park or a sticky pub floor. Sanchin's light toe grip is less useful in shoes, so the emphasis shifts to keeping the knees bent and the weight centred. On slippery surfaces, short steps and a slightly lower centre of gravity are essential.

You may be wearing a heavy coat, carrying a bag or holding a phone. Anything in your hands reduces your options. One of the simplest real-world lessons from Sanchin is that, when trouble seems possible, you put things down or drop them, bring your hands up in a non-threatening position in front of your chest, and settle your weight.

You may be against a wall, in a doorway or between parked cars. Confined spaces actually make structure *more* important, because you cannot step back to escape a push. The wall can even become an ally: with your back against it, nobody can push you over backwards, although they can certainly pin you. We look at wall work in Chapter 9.

Most importantly, you will be frightened. Fear causes the shoulders to rise, the breathing to become shallow and fast, the knees to lock and the weight to shift onto the heels. Every one of these responses destroys structure. Sanchin breathing, practised for years, is one of the best tools I know for overriding them. When I feel adrenaline rising, I breathe out slowly through my mouth, drop my shoulders and soften my knees. It is not magic, but it buys me a moment of calm, and at close range a moment is a long time.

::: street
**Street note: the settled stance**

In a tense situation you do not want to drop into a fighting stance, which looks aggressive and may escalate things, as well as looking bad on camera. Instead, use a *settled stance*: feet about shoulder-width apart, one slightly ahead of the other, knees soft, weight centred, hands raised open in front of the chest as if calming someone down. To an onlooker it looks reasonable and non-threatening. To you, it is Sanchin in disguise, with your elbows down, your hips under you and your hands already between you and the other person.
:::

::: problem
**Problem-Solving Clinic**

**Problem:** "My Sanchin feels solid in the kata, but when my partner pushes me in sparring, I go straight backwards."

**Solution:** You are probably connecting only once you have noticed the push, which is too late. Work through stage four of Drill 2.1 and the pushing square in Drill 2.2. You need to practise structure while you are distracted and moving, not only while you are concentrating on it. Also check your elbows: in sparring most people let them drift up and out.

**Problem:** "Every time I tense up for Sanchin I go red in the face and get a headache."

**Solution:** You are holding your breath and tensing your neck and face. Sanchin tension is in the body, not the head. Keep the jaw relaxed, breathe continuously, and reduce the overall level of tension until you can perform the kata without straining. If you have blood pressure problems, speak to your doctor and practise Sanchin with much lighter tension.

**Problem:** "I'm much lighter than most of my training partners. No matter how good my structure is, they can just push me over."

**Solution:** Structure will make you much harder to move, but it will not make a sixty-kilo person immovable by a hundred-kilo person. The lighter person's answer is to combine structure with angle. Do not stand directly in front of the push. Turn slightly so it slides off you, or step off the line so their effort goes past you. We cover this in detail in the next chapter.

**Problem:** "My knees hurt when I hold sanchin dachi for long."

**Solution:** Check that your knees track over your toes rather than collapsing inwards past them, and reduce the inward turn of the feet. The knee pressure in Sanchin should be gentle. If pain persists, see a physiotherapist before continuing. A slightly modified stance that you can train in for years is worth far more than a perfect one that damages you.
:::

## Bringing It Together

Structure is not glamorous. Nobody films a highlight reel of someone standing still while being pushed. But every technique in the rest of this book depends on it. A trap applied from poor structure gets shrugged off. An elbow thrown from poor structure bounces off. A throw attempted from poor structure ends with you underneath. And a counter-attack launched while you are off balance has no power.

Spend time on Sanchin. Spend time being pushed. Learn to breathe under pressure. And above all, learn the pulse of connecting and relaxing, because it is the engine that drives everything else.

::: keypoints
### Key Points

- At close range, the danger is not the push itself but the loss of options that follows being moved.
- Structure comes from alignment, connecting tension and controlled breathing, not from raw strength.
- Keep the elbows down and in. It is the single most important structural detail in close combat.
- Sanchin dachi trades a little stability in any one direction for reasonable stability in all directions and readiness to move.
- Use shime as a feedback tool, progressing from static pushes to moving shime.
- Train the pulse: connect briefly at the moment of contact, then relax completely.
- Condition progressively and sensibly. The purpose is capability, not toughness for its own sake.
:::

::: exercises
### Training Questions

1. Ask a partner to push you from eight different directions while you stand in sanchin dachi. Which direction moves you most easily? Why?
2. Perform Drill 2.3 with your eyes closed. Does your connection improve or worsen? What does that tell you about where your attention usually goes?
3. Film yourself in thirty seconds of close-range sparring. Count how many times your elbows flare away from your body. What were you doing at each of those moments?
:::

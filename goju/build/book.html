Preface

This book began as a set of handwritten notes for my own students. Every few weeks, someone would ask me a question that I could not answer properly in the few minutes at the end of a class. What is this movement in Seipai actually for? Why does my kakie fall apart when my partner speeds up? Why do I keep getting thrown when I try to clinch? How much force am I allowed to use if someone grabs me outside a pub? I began writing the answers down, drawing little diagrams in the margins, and before long the notes had become a folder, and the folder had become a box.

What you are holding is that box, sorted, tested and rewritten many times over.

It is not a history of Goju-Ryu, although there is a little history in it. It is not a kata manual, although the kata run through every chapter. And it is not a book of tricks. It is a practical guide to one particular part of the art, the close range, written for people who want to understand that range and function well within it, whether in the dojo, in sparring or, if it ever comes to it, in the street.

I have tried to write the book I wish I had been given twenty-five years ago. That means plain language rather than mystique. It means starting from problems that real people face rather than from techniques for their own sake. It means being honest about what works under pressure and what does not, about the dangers of close-range training, and about the law. And it means a great many diagrams, because a picture of where the elbow goes is worth a page of description.

Nothing in these pages is entirely my own. I have learned from teachers, training partners, students, judoka, boxers, wrestlers, police officers and door staff, and from a fair number of people who were trying very hard to hit me at the time. Where a method has proved its worth, I have kept it. Where it has not, I have left it out, however traditional it might be.

A word on language. I write in British English, so you will find defence, centre and practise (as a verb) throughout. I use Japanese terms where they are standard in Goju-Ryu dojo, always with an explanation, and there is a glossary at the back. I refer to the person applying a technique as you and to the training partner or opponent as the other person or your partner, which I find clearer than the traditional tori and uke.

Finally, please train safely. Close combat is the most intimate and the most dangerous part of martial arts, and the skills in this book can cause real harm. Find a good teacher, train with partners you trust, tap early, release quickly and look after each other. The art is meant to make you safer, not to give you injuries you will carry for the rest of your life.

How to Use This Book

Who this book is for

This book is written for three kinds of reader.

The Goju-Ryu practitioner who knows the kata and wants to understand what they are teaching at close range. You will find the kata linked to practical applications throughout, and a map of those links at the back.

The karateka of any style who keeps being smothered when sparring closes up, or who wants to add close-range skills to a long-range game. The principles of structure, sensitivity, trapping and short striking apply to every style.

The reader interested in self-protection who wants a sensible, honest guide to the distance at which most real violence happens. You do not need to have trained in Goju-Ryu to benefit, although you will need a training partner and, ideally, a teacher.

Reading the diagrams

The diagrams follow simple conventions, explained in detail in Chapter 1.

  • You are drawn in solid black.
  • The opponent or partner is drawn in white with a black outline.
  • Far-side limbs are drawn in grey, so you can tell the near arm or leg from the far one.
  • Solid arrows show the main movement; dashed arrows show a preparation or secondary movement.
  • A starburst marks a point of impact.
  • Numbered circles show the order of events within one picture; numbered panels show a sequence.
  • Footprint and overhead diagrams show footwork and positions from above, with your feet or body in black and the other person’s in white.

The figures are deliberately simple. They show where the body goes and which way the force travels. Read the text alongside them, then try the movement slowly with a partner.

How to train with this book

Go slowly. Every technique should first be practised at walking pace with a cooperative partner. Speed and resistance come later.

Follow the progressions. The drills are arranged from cooperative to resisting for a reason. Skipping stages is the fastest route to injury and frustration.

Use the twelve-week programme. If you are not sure where to begin, the programme at the back of the book takes you through the material in a sensible order, with suggested sessions for each week.

Train with a teacher. A book cannot watch you and correct your mistakes. A good teacher can. Use this book alongside regular instruction, not instead of it.

Keep a diary. Note what you practised, what worked and what did not. Over months, the diary becomes a record of your progress and a guide to what to work on next.

Introduction: The Distance Where Fights Are Decided

Close your eyes for a moment and picture a fight. Not a film fight, not a competition, but the kind of thing that happens outside a takeaway at midnight, in a car park after a football match, or in a hallway when an argument gets out of hand.

What do you see? Almost certainly not two people bouncing on their toes at kicking distance, trading neat techniques. You see two people very close together. Someone is shouting into someone else’s face. There is a push, a grab at a jacket, a wild swing, then both of them are wrapped up in each other, pulling, punching at short range, stumbling into walls, perhaps falling to the ground.

That is the close range. It is where most real violence happens, and it is where a surprising amount of sport fighting is decided too. It is also, for a great many trained martial artists, the place where their training stops working.

I have met black belts with beautiful kata and fast, accurate sparring who froze completely when someone grabbed their collar. I have met competition champions who were bundled around the mat by untrained but aggressive partners the moment the distance closed. I have met kind, capable people who had trained for years and still had no real idea what to do if someone put their hands on them. None of this was their fault. They had simply never been taught the close range in a way that made sense.

Why Goju-Ryu?

Goju-Ryu is, in my view, one of the best-equipped traditional arts for close combat. Its origins in southern Chinese boxing, its emphasis on structure through Sanchin and sensitivity through kakie, its short and powerful strikes, and the throws, joint controls and grappling movements hidden in its kata all point to the close range as its natural home.

But knowing that the material is there is not the same as being able to use it. Many Goju-Ryu practitioners perform kata full of close-range applications without ever being shown what those applications are, or being given a way to train them against a resisting partner. The kata remain beautiful shapes rather than practical skills.

This book is an attempt to close that gap.

What This Book Will Give You

By the end of this book, if you train the material properly, you should be able to:

  • stand and move with structure that holds when someone pushes, pulls or leans on you;
  • manage distance and angles so that you arrive at close range in a safe position, off the other person’s line;
  • feel what another person is doing through contact, and respond before you see it;
  • trap and control their arms to open lines for your own strikes;
  • use elbows and knees with power and precision, and string them into combinations;
  • hold your own in the clinch, control the head and posture, and get off a wall;
  • break balance and throw, using movements from the kata, and fall safely yourself;
  • control, steer and restrain with joint controls when a strike would be wrong;
  • escape from grabs, holds and neck attacks;
  • time your counter-attacks to the other person’s actions;
  • spar at close range safely and productively; and
  • understand awareness, de-escalation, the law and the aftermath of real encounters.

That is a great deal, and it will not happen quickly. Close-range skill is built slowly, over months and years. But each chapter gives you something you can use immediately, and the skills accumulate.

A Problem-Solving Book

You will notice that this book is organised around problems. That is deliberate. Techniques on their own are answers without questions. If you know thirty elbow strikes but not which situation each one solves, you will not be able to find the right one when you need it.

So every chapter starts with a problem that people really face: being pushed backwards, being grabbed, being smothered, being held from behind. It then gives you the principle that solves that kind of problem, the techniques that apply it, the drills that build it and the pressure tests that prove it. And it ends with a clinic of the difficulties students most often encounter when they train it, with the solutions that have worked for my students.

If you are stuck, look at the clinic first. Someone has almost certainly been stuck in the same place before you.

A Word About Honesty

Martial arts books have a long tradition of exaggeration. Techniques are described as unstoppable, masters as invincible, secrets as ancient and infallible. I have tried hard to avoid all of that.

Nothing in this book is unstoppable. Every technique can fail, and many will fail the first hundred times you try them against a resisting partner. Some of the kata interpretations I offer may not be what the creators of the kata intended; they are the interpretations that have proved useful under pressure. And none of this will make you invincible. Real violence is chaotic and dangerous, and even the most skilled people get hurt.

What I can promise is that the methods in this book work well enough, often enough, to make a real difference: to your karate, to your sparring and, if it ever comes to it, to your safety. They have done so for me and for many of my students. With patient training, they can do so for you.

The Close-Range Heart of Goju-Ryu

Most people who walk into a Goju-Ryu dojo for the first time expect to learn to kick and punch from a respectable distance. Within a few weeks they discover something odd. The kata keep pulling them in. The hands keep grabbing. The feet keep stepping into the other person’s space rather than away from it. This chapter explains why, and sets out how the rest of the book will help you make sense of it.

Every martial art has a home range, a distance at which its techniques feel most natural and its training makes most sense. For some it is the long range of the sport fighter, bouncing in and out, scoring with a fast reverse punch and retreating before the other person can reply. For others it is the floor, where wrestlers and judoka feel at home. Goju-Ryu’s home range is the space in between: the distance at which your forearm touches the other person’s forearm, your forehead could touch theirs, and your knee is a few inches from their thigh.

I will be honest with you. For years I did not understand this. I trained in a club that treated Goju-Ryu like any other sport karate style. We performed the kata for grading and competitions, and then we put on our mitts and sparred at a distance that the kata never seemed to address. When I asked my first instructor what the opening movements of Seiyunchin were for, he told me they were blocks. When I asked why we stood so low and so square to block a punch, he told me it was for leg strength. Both answers were partly true and both missed the point.

It was only when I began training with people who had studied on Okinawa, and later when I started to pressure-test the movements with judo players, boxers and doormen, that the picture came into focus. The kata were not describing a duel at a polite distance. They were describing what to do when someone has already grabbed you, pushed you against a wall, or swung a heavy right hand at your head from close enough to touch. In other words, they were describing the situations that real violence actually produces.

This book is about that range. It is written for the karateka who wants to understand what the kata are really teaching, for the sparring competitor who keeps getting smothered when the exchange closes up, and for the ordinary person who wants to be able to look after themselves if the worst ever happens. We will cover trapping, elbows, knees, throws, joint controls and counter-attacks, and we will do it the way I teach in my own classes: by starting with problems rather than techniques.

1.1 Where Goju-Ryu Came From

You do not need to be a historian to train well, but a little history explains a great deal about why Goju-Ryu looks the way it does.

The style grew out of the work of Kanryo Higaonna, a nineteenth-century Okinawan from Naha who travelled to Fuzhou in southern China and spent many years studying Chinese boxing there. Accounts differ about exactly whom he studied with and for how long, and you will find people arguing about the details with great passion. What matters for us is the character of the material he brought home. Southern Chinese boxing systems from that region, often associated with crane and other animal styles, tended to favour a stable, rooted posture, short powerful hand techniques, a great deal of grabbing and pulling, and a training culture built around conditioning the body to absorb and deliver force at close quarters.

Higaonna taught in Naha, and his most dedicated student was Chojun Miyagi. Miyagi trained under him for many years, made his own journeys to China, and gradually organised what he had learned into a coherent system. Around 1930, when one of his students was asked at a demonstration in mainland Japan what the art was called, there was no good answer. Miyagi chose the name Goju-Ryu, the “hard-soft school”, from a line in the old Chinese manual known as the Bubishi, which speaks of the way of breathing being both hard and soft.

Miyagi also did something that shaped the art for the next century. He added two kata of his own, Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni, designed as introductory forms, and the breathing and tension kata Tensho, which sits beside the much older Sanchin as one of the art’s two great training forms. He placed a heavy emphasis on supplementary training with traditional tools such as the chi’ishi (a stone weight on a stick) and the nigiri game (gripping jars), and on partner conditioning of the arms and body.

When you put these threads together, a clear picture appears. This was an art designed by and for people who expected to be grabbed, to grab back, and to need strong hands, strong grips and strong, stable bodies to survive. It was civilian self-protection with a Chinese accent and an Okinawan practicality.

1.2 The Kata as a Close-Combat Manual

Goju-Ryu is usually taught with twelve core kata, plus Sanchin and Tensho. Different organisations order them slightly differently, but a typical list runs: Gekisai Dai Ichi, Gekisai Dai Ni, Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseru, Seipai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei, with Sanchin and Tensho underpinning the whole syllabus.

If you look at the kata with fresh eyes, several things stand out.

First, there are very few kicks, and almost all of them are low. You will find front kicks to the groin and knee height, stamping kicks, and knee strikes, but nothing that would win a points competition by tapping someone on the head.

Second, there is a great deal of grabbing. Hands open, circle, hook and pull back to the hip. The pulling hand, the hikite, is not decoration. It is the hand that has hold of something.

Third, the stances are short and strong. Sanchin dachi, with its inward-gripping knees and narrow base, makes little sense as a stance for long-range fighting. It makes a great deal of sense as a stance for staying upright while someone is pushing, pulling and twisting you.

Fourth, there are turns, drops and changes of level that look strange as blocks but look very sensible as ways of breaking someone’s balance or getting out from under a grip.

Fifth, many of the strikes are short: elbows, hammer fists, palm heels, back fists and short uppercut-style punches. These are tools for the distance where a long reverse punch has no room to work.

Put simply, the kata describe a fight that has already closed. The person who wrote them assumed that the attacker would be close, would be grabbing or striking heavily, and would not politely stay at the end of your fist.

Kata link: Saifa

The opening of Saifa is a good example. In most dojo it is taught as a release from a wrist grab, with the free hand covering the trapped one, a step to the side and a pulling, tearing movement. It is followed by a back fist and a hammer fist. Even the most conservative interpretation accepts that the kata begins with someone having hold of you. From the very first move, the form tells you what range it is concerned with.

A word about bunkai

The analysis of kata is called bunkai, and its practical application oyo. You will find almost as many approaches to bunkai as there are instructors, and some of them are frankly fanciful. Over the years I have developed a simple set of tests that I apply to any interpretation before I teach it.

The first is the pressure test. Does the application still work when the training partner is resisting at a sensible level, or does it rely on them standing still with their arm out like a mannequin?

The second is the context test. Does the application solve a problem that people really face? Defences against a deep, lunging, single punch that stays out at full extension may be useful for teaching distance, but they are not what happens when a drunk man grabs your collar and swings.

The third is the consistency test. Does the interpretation use the movement as it actually appears in the kata, or has it needed so much alteration that the kata is no longer recognisable?

The fourth is the follow-through test. If the application works, what happens next? A technique that leaves you tangled up, off balance or with your back to the other person is not finished.

Throughout this book, every application I give you has passed those four tests in my own training and teaching. That does not mean they are the only correct readings of the kata. It means they work.

1.3 What We Mean by Close Combat

It helps to be precise about distance, because a lot of confusion in martial arts comes from people using the same words to mean different things.

I divide the fighting space into four broad ranges, measured by what the two people can do to each other without moving their feet.

The kicking range is the distance at which only a kick or a long lunging punch can reach. Most people spend very little time here in a real confrontation. It is a transitional distance.

The punching range is where a straight punch can land with the rear foot planted. Boxers live here, and so do most sport karate competitors.

The close range, sometimes called trapping range, begins at the moment the arms touch. Forearm meets forearm, a hand lands on a shoulder, or a grab is made. At this range elbows, knees, short punches, head control and trapping all become available. This is the heart of this book.

The clinch and grappling range is where the bodies are joined: chest to chest, head to head, arms wrapped. Throws, sweeps, joint controls and knees dominate here, and the fight can go to the floor at any moment.

The ranges are not fixed zones. A fight flows between them very quickly, and much of the skill of close combat lies in managing that flow: closing when it suits you, opening when it does not, and never being surprised by the change. A person who is comfortable only at one range is in trouble the moment the fight moves away from it. A boxer who has never been clinched will tire himself out in thirty seconds of tying up. A grappler who has never been struck hard will drop his head at exactly the wrong moment. Goju-Ryu, at its best, prepares you to be at ease in the space where the other two meet.

1.4 Hard and Soft at Close Range

The name of the style is not just poetry. Go means hard, ju means soft, and the idea of combining the two runs through every technique in this book.

At close range the hard and soft qualities take very specific forms.

Hardness means structure. It is the ability to take a push without folding, to deliver a short strike without your own wrist or elbow collapsing, to hold a grip without your arm being peeled away. It comes from correct alignment, sound breathing and conditioning. Sanchin, which we study in the next chapter, is above all a school of hardness.

Softness means sensitivity and adaptability. It is the ability to feel a change in pressure through your forearm and respond before you consciously see it, to yield to a strong push and redirect it rather than meeting it head on, to slip a grip, turn around a heavy arm and arrive in a better position. Tensho and the partner drill kakie are the great schools of softness.

Neither quality on its own is enough. Pure hardness becomes stiff, slow and predictable. A stiff person is easy to throw, because the moment they push back you can use their own effort against them. Pure softness becomes floppy and weak. A person who only yields eventually runs out of space and gets crushed against a wall.

What we want is something the old teachers sometimes described with the Okinawan word muchimi: a heavy, sticky, rubbery quality in the arms, soft on the surface and very hard underneath. Imagine trying to push away an arm made of wet rope wrapped around a steel bar. It gives, it clings, and then suddenly there is nothing to push against and you find yourself being pulled. That is the feeling we are chasing.

Drill 1.1: Feeling hard and soft

Stand facing a partner, both in a natural stance, and place your right forearm against their right forearm at chest height, wrists touching. Ask them to push steadily towards your chest.

First, resist with pure tension. Lock your shoulder and arm and push back. Notice how quickly you become tired, how your partner can feel exactly where your effort is going, and how easily they could pull you forward simply by relaxing.

Next, go completely soft and let your arm fold. Notice how quickly your partner’s hand reaches your chest.

Finally, keep your elbow down and your forearm in place, but let the push travel through your structure into your legs. Breathe out slowly and turn your waist a few degrees so the pressure slides off to the side. You have just felt, in a very small way, the difference between go, ju and goju.

1.5 Why the Close Range Matters

There are three good reasons to study close combat seriously, and they apply whether your interest is sport, self-protection or simply better karate.

The fight almost always closes

Watch any footage of real violence and the pattern is depressingly consistent. Very few incidents begin at kicking range and stay there. Most begin with someone already close: talking, shouting, pointing, pushing. The first blow is often thrown from a distance at which a textbook reverse punch has no room. Then, within a second or two, the people involved are grabbing each other, falling against cars, walls and furniture, and usually ending up on the ground.

Sport sparring also closes, more often than people like to admit. Many karate rule sets stop the action the moment the fighters clinch, which hides the problem rather than solving it. If you watch competitors who also train in other combat sports, you see how quickly someone who is comfortable in close is able to control the exchange.

Close range is where size and strength matter most, unless you have skill

At long range, a smaller, faster person can stay out of trouble. At close range, size, weight and strength become enormous advantages for whoever has them. A heavier person leaning on you will wear you out. A stronger person who gets a grip on your collar can throw you around.

The irony is that this is exactly why the smaller person must learn the close range. You cannot always choose your distance, and if you cannot function when someone bigger gets hold of you, everything else you know is fragile. Skill at close range is the great equaliser, because it is mostly built on leverage, timing, structure and sensitivity rather than raw power.

The close range teaches you to feel

Training at close range develops a quality that long-range sparring rarely gives you: the ability to read another person through touch. Your eyes can be fooled by feints, poor light, adrenaline and fatigue. Your forearms, once trained, are much harder to fool. A good close-range fighter often knows what the other person is going to do before they do it, because they have felt the shift of weight, the tightening of a shoulder or the drop of an elbow.

This sensitivity, once developed, changes the way you do everything else. Your blocks become lighter and more accurate. Your counters become better timed. Your kata stop being a series of shapes and start being a series of conversations.

1.6 Sparring and the Street: Two Different Problems

Throughout this book I will keep two very different contexts in view, and I want to be clear about the difference from the start.

Sparring is a cooperative game with competitive elements. You and your partner agree to rules, levels of contact and targets, and you agree to look after each other. The purpose is to learn. In sparring, a technique that would injure your partner is not a good technique, however effective it might be. You will find, throughout the book, versions of the close-range methods adapted for safe sparring, with controlled contact, safer targets and clear tapping rules.

Real-world encounters are not games. There are no rules, no referee and no agreement. The other person may be bigger, stronger, drunk, armed or accompanied by friends. They may not stop when you expect them to. The environment may be cluttered, slippery, dark or crowded. Your aim is not to win but to get home safely, and very often the best technique is the one that never needs to be used because you saw the trouble coming and left.

The techniques overlap a great deal, but the priorities are different. In sparring you might work a joint lock for thirty seconds against a resisting partner, because that is how you learn it. On the street, a joint lock that takes thirty seconds is a liability, and you should be thinking about strikes, escapes and leaving. I will flag these differences as we go.

Street note

The law in the United Kingdom allows you to use reasonable force to protect yourself or others. What counts as reasonable depends on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be at the time. We cover this properly in Chapter 18, but it is worth saying at the outset: the techniques in this book can cause serious injury. Knowing how to use them carries a responsibility to use them only when you genuinely must, and only as much as you need to.

1.7 The Problem-Solving Approach

Most martial arts books are organised by technique. Here is an elbow strike, here is another elbow strike, here is a throw. That approach has its place, but it leaves the reader with a long list of answers and no clear idea of which question each one answers.

This book is organised around problems. Real fights, and good sparring, are a series of problems that one person sets and the other person has to solve. Someone grabs your lead wrist. Someone pushes you towards a wall. Someone throws a looping punch from close range. Someone is bigger than you and is leaning their weight on you in the clinch. Each problem has a small number of good solutions and a large number of bad ones.

For every major area of close combat, I will take you through the same five-stage process.

  1. The problem. What exactly is happening? What is the other person doing, and why is it dangerous?
  2. The principle. What general idea solves this kind of problem? Usually it is something to do with structure, angle, timing or leverage.
  3. The technique. What specific movement applies that principle here? This is where the diagrams come in.
  4. The drill. How do you practise the technique so that it becomes reliable, starting cooperatively and gradually adding resistance?
  5. Pressure. How do you test it against a partner who is genuinely trying to stop you, within safe limits?

The last stage is the one that most traditional training leaves out, and it is the one that matters most. A technique you have never tried against resistance is a theory, not a skill. I will give you safe ways to add pressure to everything in this book, and I urge you to use them.

Each chapter ends with a Problem-Solving Clinic: a set of common difficulties students run into, with the solutions I have found work best. If something in your training is not working, look there first.

1.8 How to Read the Diagrams

This book contains a large number of diagrams, and they all follow the same conventions, so it is worth taking a moment to learn them.

The person drawn in solid black is you, the person applying the technique. The person drawn in white with a black outline is your training partner or the opponent. Limbs on the far side of each body, the ones that would be partly hidden from the viewer, are drawn in grey. This helps you tell the near arm from the far arm when the bodies overlap.

Solid arrows show the main movement. Dashed arrows show a preparatory or secondary movement, or a path the other person’s limb follows. A small starburst marks the point of impact for a strike. Numbered black circles show the order of events where several things happen in one picture.

Where the action needs a bird’s-eye view, usually for footwork, I use footprint diagrams seen from above. Your feet are solid black, the other person’s are white, and arrows show steps and turns.

The diagrams are deliberately simple. They are not trying to be beautiful illustrations of the human body. They are trying to show, as clearly as possible, where the limbs go, which direction the force travels and in what order things happen. Read the text and the diagram together, then go and try it slowly with a partner.

1.9 What You Need to Train

You need surprisingly little. A training partner is essential for nearly everything in this book, and ideally more than one, of different sizes and temperaments. A mat or a soft floor is necessary for throws and falls. A pair of focus mitts, a kick shield and a heavy bag are very useful for developing power in the short strikes. Mouthguards and groin guards are sensible for any pressure testing, and light MMA-style gloves or sparring mitts for the striking drills.

You also need a particular attitude. Close-range training is intimate and sometimes uncomfortable. You will be pushed, pulled, squashed and occasionally hurt. Your partner will be too. The training only works if both of you look after each other, communicate honestly and leave your egos at the door. I have seen more injuries caused by pride than by any technique. If you are tapped, let go at once. If you are caught, tap early. There is no shame in either.

Finally, you need patience. Sensitivity in particular develops slowly. In the early months of kakie practice most people feel nothing but sore forearms and frustration. Then one day something shifts and you start to feel your partner’s intention a fraction before they move. It is one of the great pleasures of the art, and it cannot be rushed.

Solution: This is very common, and it is not a character flaw. It is a gap in training. Your nervous system has never been taught that the close range is a place where you can function. Sanchin Structure: A Body That Holds Its Ground

You cannot trap, elbow, knee or throw from a body that collapses the moment someone leans on it. Before anything else, close combat demands structure: the ability to take force without folding and to deliver force without leaking it. Goju-Ryu builds that structure through Sanchin. This chapter is about what Sanchin actually trains, why it matters at close range, and how to carry its lessons out of the kata and into a moving, messy encounter.

The first time I watched a senior Goju-Ryu teacher test a student’s Sanchin, I thought it looked like a strange kind of ritual. The student stood in that narrow, pigeon-toed stance, breathing loudly, arms held out in front. The teacher walked around them slapping their thighs, shoulders and back, pushing on their arms, jabbing fingers into their abdomen. It seemed theatrical, and frankly a little odd.

Years later, after I had been pushed around a few training halls by people much bigger than me, I understood. Every slap and push was a question: is this part of the body connected to the ground, or is it floating? When the answer was “floating”, the push moved the student. When the answer was “connected”, the push went straight through them and disappeared into the floor.

That connection is what close combat lives on. At long range you can get away with poor structure, because nobody is touching you long enough to find the weakness. At close range, the other person is touching you all the time, and your weaknesses are discovered within seconds.

2.1 The Problem: Being Moved

Let us start, as always, with the problem.

When someone is close to you, they will push, pull and twist you, sometimes deliberately and sometimes simply because two bodies in contact are always exchanging force. The danger is not the push itself. The danger is what the push does to your ability to act.

If a push rocks you onto your heels, you cannot strike with any power until you have recovered. If a pull brings your head forward over your feet, you are a few inches from being thrown or kneed in the face. If a twist turns your shoulders away from the other person, you have lost your guard and exposed your back. In each case, a small loss of structure leads to a big loss of options.

In sport sparring, this shows up as the competitor who gets bundled backwards out of the area every time an opponent rushes in. In the street, it shows up as the person who gets pushed against a wall, pinned and hit, or who goes down the moment someone grabs their jacket.

The solution is not strength. A strong person with poor structure can still be moved, because they are using muscle to fight the problem rather than skeleton and ground. The solution is to organise your body so that force has a clear, short path to the floor, and so that your muscles are free to move rather than being tied up holding you together.

2.2 The Principle: Alignment, Tension and Breath

Sanchin teaches structure through three ingredients, and it is worth understanding each before we look at the stance itself.

Alignment

Your skeleton is the strongest thing you own. Bones in good alignment can bear enormous loads with very little muscular effort. Bones out of alignment rely on muscles and ligaments to stop them buckling, and muscles get tired.

At close range, alignment means a few practical things. Your head is over your spine, not poking forward. Your shoulders are down, not hunched up around your ears. Your elbows point down towards the floor, not out to the sides, so that a push on your forearm travels along the bones of your arm into your shoulder and down your back. Your pelvis is tucked slightly, so that your lower back is not arched and your spine forms a single strong column. Your knees track over your feet, so that force through your legs goes straight into the ground.

Tension

Sanchin is famous, and sometimes infamous, for the tension it develops. People who see it for the first time often think it is about making the whole body as rigid as possible. That is a misunderstanding, and practising it that way is both exhausting and bad for your blood pressure.

The tension in Sanchin is selective and connecting. You are learning to engage the muscles that link the parts of the body together, especially the large muscles of the back, the abdomen, the buttocks and the inner thighs, so that a force arriving at one point is shared by the whole body. Think of it as joining up the parts rather than freezing them.

In close combat, this connecting tension appears in short bursts. You do not walk around tense. You become connected at the moment of contact, of impact, or of resisting a push, and then you relax again. Learning to switch between the two quickly is one of the core skills of the art.

Breath

Sanchin breathing is deep, controlled and linked to movement. You breathe in through the nose as the arm draws back, and out through the mouth as it extends, with the out-breath often audible. The breathing is driven from the lower abdomen, the area the Japanese call the tanden, a little below the navel.

The practical value of this at close range is enormous. Breathing out on effort stiffens the trunk and protects the organs when you are struck. Breathing in a controlled way keeps you calm when adrenaline wants you to gasp and freeze. And breathing that stays low in the belly keeps your centre of gravity down rather than lifting it into your chest.

Common fault: holding the breath

Under pressure most people stop breathing. You will see it in sparring: a student closes the distance, starts working in the clinch and holds their breath for five or six seconds. They then have to break off, gasping. Train yourself to breathe out on every strike, every push and every effort. If you hear yourself going silent, you are probably holding your breath.

2.3 The Sanchin Stance

The stance itself, sanchin dachi, looks odd at first but makes perfect sense once you understand what it is for.

Stand with your feet about shoulder-width apart side to side. Step one foot forward so that the heel of the front foot is roughly level with the toes of the back foot. Turn both feet slightly inwards so that the toes point a little towards each other. Bend the knees slightly and press them gently inwards, as if you were trying to hold a ball between them. Tuck the pelvis, so that the buttocks are drawn under rather than sticking out. Grip the floor lightly with the toes.

When you stand like this, several things happen. The inward pressure of the knees locks the legs into a stable triangle with the floor. The tucked pelvis connects the legs to the spine. The slightly forward-and-back foot position means you can absorb a push from the front without tipping, and the narrow side-to-side width means you are not split open by a push from the side.

People often ask why the stance is so short. Surely a longer stance would be more stable? Against a single push in one direction, yes. But at close range the forces come from every direction and change constantly. A long, deep stance is very stable against a push along its length and very unstable against a push across it. It is also slow to move from. Sanchin dachi gives up a little stability in any single direction in exchange for being reasonably stable in all directions and ready to step in any of them. That is exactly the trade-off you want when someone is grabbing and pushing you.

Kata link: Sanchin

In the kata, you step forward in sanchin dachi, each step following a crescent path in which the moving foot travels in towards the standing foot and then out again. That crescent step is not decoration. It keeps the knees protected and the groin covered as you move, and it teaches you to keep your weight centred while you travel. When you step into an opponent at close range, you are using the same movement.

2.4 The Arms in Sanchin

The arms in Sanchin are held with the elbows down and roughly a fist’s width from the ribs, the forearms angled slightly upwards, and the fists at about shoulder height. As the kata progresses, you punch slowly with full tension, open the hands, circle them, and draw them back with a pulling motion.

The detail that matters most for close combat is the elbow. An elbow that stays down and close to the body is strong. A push on the forearm travels along the arm and into the back. An elbow that flares out to the side is weak. A push on the forearm simply folds the arm and opens a path to the body.

Try this with a partner. Hold your forearm up in front of you, elbow down, and ask your partner to push steadily on your wrist towards your chest. Now let your elbow drift outwards and ask them to push again. Most people are astonished by the difference. With the elbow down, they can resist a strong push with little effort. With the elbow out, their arm collapses almost immediately.

This single principle, keep the elbow down and in, will come up again and again throughout this book. It is the foundation of good blocking, of good kakie, of strong trapping and of resisting a grab. It is also the reason so many people’s guards collapse in sparring: they hold their hands up, but their elbows float away from the body, and the first push or heavy punch smashes straight through.

2.5 Testing Structure: Shime

The traditional method of testing Sanchin is called shime, which means something like “tightening” or “checking”. The teacher or a partner pushes, presses and strikes the person performing the kata to see whether their structure holds.

Done badly, shime becomes a test of how hard someone can hit and how much punishment the student can take. That is not the point, and it is not a sensible way to train for most people. Done well, shime is a feedback tool. The partner is asking questions of the structure, and the person performing is learning to answer them.

Here is a progressive way to use shime for close-combat training.

Drill 2.1: Progressive shime

Stage one: static pushes. Stand in sanchin dachi with the arms in the guard position. Your partner pushes steadily on the front of your shoulders, then on your forearms, then on your back between the shoulder blades, then on the sides of your hips. Each push builds slowly over three seconds and is held for three seconds. Your job is to stay still and breathe, finding the alignment that sends the push into the floor.

Stage two: varying direction. Your partner pushes from changing directions without warning, still slowly. Notice which directions find you out. Most people are weakest to a push diagonally backwards across the line between their feet.

Stage three: sudden pushes. Your partner now gives short, sharp pushes rather than slow ones. You will have to connect quickly at the moment of contact rather than holding tension all the time.

Stage four: moving shime. You step forward and back through the kata pattern while your partner pushes. This is the bridge between static structure and moving structure, and it is where most people’s Sanchin falls apart for the first time.

The fourth stage is the most important for close combat, because nobody fights standing still. If your structure only works when you are stationary, it will not help you when someone rushes you.

2.6 From Static Structure to Moving Structure

This is where many traditional practitioners get stuck. They develop a beautiful, solid Sanchin in the kata, and then they go to spar and their structure disappears. They stand up tall, lean back, flare their elbows and get pushed around like everyone else.

The reason is simple. In the kata, structure is the only thing they are thinking about. In sparring, they are thinking about a hundred other things: the punch coming at them, the punch they want to throw, the score, their breathing, their fear. Structure drops to the bottom of the list.

The answer is not to think harder about structure. It is to train structure under progressively more distracting conditions until it becomes automatic. Here are the stages I use.

First, structure while being pushed in the kata, as in Drill 2.1.

Second, structure while pushing. Two partners face each other in sanchin dachi, forearms touching, and push against each other slowly. The aim is to make your partner adjust their stance without adjusting your own.

Third, structure while pushing and moving. The same exercise, but now each person may step to gain an advantage. This begins to look like the pushing games found in many Chinese styles and in sumo training.

Fourth, structure while striking. Short punches and elbows on a pad held firmly against your partner’s chest, with your partner leaning forward into you. You must strike without being pushed backwards.

Fifth, structure while being struck. Light body shots on the abdomen and ribs while you maintain your stance and breathing. This is the classic conditioning element of Sanchin, and it should be built up very gradually.

Sixth, structure in kakie and clinch work, which we will cover in later chapters.

Drill 2.2: The pushing square

Mark out a square about three metres across. Two partners start in the middle, hands on each other’s shoulders, in a natural fighting posture. On the signal, each tries to push the other out of the square. Strikes are not allowed, and neither are throws for now. Rounds last thirty seconds.

You will learn more about structure in five rounds of this game than in a month of standing in sanchin dachi, but only if you have done the standing first. Watch for the moment your hips go back and your head goes forward. That is the moment you lose.

2.7 The Hips and the Ground

Everything I have said so far comes down to one idea: force needs a path to the ground, and the hips are the junction where the upper body meets the legs. If the hips are in the right place, force from the arms and shoulders flows into the legs and down. If they are not, force stops at the hips and bends you in half.

There are three common hip errors at close range.

The hips back error. The person leans forward from the waist with their backside sticking out. This is very common when someone is trying to push forward or resist a push. It feels strong, but it is not. The hips are behind the feet, the head is in front of them, and a simple pull or a step to the side will make the person fall forward.

The hips forward error. The person leans back with their hips pushed forward, usually because they are trying to get their head away from a strike. Now the hips are in front of the feet and the head behind, and a push on the chest or a sweep of the legs will put them on their back.

The hips square error. The person’s hips face directly at the opponent and stay there. This is not always a mistake, but at close range it means they cannot turn to generate power for strikes, and they offer the whole front of their body as a target.

Good close-range structure keeps the hips under the shoulders, the head over the hips, and the hips free to turn. Sanchin teaches the first two. Tensho and the turning movements of the other kata teach the third.

2.8 Tension and Relaxation: The Pulse

I mentioned earlier that close-combat tension comes in bursts. I want to explain this in more detail, because it is one of the most misunderstood aspects of Goju-Ryu.

Watch a skilled close-range fighter and you will see that they are soft most of the time. Their arms are relaxed, their shoulders are loose, their breathing is easy. Then, at the moment of contact, of striking or of being pushed, their whole body connects for a fraction of a second. Then they relax again.

I call this the pulse. It is like a heartbeat of tension and release, and it is what makes their movement both fast and powerful. Fast, because relaxed muscles move quickly. Powerful, because the body connects at the moment it matters.

Sanchin, practised slowly with full tension throughout, develops the capacity for connection. It teaches you which muscles to engage and how it feels when the body is joined up. But on its own it can produce a person who is tense all the time. To develop the pulse, you need to practise the kata, and close-range drills, with the tension applied only at the end of each movement.

Drill 2.3: Pulse punching

Stand in sanchin dachi in front of a heavy bag or a pad held firmly by a partner. Rest your fist lightly on the target, arm relaxed. Without drawing back, connect your whole body in one sharp pulse: grip the floor, tighten the abdomen, turn the hips a few degrees and breathe out sharply. The fist should drive into the target with surprising force.

Then relax completely. Repeat ten times, then ten more with the other hand. The aim is maximum connection for the shortest possible time, followed by complete relaxation. This is the root of every short strike in this book.

2.9 Conditioning: Building a Body That Can Take Contact

No discussion of structure in Goju-Ryu would be complete without mentioning conditioning. Close combat involves contact, and the body needs to be prepared for it.

The traditional methods of Goju-Ryu conditioning, known collectively as hojo undo, include the chi’ishi, the nigiri game, the makiwara and a range of partner exercises such as kote kitae, in which partners strike their forearms together in a set pattern. These methods develop grip strength, wrist and forearm toughness, shoulder stability and the ability to absorb impact.

I want to add a word of caution here. Conditioning is valuable, but it is easy to overdo, and the damage can last a lifetime. Hand conditioning on a makiwara, done badly, can lead to arthritis in later life. Body conditioning, done too hard too soon, can cause internal injuries. Forearm conditioning, done to show off, produces nothing but bruises and a lot of ego.

The principle is the same as for any physical training: progressive overload, adequate recovery and attention to warning signs. Start lightly. Build gradually over months, not days. Stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling. And remember that the purpose is to make you more capable, not to prove how tough you are.

A simple conditioning routine

For most readers, the following routine, done two or three times a week, will provide everything you need for the close-combat material in this book.

  • Grip. Squeezing a stress ball or gripper, farmer’s walks with heavy weights, or the traditional nigiri game. Three sets of thirty to sixty seconds.
  • Wrist and forearm. Chi’ishi or a weighted stick, with slow circles, figures of eight and lifts. Three sets of ten in each direction.
  • Forearm contact. Kote kitae with a partner, starting at very low intensity. Two minutes.
  • Core. Planks, side planks and slow sit-ups with a controlled out-breath. Three sets of thirty to sixty seconds.
  • Body contact. Light partner slaps and pushes on the abdomen, ribs, shoulders and thighs while you hold Sanchin structure and breathe. Two minutes, building very gradually.
  • Neck. Gentle isometric presses in four directions using your own hand for resistance. Five presses of ten seconds each way.

The neck work is often forgotten and it should not be. A strong neck reduces the effect of blows to the head and makes you far harder to control in the clinch, where the other person will try to pull your head down.

2.10 Structure in the Real World

It is worth saying a few words about how structure translates outside the dojo, because the real world presents some particular challenges.

You may be wearing shoes that grip badly, or standing on a wet pavement, an icy car park or a sticky pub floor. Sanchin’s light toe grip is less useful in shoes, so the emphasis shifts to keeping the knees bent and the weight centred. On slippery surfaces, short steps and a slightly lower centre of gravity are essential.

You may be wearing a heavy coat, carrying a bag or holding a phone. Anything in your hands reduces your options. One of the simplest real-world lessons from Sanchin is that, when trouble seems possible, you put things down or drop them, bring your hands up in a non-threatening position in front of your chest, and settle your weight.

You may be against a wall, in a doorway or between parked cars. Confined spaces actually make structure more important, because you cannot step back to escape a push. The wall can even become an ally: with your back against it, nobody can push you over backwards, although they can certainly pin you. We look at wall work in Chapter 9.

Most importantly, you will be frightened. Fear causes the shoulders to rise, the breathing to become shallow and fast, the knees to lock and the weight to shift onto the heels. Every one of these responses destroys structure. Sanchin breathing, practised for years, is one of the best tools I know for overriding them. When I feel adrenaline rising, I breathe out slowly through my mouth, drop my shoulders and soften my knees. It is not magic, but it buys me a moment of calm, and at close range a moment is a long time.

Street note: the settled stance

In a tense situation you do not want to drop into a fighting stance, which looks aggressive and may escalate things, as well as looking bad on camera. Instead, use a settled stance: feet about shoulder-width apart, one slightly ahead of the other, knees soft, weight centred, hands raised open in front of the chest as if calming someone down. To an onlooker it looks reasonable and non-threatening. To you, it is Sanchin in disguise, with your elbows down, your hips under you and your hands already between you and the other person.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My Sanchin feels solid in the kata, but when my partner pushes me in sparring, I go straight backwards.”

Solution: You are probably connecting only once you have noticed the push, which is too late. Work through stage four of Drill 2.1 and the pushing square in Drill 2.2. You need to practise structure while you are distracted and moving, not only while you are concentrating on it. Also check your elbows: in sparring most people let them drift up and out.

Problem: “Every time I tense up for Sanchin I go red in the face and get a headache.”

Solution: You are holding your breath and tensing your neck and face. Sanchin tension is in the body, not the head. Keep the jaw relaxed, breathe continuously, and reduce the overall level of tension until you can perform the kata without straining. If you have blood pressure problems, speak to your doctor and practise Sanchin with much lighter tension.

Problem: “I’m much lighter than most of my training partners. No matter how good my structure is, they can just push me over.”

Solution: Structure will make you much harder to move, but it will not make a sixty-kilo person immovable by a hundred-kilo person. The lighter person’s answer is to combine structure with angle. Do not stand directly in front of the push. Turn slightly so it slides off you, or step off the line so their effort goes past you. We cover this in detail in the next chapter.

Problem: “My knees hurt when I hold sanchin dachi for long.”

Solution: Check that your knees track over your toes rather than collapsing inwards past them, and reduce the inward turn of the feet. The knee pressure in Sanchin should be gentle. If pain persists, see a physiotherapist before continuing. A slightly modified stance that you can train in for years is worth far more than a perfect one that damages you.

2.11 Bringing It Together

Structure is not glamorous. Nobody films a highlight reel of someone standing still while being pushed. But every technique in the rest of this book depends on it. A trap applied from poor structure gets shrugged off. An elbow thrown from poor structure bounces off. A throw attempted from poor structure ends with you underneath. And a counter-attack launched while you are off balance has no power.

Spend time on Sanchin. Spend time being pushed. Learn to breathe under pressure. And above all, learn the pulse of connecting and relaxing, because it is the engine that drives everything else.

Key Points

  • At close range, the danger is not the push itself but the loss of options that follows being moved.
  • Structure comes from alignment, connecting tension and controlled breathing, not from raw strength.
  • Keep the elbows down and in. It is the single most important structural detail in close combat.
  • Sanchin dachi trades a little stability in any one direction for reasonable stability in all directions and readiness to move.
  • Use shime as a feedback tool, progressing from static pushes to moving shime.
  • Train the pulse: connect briefly at the moment of contact, then relax completely.
  • Condition progressively and sensibly. The purpose is capability, not toughness for its own sake.

  2. Perform Drill 2.3 with your eyes closed. Does your connection improve or worsen? What does that tell you about where your attention usually goes?
  3. Film yourself in thirty seconds of close-range sparring. Count how many times your elbows flare away from your body. What were you doing at each of those moments?

Range, Lines and Angles: The Geometry of Close Combat

At close range, a few centimetres decide everything. Standing slightly to one side of someone rather than directly in front of them can turn a dangerous position into a safe one. Arriving on the outside of their arm rather than the inside can mean the difference between controlling them and being hit by both hands. This chapter is about the geometry of the close range: distance, lines, angles and the footwork that lets you use them.

I once trained with a former boxer who had turned to karate in his fifties. He was not particularly fast any more and he certainly was not flexible, but he was almost impossible to hit cleanly at close range. When I asked him how he did it, he laughed and said, “I’m never where you think I am. I’m always a step to the side of it.”

It took me a long time to understand how much was packed into that remark. He did not move much. He did not dance about. He simply arranged himself, constantly and almost invisibly, so that the other person’s weapons were pointed slightly past him and his own were pointed straight at the other person. That is the art of angles, and it is one of the most powerful tools a smaller or older fighter can have.

3.1 The Problem: Standing in Front of Someone

The most dangerous place to be in a fight is directly in front of the other person, facing them squarely, at a distance where both their hands can reach you. From there, they can hit you with either hand, kick you with either leg, push you straight back, grab you with both hands and headbutt you. Everything they have is pointed at you.

Unfortunately, it is also where most people end up, because it is where most conversations happen. Two people arguing tend to stand face to face, squared up, at roughly arm’s length. If the argument turns physical, the first exchange starts from exactly the wrong place.

The same thing happens in sparring. Two competitors face each other square-on along a straight line, and when they close, they collide head-on. Whoever is bigger or more aggressive tends to win the collision.

The solution is not to avoid the close range. It is to arrive there at an angle.

3.2 The Principle: Lines and Gates

To think clearly about angles, it helps to have a few simple ideas.

The centre line

Imagine a vertical line running down the middle of the other person’s body, from the top of the head through the nose, the chin, the breastbone and the navel to the groin. This is their centre line. Most of the vital targets lie along it or close to it, and most of their power is directed along it. When someone squares up to you, their centre line points straight at you.

You have a centre line too. Much of close-range skill is about keeping your centre line protected while getting access to theirs, or about turning their centre line away from you so that their weapons point somewhere else.

The line of attack

The line of attack is the straight line joining your centre to theirs. If you stay on this line, you are in the path of everything they throw. If you step off it, even slightly, you are no longer where their strikes are going, and they have to adjust before they can reach you again. That adjustment costs time, and at close range time is everything.

Inside and outside

When your arms meet the other person’s arms, you will be in one of two positions relative to each arm.

You are on the inside when your arm is between their arms, in front of their chest. From here you have a direct path to their centre line, but both of their hands also have a direct path to you. The inside is a busy and dangerous place.

You are on the outside when your arm is on the far side of one of their arms, beyond their elbow. From here their arm is between you and their body, and that arm actually blocks their other hand from reaching you. The outside is sometimes called the dead side, because the other person’s tools are largely dead to you there.

One of the great lessons of the Goju-Ryu kata, and of close combat in general, is the value of the outside position. Many of the opening movements of the kata, which look like blocks, are best understood as ways of getting to the outside of an incoming arm and taking control of it from there.

3.3 Distance at Close Range

In Chapter 1 we divided distance into four ranges. Now I want to look more carefully at the two close ranges, because within them there are important differences.

The touch

The moment your forearm touches the other person’s forearm is a decisive one. Before the touch, you rely on your eyes. After the touch, you can rely on feel, which is faster and harder to fool. At the moment of touch, both people have to decide what to do with the contact: push through it, go around it, grab it or pull it.

In Goju-Ryu we call this moment of contact the bridge. Much of the next part of the book is about what to do once you have made it. For now, what matters is the distance: at the bridge, your forearm is roughly in the middle of the space between you, and both of you can reach the other with a short step or a lean.

Elbow distance

Closer still is the distance at which you can hit the other person’s head with an elbow without stepping. Here your forearms are crossed or folded, your hands are near their shoulders, neck or head, and the space between your chests is about the length of a forearm. Punches still work, but they become short, hooking and uppercut-shaped. Elbows and knees become the main weapons.

Chest to chest

At the closest range, the bodies are in contact. Heads may be touching, hands are on the neck, the arms or around the body, and the hips are close. Here, strikes are short and heavy, and the dominant skills are balance-breaking, throwing and controlling the head and arms.

The skilled close-range fighter moves between these three distances deliberately. A common mistake is to drift into chest-to-chest range simply because the fight has closed, and then get stuck there. Sometimes you want to stay at elbow distance and strike. Sometimes you want to close completely and throw. And sometimes you want to open the distance again and leave. The footwork in this chapter is what gives you the choice.

3.4 Footwork for Close Range

Goju-Ryu footwork is not flashy. It is short, grounded and economical. The feet rarely leave the floor by more than a few millimetres, and the weight is kept centred so that you can change direction quickly. There are four basic patterns that matter most at close range.

Sliding step (suri ashi)

The sliding step is the basic way of moving forwards, backwards and sideways without crossing your feet. To move forward, the front foot slides forward first, the rear foot follows the same distance, and the stance stays the same width throughout. To move back, the rear foot moves first. To move sideways, the foot nearest the direction of travel moves first.

The key is that you are never on one leg and never with your feet crossed. This means you can absorb a push or launch a strike at any moment during the step.

Crescent step

The crescent step from Sanchin, in which the moving foot travels in towards the standing foot and then out again, is useful whenever you need to step forward while staying protected. The inward curve protects the groin and knees, and it also lets you change the angle of your stance as you step, landing slightly to one side of where you began.

Pivot (tenshin)

The pivot is a turn on the ball of one foot, swinging the other foot round in an arc. It is one of the most important movements in close combat, because it lets you move off the line of attack without increasing the distance. When someone pushes or charges you, a pivot takes your body out of the path of their force while keeping you close enough to strike, trap or throw.

There are two main pivots. In a front pivot, you turn on your lead foot and swing your rear foot round, so that your body turns to face the side. In a back pivot, you turn on your rear foot and swing the lead foot round. Each takes you to a different angle, and each is useful against different attacks.

Angled entry

The angled entry is a step forward and slightly to the side, usually about forty-five degrees from the line of attack, so that you arrive beside the other person rather than in front of them. Combined with a covering arm, it is the most common way of moving from punching range to close range safely.

Kata link: Gekisai and Saifa

Both Gekisai kata contain angled steps that land you off the line of the original movement. In Saifa, the step out to the side at the very beginning is a lesson in moving off the line while keeping hold of the person. If you look closely at any of the kaishu kata, you will find they rarely step straight forward into an opponent. They step at angles, turn, and pivot, and that is exactly what this chapter is about.

3.5 Getting In: The Bridge

The most dangerous moment in any close-range exchange is the moment of closing the distance. You are coming into range of the other person’s weapons, and until you have made contact you have no way of feeling what they are about to do. A large part of close-range skill is getting through this moment safely.

The traditional answer, found in many Chinese and Okinawan systems, is to bridge: to close the distance with your arms extended in front of you, so that the first thing that makes contact is your forearm against their forearm rather than their fist against your face. Once the bridge is made, you have touch, and you can feel your way in.

There are three common ways to bridge.

The first is the cover entry. You raise both forearms in front of your face, elbows forward, and step in at an angle. Anything the other person throws lands on your forearms, and as soon as it does you have contact.

The second is the reaching entry. You extend your lead hand towards their lead hand or forearm, as if to shake hands or push it aside, and step in behind it. This is quick and natural, and it works very well against someone who is holding their hands up in front of them.

The third is the counter entry. You wait for the other person to attack, meet their arm with yours as it comes in, and use the contact as your bridge. This is the most efficient method, because they have done half the work of closing the distance for you, but it requires good timing.

Drill 3.1: Bridging on the line

Your partner stands with a pair of focus mitts or light gloves and throws slow, straight punches at your head. You start at punching range. On each punch, step in at an angle using the cover entry, and make contact with their punching arm with your forearm. Freeze. Check your position: are you off their line? Is your elbow down? Is your other hand ready? Then reset.

Begin at half speed and build to three-quarter speed over several sessions. When the cover entry is reliable, add hooking punches, then a mixture of straight and hooking punches thrown at random.

3.6 Getting Out: Opening the Distance

Close-range training tends to focus on getting in, but getting out is just as important. On the street, your aim is to escape, and you cannot escape if you do not know how to create space. In sparring, the ability to step out of a clinch that is going badly is what stops a bad position from becoming a lost round.

The most important principle is this: never simply step straight back from someone who is close to you. If they follow you, they will catch you, because moving forward is faster than moving backward. And if you are on a slippery or uneven surface, stepping back blind is a good way to fall over.

Instead, create space in one of three ways.

Push and pivot. Put both palms on the other person’s chest or shoulders, push firmly, and pivot away at an angle as they rebound. You are now off their line and at a distance.

Frame and step. Put your forearm across their collarbone or neck, elbow down, so that your arm acts like a strut between you, and step back and to the side while keeping the strut in place. They cannot close the distance without driving into your forearm.

Strike and step. Hit something that makes them flinch, such as a short palm to the face or a knee to the thigh, and step out at an angle while they are reacting.

3.7 Angles Against Common Attacks

Let us look at how angles solve some of the most common close-range problems. These are worth learning as principles rather than fixed responses, because the details will change every time.

Against a straight push

Someone pushes you with both hands in the chest. This is perhaps the most common opening to street violence, and it is often followed immediately by a punch.

If you stand still and resist, you meet their strength with your strength. If you step back, they will follow. The best answer is usually a pivot. As the push arrives, turn on your lead foot so that your body rotates out of the line of the push. Their hands slide off your chest and their own momentum carries them slightly past you. You are now at their side, close enough to strike or control them, and their weapons are pointed past you.

Against a heavy hook

Someone swings a big, looping punch at your head. This is the classic untrained punch, thrown with the rear hand, and it is very dangerous because it comes from outside your vision and carries the full weight of the body.

The answer is usually to step in, not away. A hook is most dangerous at the end of its arc, where the fist is moving fastest. Closer to the person, near their shoulder, the arm is moving much more slowly. By stepping in with a cover entry towards their punching shoulder, you meet the arm where it has little power. Your forearm takes the impact near their elbow or bicep, and you are now in close range with the bridge made.

Against a grab and punch

Someone grabs your clothing with one hand and draws back the other to punch. This is the problem the opening of Saifa addresses.

Standing in front of them, you are exactly where they want you. The grabbing arm holds you on the line while the punching arm lines you up. The answer is to move to the outside of the grabbing arm. Step at an angle to the side of their grabbing arm, ideally while covering your head on the punching side. Now the grabbing arm is between you and the punch, and their own arm blocks their attack.

3.8 Walls, Corners and Crowds

Real encounters rarely take place in open space. There are walls, cars, tables, doorways, other people and objects on the floor. These change the geometry completely, and it is worth thinking about them in advance.

A wall behind you takes away the option of stepping back and makes you easier to pin. On the other hand, it stops you being knocked over backwards, and it gives you something to push off. Your priority against a wall is to avoid being pinned square-on. Keep your elbows in to protect your body, and move along the wall at an angle rather than trying to push straight back out.

A corner is worse, because it removes two directions of escape. If you find yourself backed into one, your aim should be to move out of it along one of the walls before the other person can close the gap completely.

A wall behind the other person is an advantage. If you can turn them so that their back is against a wall, they lose the option of stepping back, and your strikes and pushes become much more effective. Many throws are also easier against a wall.

Crowds add the danger of other people joining in. In a crowded space, avoid going to the ground at all costs, keep your head moving, and look for a way out rather than a way to win.

Street note: position before technique

On the street, your position matters more than any technique. Before anything happens, if trouble seems likely, look around. Where are the exits? Where are the walls? Who else is nearby? Where are the obstacles on the floor? A few seconds spent noticing these things can tell you which way to move if things go wrong, and moving the right way at the right moment is often enough to avoid violence altogether.

3.9 Angles in Sparring

In sport sparring, angles are equally powerful, though the context is different. Most sport karate rules restrict close-range work, but within them a fighter who uses angles well has a huge advantage.

The most effective sparring angle is the step to the outside of the opponent’s lead hand. Most competitors stand with their lead foot pointing roughly towards the opponent and their lead hand forward. A small step to the outside of their lead foot puts you at an angle where their reverse punch, the strongest weapon in sport karate, is blocked by their own lead arm and has to travel across their body to reach you.

From there, a short reverse punch or a hooking punch to the body or head is very difficult for them to defend, because their guard is facing the wrong way. In rule sets that allow sweeps, the same angle opens up their lead leg for a foot sweep.

Drill 3.2: Owning the outside

Both partners start in fighting stance at punching range. Partner A tries to take the outside position by stepping to the outside of B’s lead foot and touching B’s lead elbow. Partner B tries to prevent this by pivoting to keep A in front. No strikes, only footwork and touches. One-minute rounds, swapping roles.

Then progress: when A gains the outside, A may throw one light strike. When B stops A gaining the outside, B may throw one light strike. You will quickly learn how valuable this position is, and how hard a good opponent works to keep you off it.

3.10 The Triangle

There is one more geometric idea that I want to introduce, because it ties together everything in this chapter and recurs throughout the book. It is the idea of the triangle.

When two people stand facing each other, each has a stable base formed by their two feet. Draw a line between someone’s feet and then draw a third point, either in front of them or behind them, so that the three points form a triangle. The third point of the triangle is the direction in which they have no support, and it is where they can most easily be pushed, pulled or thrown.

For a person standing with one foot forward, the weak direction is usually diagonally to the side, roughly at right angles to the line between their feet. Push them along the line of their feet and they will resist easily. Push them across it and they fall.

This has enormous practical consequences. It tells you where to stand to be safe: with your feet arranged so that the other person’s force is likely to come along your strong line. And it tells you where to direct your own force: towards the third point of their triangle, across their weak line.

We will come back to the triangle in detail in Part Four, when we study balance-breaking and throws. For now, start noticing it. Every time you see someone standing, look at their feet and ask yourself where their triangle points.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “When I try to step in at an angle, my partner just turns to face me again.”

Solution: That is what a good partner should do, so this is a sign that you are training against realistic movement. The answer is to combine the angle with contact. Step at an angle and make the bridge at the same moment. Once you have contact with their arm, you can feel them turning and either follow them or use their turn to pull them further. An angle without contact is easy to cancel; an angle with contact is much harder.

Problem: “I keep getting hit as I come in.”

Solution: You are probably entering in a straight line, too slowly, or with your hands too low. Work through Drill 3.1 at slow speed and check that your forearms are up, your elbows are forward, and your step is at an angle rather than straight. Also check your timing: entering just as the other person finishes a punch is much safer than entering just as they begin one.

Problem: “Pivoting feels slow and clumsy.”

Solution: Pivots become fast only through repetition. Practise them on their own, without a partner, for a few minutes every day. Keep the knees bent, the weight centred, and turn on the ball of the foot rather than the heel. Then add a partner pushing you slowly, then faster. Most people need several weeks before a pivot feels natural.

Problem: “I’m against a wall and can’t move.”

Solution: You almost always can move, but only along the wall, not away from it. Keep your elbows tight to protect your body, keep your chin down, and slide along the wall at an angle while striking or framing to create a little space. Never stay square-on to someone with your back to the wall. We cover wall work in much more detail in Chapter 9.

3.11 Bringing It Together

The geometry of close combat is not complicated, but it is subtle. A few centimetres of movement, a slight turn of the body or a step to one side rather than straight ahead can transform a position. The fighters who are best at close range are rarely the fastest or the strongest. They are the ones who are always slightly to one side of where you expect, whose weapons always seem to be lined up on you while yours are lined up on empty air.

Practise the footwork in this chapter until it is boring. Then practise it some more. It will become the platform on which everything else in this book rests.

Key Points

  • Standing directly in front of someone, square-on, puts you in the path of everything they have.
  • The outside position, beyond the other person’s elbow, is far safer than the inside, because their own arm blocks their other hand.
  • Within close range there are three distances: the touch, elbow distance and chest to chest. Move between them deliberately.
  • The pivot lets you leave the line of attack without leaving the close range.
  • Bridge as you enter, so that the first contact is forearm to forearm rather than fist to face.
  • Never step straight back from someone close to you. Create space with a push and pivot, a frame, or a strike and step.
  • Every person standing has a strong line and a weak line. The weak line points to the third corner of their triangle.

Training Questions

  1. Stand facing a partner and ask them to push you with both hands, slowly. Try resisting, stepping back and pivoting. Which leaves you in the best position afterwards?
  2. Watch a sparring match and count the times each fighter gains the outside of the other’s lead arm. Does the fighter who gains it more often score more?
  3. Next time you are in a pub, a shop or a station, take a moment to notice the walls, exits and obstacles around you. How would they shape what you did if trouble started?

Kakie: Sticky Hands and the Art of Feeling

If there is one exercise that unlocks the close range in Goju-Ryu, it is kakie. Two people stand with their wrists touching and push, circle and feel. To a spectator it looks gentle, almost meditative. To the people doing it, it is a constant conversation of pressure, timing and intention. This chapter teaches the exercise from the beginning, explains what it is really for, and shows how it grows into a live close-range skill.

I remember my first proper kakie session very clearly, mainly because it was so humbling. I was paired with a woman in her sixties, about half my weight, who had trained on Okinawa decades earlier. We placed our wrists together and began to circle. Within a minute, she had pushed my hand into my own face three times, pulled me off balance twice, and gently placed her fingertips on my throat while I was still trying to work out what was happening.

She was not faster or stronger than me. She was simply listening through her arm while I was only pushing through mine. Every time I tensed up to push, she felt it before it happened and moved out of the way, and my own push pulled me forward into her hand. Every time I relaxed to recover, she felt the gap and filled it.

It took me years to get even a little of that skill. But that first session showed me something I had never understood before: at close range, the fight is felt rather than seen.

4.1 The Problem: Eyes Are Too Slow

Human reaction time to something you see is roughly a quarter of a second for a simple, expected stimulus, and considerably longer for a complex or unexpected one. At long range, that is usually enough. A punch thrown from two metres away takes long enough to arrive that a trained person can see it and respond.

At close range, it is not enough. A short elbow or hook from a few inches away can arrive in less time than it takes you to see it start. And at close range your field of vision is crowded: the other person’s head, shoulders and arms fill your view, and it is very hard to see what their hands are doing below your eye line.

Touch, on the other hand, is fast and direct. When your forearm is in contact with theirs, you can feel the beginning of a movement before it becomes visible: the tightening of the muscle, the shift of pressure, the slight change of angle as their shoulder prepares to move. Trained sensitivity lets you respond to these early signals, which means you are responding to the intention rather than the finished movement.

There is a second problem too. Under stress, vision narrows. People in fights often report that they could see only the face or the fist of the other person, and nothing else. Touch is much less affected by this tunnel vision, which makes it a more reliable sense when adrenaline is running high.

4.2 The Principle: Listening Through Contact

The idea at the heart of kakie is that contact is information. Every point at which your body touches the other person’s body is a source of information about what they are doing, and a channel through which you can influence them.

To use this information you need three things.

First, you need soft contact. A stiff, tense arm cannot feel anything, because the muscles are so busy pushing that they cannot register small changes. Think of trying to feel the texture of a piece of cloth while making a tight fist. The contact must be light enough to feel and firm enough not to slip off.

Second, you need structure behind the contact. Soft does not mean weak. If the other person pushes, your structure must be there to carry the push into the ground, as we practised in Chapter 2. Kakie trains the combination of a soft surface and a strong frame: the muchimi quality we talked about in Chapter 1.

Third, you need attention. This sounds obvious, but it is the hardest part. Most people, when they first start kakie, are so busy thinking about what they want to do that they do not notice what their partner is doing. Sensitivity is above all a habit of attention.

4.3 The Basic Exercise

There are many versions of kakie. The one I describe here is a common form taught in many Goju-Ryu schools, and it makes a good foundation for everything else in this part of the book.

The starting position

Stand facing your partner, each with your right foot forward in a short stance, about a forearm’s length apart. Raise your right hand so that the back of your wrist touches the back of your partner’s wrist at about chest height. Your elbows are down, your fingers are open and relaxed, and your left hand is held near your chest or resting lightly on your hip.

Both of you should feel a gentle, even pressure at the point of contact, as if you were each leaning very slightly into the other.

The circle

One partner begins by pushing forward with the palm, turning their hand over so that their palm presses towards the other person’s face or chest. The receiving partner keeps contact, yields to the push by drawing their arm back towards their own body, and turns their hand so that their palm faces the pusher. As the push reaches its end, about a fist’s width from the receiver’s face, the receiver turns the push aside by rotating their waist slightly and circling their hand outwards and down.

Now the roles reverse. The receiver, who has just deflected the push, now pushes forward along a slightly different path towards the first partner, who yields and turns it aside in the same way.

The two arms trace a continuous circle, or more accurately an oval, between the two people. Each person is alternately pushing and yielding, and the contact never breaks.

The pace

In the beginning, kakie should be slow. A full cycle might take four or five seconds. The pushes should be steady and the yields should be smooth. There should be no jerks, no sudden changes of pressure, and no attempts to trick your partner.

Many students find this frustrating. They want to get on with the “real” training. But the slow, cooperative stage is where sensitivity is built. You are teaching your nervous system what normal, relaxed contact feels like, so that later it can detect anything abnormal. You cannot feel a sudden change if you have never felt the steady state.

Drill 4.1: The silent circle

Perform basic kakie with a partner for three minutes on each side, slowly and smoothly. Neither of you may speak. Your only task is to keep the contact even: the pressure on your wrist should feel the same throughout the cycle.

At the end, discuss with your partner where the pressure changed. Most pairs find that it increases at the end of the push and drops sharply during the turn. Smoothing out these changes is the first real step in kakie.

4.4 What Kakie Teaches

It is worth being clear about what the exercise develops, because if you understand its purpose you will practise it much better.

Pressure reading

The first and most basic skill is reading pressure. Is your partner pushing harder or more softly than a moment ago? Is the pressure moving towards your centre, or away from it? Is it steady, or is there a slight tremor that suggests tension building?

Through hundreds of hours of practice, your forearm becomes something like a very sensitive instrument. You begin to notice changes of a few grams of pressure and a few millimetres of position, and to associate them with what your partner is about to do.

Centre and line

The second skill is understanding where your partner’s centre is and where your own is. In kakie, every push is directed at the other person’s centre, and every yield is a way of turning that push away from your own. You learn to feel when a push is lined up on your centre and when it is drifting off line, and you learn to keep your own pushes lined up while turning theirs aside.

Timing

The third skill is timing. Kakie teaches you the rhythm of push and yield, and once you understand the rhythm you can begin to break it. The moment of greatest opportunity in kakie is the moment when the other person’s push has just finished and they are changing from pushing to yielding. At that instant their arm is neither strong nor ready, and a well-timed push, pull or strike can get through.

Relaxed power

The fourth skill is the ability to generate force without tension. Because kakie requires soft contact, you cannot push with your arm muscles alone. You have to learn to push from your legs and waist, with the arm acting as a relaxed but connected channel. This is exactly the kind of power you need for short strikes at close range.

Composure

The fifth skill is psychological. Kakie gets you used to being close to another person, in contact with them, with their hands near your face. For many people this is very uncomfortable at first. After a few months it becomes normal, and that composure is one of the most valuable things you can bring to a real confrontation.

4.5 Developing the Exercise

Once the basic circle is smooth, you can develop kakie in stages. I recommend not moving to the next stage until the previous one feels comfortable with several different partners.

Stage two: the grab

At the end of your partner’s yield, as their hand turns over, you grab their wrist and pull it towards your hip, while pushing your other hand towards their face. Your partner responds by stepping in, turning their wrist out of the grab and covering your push with their free hand.

This adds the first element of trapping and pulling, and it introduces the free hand, which in the basic exercise does nothing.

Stage three: the turn

At the end of a push, instead of being turned aside, you continue the push and turn your partner’s arm across their own body, closing the line between their two hands. This is the beginning of the trapping work we study in the next chapter.

Stage four: footwork

Both partners now move during the exercise, stepping forward, back and to the sides while keeping the circle going. This is much harder than it sounds, because every step changes the distance and angle, and the contact must adapt to it. It is also much more realistic.

Stage five: changing sides

Partners switch from right-hand contact to left-hand contact and back again without breaking the flow, either by passing the contact from one hand to the other or by changing the lead foot. This teaches you to work on both sides of the body and to handle the moment when the contact switches.

Stage six: double-arm kakie

Both partners use both arms, with each forearm in contact with one of the other person’s forearms. This is far more complex, because there are now two conversations happening at once, and it is the closest of the cooperative forms to a real close-range exchange.

4.6 Free Kakie

The final stage of kakie training is free kakie, sometimes called kakie kumite. Here, there is no fixed pattern. Partners start in contact and try to achieve a specific goal while preventing their partner from achieving it.

Free kakie must be built up gradually and conducted with great care. It is easy for it to turn into a shoving match, which teaches nothing. I recommend the following progression of goals, each practised for several weeks before moving on.

Touch the shoulder. Each partner tries to touch the other’s shoulder with their open hand while keeping contact with the forearm. No pushing or pulling allowed.

Touch the face. As above, but the target is a light touch to the forehead or cheek. This is harder to defend and requires much better line control.

Off balance. Each partner tries to make the other take a step. Pushes and pulls are allowed; strikes and throws are not.

Clear and touch. Each partner tries to clear the other’s arm out of the way with a trap or pull and then touch the chest with the free hand.

Mixed. Light strikes with an open hand to the body and head, light pushes and pulls, and simple traps are all allowed. This is the bridge between kakie and close-range sparring, and it should be done with protective equipment and a very clear agreement about contact.

Drill 4.2: The shoulder game

Partners start in single-arm kakie on the right side. On the signal, each tries to touch the other’s right shoulder with the right hand while keeping the wrists in contact. You must not lose contact, push or pull. You can only move your arm around your partner’s arm, and turn your body.

Score a point for each clean touch. Rounds last one minute. Swap to the left side after each round.

You will discover that the person who wins is not the one who tries hardest to touch, but the one who stays relaxed enough to feel the gap when it opens.

4.7 Common Faults in Kakie

Kakie is easy to do badly, and some of the most common faults can undo all its benefits. It is worth looking at them carefully.

Fault: pushing too hard

The most common fault by far. A student wants to win, so they push hard. Their partner pushes back. Soon both are leaning into each other with stiff arms, and neither can feel anything.

Correction: Agree with your partner on a pressure level, perhaps a third of maximum, and stick to it. If you find yourself pushing harder, stop, reset and start again. The aim is to feel, not to overpower.

Fault: the floating elbow

The student’s elbow rises and drifts out to the side, so that their forearm is no longer a strong lever and their partner can push it easily into their body.

Correction: Keep the elbow down and in, roughly a fist’s width from the ribs, throughout the exercise. If necessary, place a folded towel under the armpit and keep it there while you practise.

Fault: breaking contact

The student loses contact with their partner’s arm, usually because they have moved too fast or yielded too much. Once contact is broken, the exercise loses its purpose.

Correction: Slow down. Make maintaining contact the top priority. In the beginning it is better to have a smooth, slow, unbroken circle than a fast, jerky one.

Fault: leaning

The student leans their body weight forward into their partner, relying on their partner’s resistance to hold them up. If the partner suddenly yields, the student falls forward.

Correction: Keep your weight over your own feet. Your partner’s arm should be something you are in contact with, not something you are leaning on. A useful test is for your partner to withdraw suddenly from time to time. If you stumble, you were leaning.

Fault: a dead free hand

The student forgets about their free hand, which hangs at their side or rests uselessly on their hip. In a real exchange, the free hand is often the decisive weapon.

Correction: Keep the free hand up, near your chest, ready to cover or strike. In the more advanced stages, use it constantly.

4.8 Kakie and the Kata

Kakie is not a separate thing from the kata. It is a way of bringing the kata to life.

Many movements in the kata, particularly the open-hand circular movements of Tensho and the circling hands of kata such as Seipai, Shisochin and Kururunfa, closely resemble kakie movements. When you have practised kakie for a while, you begin to feel these kata differently. The circling hand is not an abstract shape but a way of keeping contact with an arm and turning it aside. The pulling hand is not a decoration but a hand that has caught something.

Kata link: Tensho

Tensho is sometimes called the soft counterpart of Sanchin, and the connection with kakie is very direct. The slow, flowing hand movements of Tensho, in which the open hand circles, turns over and presses forward, are almost identical to the movements of the kakie cycle. Practising Tensho with kakie in mind, imagining an arm against your forearm as you move, is one of the best solo exercises for developing sensitivity. You will find that your Tensho becomes softer and more alive, and your kakie becomes more fluent.

4.9 From Kakie to Fighting

It is fair to ask how kakie, which is slow and cooperative, translates into a real exchange, which is fast and chaotic. The answer is that kakie trains a set of qualities rather than a set of techniques, and those qualities show up in everything you do at close range.

When someone grabs you, kakie has trained you to feel which way they are pulling and to move with it rather than against it.

When someone pushes you, kakie has trained you to yield and turn rather than freeze or shove back.

When your forearm meets someone’s forearm as you bridge in, kakie has trained you to stay in contact and feel what they do next.

When someone’s arm is in the way of your strike, kakie has trained you to move it out of the way or go around it without losing contact.

And when the exchange gets frightening, kakie has trained you to stay calm, relaxed and attentive, because being close to another person with their hands near your face has become familiar.

None of this happens overnight. In my experience, most students need about six months of regular kakie, two or three times a week, before they begin to notice it showing up in their sparring. After a year or two, it changes the way they move completely.

Street note

In a real confrontation, you will not be standing in a neat kakie stance with your wrist politely against someone else’s. But the moment they grab your arm, put a hand on your chest or push your forearm aside, you are in contact, and everything kakie has taught you becomes available. Many people freeze when they are grabbed. A person with kakie experience feels the grab as information, and their body starts moving before their conscious mind has caught up.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “I’ve been doing kakie for months and I still don’t feel anything except sore forearms.”

Solution: You are probably pushing too hard, or your attention is on what you are doing rather than on what your partner is doing. Halve the pressure and close your eyes for some of the time. With the eyes closed, your brain has no choice but to pay attention to touch. Also try working with a more experienced partner who can give you clear, slow signals to read.

Problem: “My partner keeps turning kakie into a pushing contest.”

Solution: Stop and agree on a pressure level before you start. If it keeps happening, change the goal of the exercise. For example, do the silent circle (Drill 4.1) with a rule that whoever pushes hardest loses. If your partner still cannot keep the pressure down, train with someone else for a while.

Problem: “Free kakie always ends up with both of us grabbing each other and wrestling.”

Solution: The goals are too vague. Use the progression of specific, limited goals described in the free kakie section, and keep each round short. Wrestling usually happens when both people get frustrated and stop listening. Resetting every thirty seconds prevents this.

Problem: “I can feel what my partner is going to do, but I can’t respond fast enough.”

Solution: Good: the feeling has arrived, and the response will follow. Work on keeping your arms relaxed and your weight centred so that you are ready to move in any direction. Tension slows everything down. Also make sure your responses are small. Most kakie responses involve moving a few centimetres, not big sweeping movements.

4.10 Bringing It Together

Kakie is slow, unglamorous and often frustrating, and it is the single most valuable close-range exercise in Goju-Ryu. It teaches you to read the other person through touch, to stay relaxed and structured under pressure, to control the line, and to time your actions to the other person’s rhythm.

The next two chapters build directly on it. Trapping, which we study in Chapter 5, is really just kakie with a purpose: using what you feel to pin, clear and control the other person’s arms. Chapter 6 then shows how traps lead into strikes. Without kakie, trapping is a set of memorised patterns that fall apart under pressure. With kakie, it becomes a natural response to what you feel.

Key Points

  • At close range, the eyes are too slow. Trained touch lets you respond to intention rather than to finished movement.
  • Contact is information. To use it, you need soft contact, structure behind the contact, and attention.
  • Begin kakie slowly and cooperatively. You cannot detect an abnormal change until you know the normal state.
  • Kakie develops pressure reading, line control, timing, relaxed power and composure.
  • Develop the exercise through grabs, turns, footwork, side changes and double-arm work before moving to free kakie with specific, limited goals.
  • The commonest faults are pushing too hard, floating elbows, breaking contact, leaning and a dead free hand.
  • Practise Tensho with kakie in mind. The two illuminate each other.

Training Questions

  1. Do three minutes of basic kakie with your eyes closed, then three with them open. What changes?
  2. During kakie, ask your partner to make one sudden change of pressure at a random moment each minute. How quickly do you notice it? Does this improve over several sessions?
  3. Perform Tensho slowly while imagining a partner’s arm against yours. Which movements make most sense? Which make least?

Trapping: Controlling the Arms and Clearing the Line

A trap is any action that stops the other person using one of their arms, even for a moment. It might be a pin against their body, a pull that drags the arm out of the way, or a jam that stops it before it starts. At close range, where both people’s hands are within easy reach of each other’s heads, a single half-second in which one of their arms is out of action can decide the whole exchange. This chapter teaches the main trapping methods found in Goju-Ryu and explains how to make them work against someone who does not want to be trapped.

Trapping has a mixed reputation. Some people think of it as the most sophisticated skill in close-range fighting. Others think of it as a cooperative fantasy that never works against a resisting opponent. Both views have some truth in them.

The truth is that trapping, as it is often taught, does not work well. Long, elaborate sequences in which one person pins the other’s arm, then the other arm, then switches hands and pins again, rely on a partner who leaves their arms out to be trapped and does not move. Under real pressure, nobody does that. Their arms are moving, pulling back, punching, grabbing and pushing, and by the time you have trapped one arm the situation has already changed.

But trapping as a principle works very well indeed. The idea that you should control the other person’s arms while yours are free is one of the oldest and most effective ideas in close combat. The trick is to keep traps short, simple and opportunistic, and to use them as a bridge to something else, usually a strike, a throw or an escape. Think of a trap as a door you hold open for half a second rather than a room you intend to live in.

5.1 The Problem: Two Arms in the Way

At close range, the other person has two arms between you and their head and body. Even an untrained person will instinctively raise them when threatened. Those arms block your strikes, grab your clothing, push you away and, of course, hit you.

You have three basic choices.

You can go around the arms, using angles and hooking strikes to reach targets the arms are not protecting. We looked at angles in Chapter 3.

You can go through the arms, hitting so hard or so suddenly that the guard is overwhelmed. This works sometimes, particularly against untrained people, but it is unreliable against a good guard and a bigger opponent.

Or you can move the arms out of the way. That is trapping.

The great advantage of trapping is that it deals with both halves of the problem at once. When you trap an arm, you remove it as a shield and as a weapon at the same moment. And if you trap it in the right place, it may also block the other arm, as we saw with the outside line in Chapter 3.

5.2 The Principle: Control the Elbow, Clear the Line

There are many trapping techniques, but they rest on a small number of principles.

Control the elbow, not the wrist

The wrist is the part of the arm that moves fastest and most freely. It is very hard to catch and very easy to pull free. The elbow, on the other hand, is close to the body, moves relatively slowly, and controls the whole arm. If you control the elbow, the wrist and hand have nowhere to go.

This is why many of the most effective traps in Goju-Ryu involve pressing on or pulling at the area just above or below the elbow rather than grabbing the wrist. A grab at the wrist is easy to break; a press on the elbow is much harder to escape.

Pin against something

An arm that is pinned against the other person’s own body, or against their other arm, is much easier to control than an arm in open space. When you press an arm into their chest, they have to push against you and against their own body to free it. When you press one arm across the other, both are tied up at once.

Clear the line, then use it

A trap is only useful if it opens a line for something else. The question to ask whenever you trap an arm is: what does this let me do? If the answer is “nothing”, the trap is wasted effort. Usually the answer should be a strike, a change of position or a throw, and it should follow immediately.

Short duration

A trap rarely lasts more than half a second against a resisting opponent. Plan for that. Do not try to hold a trap while you think of what to do next. Trap and strike should feel like a single action.

Feel, do not force

Traps work best when they follow the other person’s movement rather than fighting it. If they are pulling their arm back, a trap that helps them pull it further, into their own body, works far better than one that tries to pull it the other way. This is where the sensitivity from kakie becomes essential.

5.3 The Main Traps

Here are the traps I find most useful. Each is described in its basic form, followed by notes on how to make it work under pressure.

The palm pin (osae)

The palm pin is the simplest and most useful trap of all. You place your palm on the other person’s forearm or upper arm, near the elbow, and press it into their body. That arm is now briefly pinned and cannot be used to strike or block.

The pin works best when the other person’s arm is already near their body, for example when they are holding a guard, have just pulled back a punch, or are pushing against you with a bent arm. You simply add a little pressure in the direction it is already going.

The most common use is to pin the lead arm with your lead hand and then strike over the top of it with your rear hand. Because their lead arm is pinned across or against their body, it cannot rise to block the strike.

Under pressure: The palm pin fails when you reach for the arm, because the arm moves before you get there. Make it from contact. If your forearm is already touching theirs, as it is after a bridge or in kakie, you can slide your hand into position without reaching. And press towards their centre, not straight back, so that the arm is pinned across their own body.

The hooking pull (hikite)

The hooking pull uses an open hand, shaped like a hook, to catch the other person’s wrist or forearm and pull it towards your own hip. As the arm comes towards you, it drags their shoulder forward and turns their body, opening the side of the head and the ribs.

This is the true meaning of the hikite, the pulling hand that returns to the hip in nearly every basic karate technique. In kihon it often looks like a gesture for balance. In application it is a hand that has caught something and is pulling it.

The hooking pull is especially effective against a straight punch or a grab. As the arm comes towards you, you hook it and pull it along its own line of travel, which unbalances the other person and draws them onto your strike.

Under pressure: Do not try to grip the wrist tightly. A tight grip is slow to make and easy to feel. A hook, with the fingers together and the thumb alongside them, catches the arm without needing a full grip, and can be released instantly. And pull with your body, not just your arm: turn your hips and step back slightly as you pull.

The forearm jam

The forearm jam stops an arm before it can be used. You place your forearm against the other person’s arm, usually across the inside of the elbow or the bicep, as they start to punch or pull back. The arm is jammed and cannot extend.

This is particularly useful against hooking punches and against someone who is trying to draw their arm back to punch. Instead of waiting for the punch to come, you stop it before it starts.

Under pressure: The jam must be early. Once the punch is moving fast, a jam will be overpowered. That means you need to read the preparation: the shoulder dipping, the elbow drawing back, the weight shifting. This is the kind of reading that kakie trains.

The cross trap

The cross trap uses one of the other person’s arms to block the other. You take their lead arm and press or pull it across their body so that it lies over their rear arm. Now neither arm can be used, at least for a moment.

The cross trap is particularly effective from the outside position. From there, a pull on the lead arm across their body naturally brings it over their rear arm, and you can strike over the top of both.

The elbow lift

The elbow lift raises the other person’s arm, usually by lifting under the elbow with your forearm or palm, so that it can no longer protect their body. It also tends to lift them slightly onto their toes and break their posture.

This is useful against someone who is holding a high guard, gripping your collar, or pushing at your face. Lifting the elbow exposes the ribs, the armpit and the side of the body, and sets up knees and short strikes.

The pass

The pass is less a trap than a way of clearing an arm out of the line and moving past it. You take contact with the other person’s arm and guide it past your body as you move to the side, so that the arm goes one way and you go the other. Once the arm has passed, you are on the outside line, with their arm behind you.

The pass is very common in the kata, often as a circular sweeping movement of the open hand that looks like a block. It is one of the safest and most reliable ways to get to the outside.

Kata link: kake uke and mawashi uke

The hooking block, kake uke, appears throughout the Goju-Ryu kata. Read as a block, it is a slightly odd movement: the hand rises, turns over and pulls back. Read as a trap, it makes perfect sense. The hand meets the incoming arm, hooks over it and pulls it into a position of control.

The circular block, mawashi uke, or tora guchi (the “tiger’s mouth”), is similarly rich. It contains a pass, a pin and a double-handed push or strike, all in one movement. At the end of Sanchin, Tensho and several other kata, mawashi uke is the culminating movement, and it can be understood as a complete summary of trapping: take the arm, turn it aside, pin it, and strike.

5.4 The Free Hand

I have mentioned the free hand several times already, and I want to emphasise it now. In almost every trap, one of your hands is controlling one of the other person’s arms. That leaves you with one hand free, and that free hand is usually your weapon.

But it is not only your weapon. It is also your protection against their free hand. Remember that if you trap one of their arms, they still have the other one. A trap that leaves you open to their other hand is a bad trap.

There are three ways to deal with this.

First, you can trap in a way that ties up both arms, as with the cross trap.

Second, you can trap from the outside line, so that their trapped arm is between you and their free hand.

Third, you can use your free hand to cover rather than strike, at least for a moment, and strike with an elbow, a knee or your head instead.

Most of the time, the second option is the best. This is why the outside line keeps coming up. A trap from the inside line, between their two arms, leaves you exposed to the free hand. A trap from the outside, beyond their elbow, puts their own arm in the way.

5.5 Trapping from Different Contacts

Traps almost never begin from a clean, neutral position. They begin from whatever contact happens to arise. Here are the common contacts and the traps that flow naturally from them.

From a bridge

You have closed in with a cover entry and your forearm is in contact with their forearm. From here, the palm pin and the hooking pull are the most natural traps. If your forearm is on the outside of their arm, you can slide your hand down to their elbow and press it across their body. If it is on the inside, you can turn your hand over, hook their wrist and pull.

From a block

You have blocked a punch and your forearm is in contact with their punching arm as it starts to withdraw. This is one of the best moments to trap, because their arm is moving back towards them anyway. Follow it with a palm pin, pressing it into their body as it retracts, and strike with your other hand.

From a grab

They have grabbed your clothing, wrist or arm. A grabbing arm is a gift, because it is fixed in place and its owner has committed one hand to holding you. You can pin the grabbing hand against your own body, trapping it there, while you strike with your free hand. Or you can pass the grabbing arm and move to the outside line. We look at releases from grabs in more detail in Chapter 15.

From a push

They push you with one or both hands. A pushing arm is extended and committed, and its owner’s weight is moving towards you. You can pivot, as we practised in Chapter 3, and pass the pushing arm as it goes by, or you can pin the pushing arm against your chest with your hand and turn, so that their own push takes them off balance.

From a guard

They are holding a high guard, hands up in front of their face. This is the hardest situation to trap from, because they are not committing anything. Here, the best traps tend to be quick, light pulls and pins that move the guard just enough to open a line for a short strike, often combined with a feint to make them move their hands first.

Drill 5.1: Trap from any contact

Your partner wears focus mitts. They create a contact of their choosing, such as a punch that you block, a grab on your wrist, a push on your chest or a high guard that you touch. As soon as contact is made, you apply the most natural trap you can feel, followed immediately by a single strike to a mitt that your partner presents.

Begin slowly and cooperatively, with your partner telling you which contact they will use. Progress to random contacts. The aim is to learn to recognise the trap that each contact offers, rather than trying to force a favourite trap into every situation.

5.6 Making Traps Work Against Resistance

All of this is well and good when your partner cooperates. Against resistance, traps become much harder. Here are the principles I have found most useful for making them work against someone who is trying to stop you.

Use their reaction

The best time to trap an arm is when the other person is moving it for their own reasons. When they are drawing back to punch, the arm is coming towards their body and can be pinned there. When they are pushing forward, the arm is extended and can be pulled or passed. When they are reacting to a strike, the arm is moving to protect themselves and can be trapped in that position.

This is why traps and strikes work together so well. Strike first, and the other person reacts. Their reaction moves their arms, and you trap the arms in the position the reaction has put them in. Then strike again over the trapped arm.

Short, sharp and simple

Under pressure, complicated traps fall apart. The traps that survive are the simple ones: a single pin, a single pull, a single pass. Learn a small number of simple traps really well rather than a large number of complicated ones.

Use body weight, not arm strength

A trap made with arm strength alone is easy to overpower. A trap made with body weight behind it is not. When you pin an arm, lean slightly into it, so that the pressure comes from your whole body. When you pull an arm, turn your hips and shift your weight back, so that the pull comes from your legs.

Combine with off-balancing

An arm is much easier to trap when its owner is off balance, because they have to use it to recover. Combine traps with small pulls and pushes that break the other person’s posture, and their arms will often end up exactly where you want them.

Accept failure gracefully

Many traps will fail. The other person will pull their arm free, move away or strike first. That is fine. A failed trap that leaves you in a good position is not a disaster; it is just one more step in the exchange. What you must avoid is a failed trap that leaves you exposed, for example reaching too far for an arm and leaving your head open. Keep your structure, keep your free hand ready, and if the trap does not work, move on.

Fault: chasing the arm

The most common error in trapping practice is reaching for the other person’s arm. You see it, you want it, and you stretch towards it. By the time your hand arrives, the arm has moved, and you are now off balance with your arm extended and your head forward.

Correction: Never reach. Trap from contact, or wait for the arm to come to you. If you cannot trap from where you are, change your position first.

5.7 Trapping in Sparring

Most sport karate rules do not allow much trapping. Grabbing is often prohibited or allowed only briefly, and many rule sets stop the action as soon as the fighters are in contact. Nevertheless, simple traps can be very effective within the rules.

The most useful sparring trap is a quick palm pin or slap on the opponent’s lead hand, followed instantly by a reverse punch. If the pin is quick and light, it is usually within the rules, and it creates a moment in which the opponent’s lead hand cannot block. Another useful trap is a brief hooking pull on the opponent’s lead hand as you step in, which draws them onto your punch.

In rule sets that allow more contact and grappling, such as some knockdown and mixed rules, the full range of traps becomes available, and they can be devastatingly effective, particularly when combined with elbows, knees and throws.

Drill 5.2: Pin and punch

Both partners wear sparring gloves. Partner A holds a guard and moves around freely, but does not strike. Partner B tries to land light punches to A’s head and body. B may use a quick palm pin or hooking pull on A’s lead hand to open a line, but must strike within half a second of the trap.

Rounds of one minute. A scores a point every time B’s trap is held for more than half a second without a strike following. This teaches B to keep traps short and connected to strikes.

Street note

In a real confrontation, a trap is almost always a means to an end. You are not trying to hold someone’s arm; you are trying to create a moment in which you can strike, escape or both. The most useful street traps are the pin against your own body when someone grabs you, the pass when someone pushes or swings at you, and the hooking pull when someone throws a big punch. Keep them simple, and follow them immediately with a strike or with leaving.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My traps work in drills but never in sparring.”

Solution: In drills your partner leaves their arms in place. In sparring they do not. Work on trapping from reactions: strike first, then trap the arm where it ends up. Use Drill 5.1 with random contacts and Drill 5.2 with a resisting partner. And check that you are trapping from contact rather than reaching.

Problem: “When I trap one arm, I get hit by the other.”

Solution: You are trapping from the inside line. Move to the outside before or as you trap, so that their trapped arm is between you and their free hand. If you cannot get to the outside, use your free hand to cover rather than strike, and strike with an elbow or knee instead.

Problem: “My partner is much stronger than me and just pulls their arm free.”

Solution: Do not try to hold the arm with strength. Use your body weight, keep the trap short, and pin the arm against something, either their body or their other arm. Also use their pulling: if they are pulling their arm back, go with it and pin it into their own body. A strong person pulling against you is giving you energy you can use.

Problem: “I can never remember which trap to use.”

Solution: That is because you are thinking about techniques rather than feeling contacts. Go back to kakie and Drill 5.1. With enough practice, the trap that each contact offers becomes obvious, and you stop needing to choose. In the meantime, limit yourself to the palm pin and the hooking pull. Between them, they cover most situations.

5.8 Bringing It Together

Trapping is the art of making the other person’s arms busy while yours are free. Done well, it creates moments of opportunity that would never exist otherwise. Done badly, it becomes a cooperative dance that falls apart the instant someone resists.

The key is simplicity. Learn a few traps well. Make them from contact, not by reaching. Control the elbow rather than the wrist. Pin against something. Use the other person’s own movement. And always follow the trap immediately with something else, usually a strike.

The next chapter takes that “something else” and makes it the focus: how to link traps to strikes, how to string several actions together into fluent combinations, and how to keep the other person’s arms tied up long enough to finish the exchange.

Key Points

  • Trapping is a principle, not a set of fixed sequences. Keep traps short, simple and opportunistic.
  • Control the elbow, not the wrist. Pin the arm against the body or against the other arm.
  • Every trap should open a line for something else, usually a strike that follows immediately.
  • The main traps are the palm pin, the hooking pull, the forearm jam, the cross trap, the elbow lift and the pass.
  • Kake uke and mawashi uke in the kata are full of trapping applications.
  • Trap from the outside line wherever possible, so that their trapped arm blocks their free hand.
  • Use the other person’s reactions and movements to set up your traps. Never reach for an arm.

  2. Watch a video of a boxing or MMA bout and look for moments when one fighter briefly traps or controls the other’s arm. How long does each trap last? What follows it?
  3. Take one kata you know well and find three movements that could be traps. Test each with a partner using the four bunkai tests from Chapter 1.

From Contact to Finish: Trap-and-Strike Combinations

A single trap opens a door. A single strike may or may not go through it. What makes close-range fighting effective is the flow from one action to the next: the strike that provokes a reaction, the trap that catches the reaction, the second strike that lands over the trap, and the change of position that makes the next strike possible. This chapter is about building that flow, so that your close-range work stops being a series of separate techniques and becomes a continuous attack.

When I watch beginners at close range, I see the same pattern again and again. They do one thing, then stop, then look to see what happened, then try to think of the next thing. The pause might last only half a second, but at close range half a second is an eternity. In that pause, the other person recovers, adjusts, and very often hits back.

When I watch experienced close-range fighters, I see something completely different. Their actions run into each other. A strike becomes a trap, which becomes a second strike, which becomes a pull, which becomes a knee. There is no pause in which the other person can recover. Each action creates the conditions for the next one.

This is not because experienced fighters have memorised long sequences. It is because they have learned the connections between actions: what each action does to the other person, and what that opens up. Once you understand the connections, you can link actions together on the fly, adapting to whatever the other person does.

6.1 The Problem: The Gap Between Actions

The gap between one action and the next is where you are most vulnerable. During an action, you are doing something to the other person, and they are usually busy dealing with it. Between actions, you are doing nothing, and they are free to act.

There are three common causes of the gap.

The first is waiting to see what happened. You strike, then pause to see whether it landed. This is natural, but it is fatal at close range. By the time you have seen the result, the moment has passed.

The second is resetting. After each action, you return to a neutral position, such as a guard, before starting the next. This feels safe, but it means each action starts from scratch, and the other person has time to reset too.

The third is not knowing what comes next. You have a strike and a trap in your repertoire, but you have never practised moving from one to the other, so when the moment comes you have to think about it.

The solution to all three is the same: practise the connections until they become automatic.

6.2 The Principle: Action and Reaction

Every action you take at close range causes a reaction in the other person. If you understand the reaction, you can prepare for it, and your next action can be aimed not at where they are now, but at where your first action will put them.

This is the central idea of combination work, and it is worth looking at in detail.

Strikes cause flinches

When you strike at someone’s face, even if you miss, they flinch. Their head moves back, their hands come up towards their face, and their eyes close or narrow. That flinch is predictable, and it opens other targets. The body and ribs are exposed when the hands come up. The legs are exposed when the weight shifts back. And the arms, now up in front of the face, can be trapped.

Traps cause resistance

When you pin or pull an arm, the other person resists. They pull the arm back, push it forward or try to twist it free. That resistance is also predictable. If you pull their arm towards you, they pull it back towards themselves, and you can push it into their body. If you push their arm into their body, they push back, and you can pull it towards you.

Pulls cause posture changes

When you pull someone forward, they lean back to resist. When you push them back, they lean forward. When you pull their head down, they try to lift it. Each of these posture changes opens new targets and new opportunities for throws.

Pain causes withdrawal

When you strike something that hurts, the other person tends to withdraw that part of the body. A strike to the thigh makes them lift or move the leg. A strike to the ribs makes them bring the elbow down. A strike to the face makes them turn the head away.

Once you start looking at close-range fighting in terms of action and reaction, combinations become much easier to understand. You are not trying to remember a sequence. You are reading the reaction your last action caused, and choosing the action that takes advantage of it.

6.3 Three Basic Combinations

Rather than give you dozens of combinations, I want to teach you three in detail. Each illustrates a different principle, and between them they form the backbone of close-range combination work. Once you understand them, you will find that most other combinations are variations on these three.

Combination one: strike, pin, strike

This is the simplest and most useful combination in close combat.

You throw a short strike at the face with your lead hand, such as a back fist, a palm heel or a jab. The other person flinches and raises their hands. As their lead arm comes up, you pin it against their body with your lead hand, using the palm pin from Chapter 5. Their lead arm is now briefly out of action, and you strike over it with your rear hand.

The beauty of this combination is that the first strike sets up the trap, and the trap sets up the second strike. You do not need to find their arm; the flinch delivers it to you.

The key detail is timing. The pin must come at the moment their arm rises, not after it has settled into a guard. That means the pin must follow the first strike instantly. In practice, your lead hand strikes and then, without withdrawing, drops onto their arm.

Combination two: pull, strike, pull

The second combination uses the hooking pull.

You hook the other person’s lead wrist or forearm and pull it towards your hip. This drags their lead shoulder forward and turns their head towards you. As they come forward, you strike their face or neck with your rear hand. They will instinctively pull back against your pull. As they do, you release the hook and immediately hook their other arm, or the same arm at a different point, and pull again, drawing them onto another strike.

This combination teaches the rhythm of pull and release. Each pull draws them onto a strike; each release lets their own resistance carry them into a new position.

Combination three: strike, change line, strike

The third combination uses a change of position rather than a trap.

You strike from the inside line, for example with a short straight punch to the face. The other person blocks or covers with their lead arm. As they block, you use the contact with their arm to pass it, moving to the outside line with a small step or pivot. Now their blocking arm is between you and their rear hand, and you strike from the outside with a hook or an elbow to the side of the head.

This combination teaches you to use the other person’s defence as a bridge to a better position. Their block gives you contact, and the contact gives you the information and leverage to move around their arm.

Drill 6.1: The three combinations

Your partner wears focus mitts and holds them close to their face, as if they were a guard. Work through each of the three combinations slowly, with your partner reacting as described: flinching on the first strike, resisting the pull, blocking the straight punch. Your partner moves a mitt into position for each strike.

Do ten repetitions of each combination on each side, at half speed. Then do them at three-quarter speed, with your partner varying the reaction slightly each time. The aim is not to memorise the combination but to feel how each action leads to the next.

6.4 Keeping the Arms Busy

The principle that ties all close-range combinations together is this: keep the other person’s arms busy. As long as their arms are occupied, defending, resisting traps or recovering from pulls, they cannot be used to hit you. The moment their arms are free, you are in danger.

This means that the best combinations are those that give the other person’s arms something to do at every moment. A strike that they have to block keeps an arm busy. A trap keeps an arm busy. A pull keeps an arm busy, because they are using it to resist. Even a strike to the body keeps their arms busy, because they drop them to protect themselves.

What does not keep their arms busy is a gap. If you strike and then pause, their arms are free. If you trap and then hold the trap without doing anything, they will free the arm or use the other one.

Fault: the one-handed fighter

Many people, when they start working at close range, use only one hand. They trap with one hand and then strike with the same hand, or they strike repeatedly with their dominant hand while the other hangs uselessly. This leaves one of the other person’s arms completely free.

Correction: Work both hands in every combination. One hand controls, the other strikes, and then they swap. If you find yourself using only one hand, deliberately start your next combination with the other one.

6.5 Short Strikes for Close Range

Combinations at close range use short strikes. Long, straight punches with a full step need more room than you have. Here are the main strikes we use in this chapter, and which we will develop further in later chapters.

The back fist (uraken) is a snapping strike with the back of the knuckles, usually to the face or the side of the head. It is fast, needs very little room, and is often the first strike in a combination because it causes a sharp flinch.

The palm heel (shotei) is a strike with the heel of the open hand, usually to the chin, the nose or the side of the jaw. It is safer for your hand than a closed fist and very effective at close range.

The hammer fist (tettsui) is a strike with the bottom of the closed fist, like hitting a table with your fist. It can be thrown downwards, sideways or backwards, and it is very powerful at close range.

The short hook is a curved punch to the side of the head or body, thrown with the elbow bent at about ninety degrees. It goes around the other person’s guard rather than through it.

The short uppercut is a rising punch, usually to the chin or the solar plexus, thrown from close range with the elbow bent. It comes up through the gap between the other person’s arms.

The forearm strike uses the bony edge of the forearm, often to the side of the neck or the jaw. It is very strong and very hard to stop at close range.

The elbow is so important at close range that it has its own chapter, Chapter 7. For now, note that many combinations end with an elbow, because once you have trapped an arm and closed the distance, the elbow is often the most natural strike.

6.6 Combinations from Kata

The Goju-Ryu kata are full of combinations, and they illustrate the principles of this chapter beautifully once you know how to look.

Kata link: Saifa

After the opening release, Saifa moves through a series of actions that can be read as a single close-range combination: a pull with one hand while the other covers, a back fist to the face, a hammer fist downward, and a step that takes you around the other person. Read as a sequence of blocks and strikes against several attackers, it looks disjointed. Read as a combination against one person at close range, it flows: release, pull them onto the back fist, strike down as they double over, and move to their side.

Kata link: Seiyunchin

Seiyunchin is almost entirely performed in a low straddle stance, and it contains no kicks. Many of its movements involve both hands working together: one hand pulling or pinning while the other strikes or presses. The repeated pattern of a pulling hand returning to the hip while the other hand strikes is a lesson in combination work: control with one hand, strike with the other, and use the lowered stance to break the other person’s posture as you pull.

When you practise kata, try to identify the combinations within them. Look for places where one hand pulls while the other strikes, where a block is followed immediately by a strike from the same arm, or where a turn or step changes the angle. Each of these is a combination principle in action.

6.7 Building Combinations Under Pressure

Combinations in drills are one thing. Combinations against a resisting partner are quite another. Here is a progression for building them up.

Stage one: fixed combination, fixed reaction

You perform a specific combination, and your partner reacts in a specific way. This is where you learn the mechanics. Drill 6.1 is a stage-one drill.

Stage two: fixed combination, varied reaction

You perform a specific combination, but your partner varies their reaction. Sometimes they flinch, sometimes they block, sometimes they step back. You have to adapt the rest of the combination to their reaction.

Stage three: free combination, cooperative partner

You strike, trap and move freely, and your partner reacts naturally but does not counter-attack. You build combinations on the fly based on what you feel.

Stage four: free combination, resisting partner

Your partner reacts and resists, and may counter with light strikes when you leave a gap. This is close-range sparring, which we cover in detail in Chapter 17.

Drill 6.2: Three actions

Your partner wears gloves and a head guard and stands in a guard position at close range. You must perform exactly three actions, any combination of strikes, traps, pulls and changes of position, without a pause between them. Your partner reacts naturally but does not counter. Strikes are light and controlled.

After each set of three actions, reset. Do ten sets, then swap. The rule of exactly three forces you to link actions rather than stopping after one, while keeping the drill short enough to stay controlled.

As you improve, increase the number of actions to four, then five. Most people find that beyond five, the quality drops sharply. That is fine: in real exchanges, most decisive combinations are short.

6.8 When the Combination Breaks Down

Every combination breaks down eventually. The other person does something you did not expect, or your trap fails, or your strike misses. What matters is what you do next.

The worst thing you can do is to stop. A broken combination leaves you close to the other person with no plan, and that is exactly when you are most vulnerable.

The second-worst thing is to try to force the combination through, for example by grabbing harder for an arm that has escaped. This usually leaves you overextended and off balance.

The best thing is to flow into whatever is available. If your trap fails and their arm escapes, their arm is now somewhere else, and that may open a different target. If your strike misses because they moved their head, their head has moved, and that may set up a different strike or a pull. If they push you away, you can use the push to pivot to the outside.

This is where kakie becomes so important. A person who has done a lot of kakie is used to the contact changing constantly and to adapting moment by moment. A combination that breaks down is just another change of contact.

When there is genuinely nothing available, the fallback is simple: cover, frame and reset. Bring your forearms up to protect your head, put a forearm or palm on the other person to create a frame, and use the frame to create space or move to a better angle. Then start again.

Street note: the short combination

On the street, the ideal combination is short: two or three actions that create enough damage or enough space to escape. A long exchange of traps and strikes increases the chance of something going wrong, of you being hurt, of friends joining in, and of the incident escalating. Train long combinations in the dojo for skill. On the street, aim to strike, create an opening and leave.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “I always pause after my first strike.”

Solution: This is the most common problem of all. Use Drill 6.2 with the rule of exactly three actions. Also practise striking without looking at the result: hit a pad and immediately follow with a second action, without checking whether the first one landed cleanly. Over time, your body learns to move on without waiting for confirmation.

Problem: “My partner doesn’t react the way the combination expects.”

Solution: Good. That means your partner is behaving like a real person. Move to stage-two training, where your partner varies their reaction deliberately, and practise adapting. Remember that combinations are not fixed sequences; they are chains of action and reaction. If the reaction changes, the next action changes with it.

Problem: “I run out of breath after a few seconds of combinations.”

Solution: You are probably holding your breath or tensing throughout. Breathe out on every strike, keep your shoulders relaxed between actions, and use the pulse of tension and relaxation from Chapter 2. Also build your conditioning with rounds of pad work at close range: thirty seconds of continuous combinations, thirty seconds of rest, repeated six to eight times.

Problem: “Whenever I get close, my partner just grabs me and we end up wrestling.”

Solution: That means your combinations are leaving gaps that they can fill with a clinch. Keep your strikes shorter and your traps tighter. Also learn the clinch skills in Chapter 9 and the throws in Part Four, because some exchanges will inevitably become grappling, and you need to be comfortable there too.

6.9 Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Two. We have moved from the quiet listening of kakie, through the specific traps that grow out of it, to the combinations that turn traps into finished exchanges.

The thread running through all three chapters is sensitivity: feeling what the other person is doing and responding to it. Kakie develops the feeling. Trapping uses it to control the arms. Combinations use it to link actions together, so that each action is aimed at the reaction the previous one caused.

In Part Three, we turn to the short weapons: elbows and knees. These are the strikes that close-range combinations most often end with, and they deserve detailed study in their own right.

Key Points

  • The gap between actions is where you are most vulnerable. Close it by practising the connections between actions.
  • Every action causes a reaction. Strikes cause flinches, traps cause resistance, pulls cause posture changes, and pain causes withdrawal.
  • The three core combinations are strike–pin–strike, pull–strike–pull, and strike–change line–strike.
  • Keep the other person’s arms busy at every moment. A gap gives them a free hand.
  • Use short strikes at close range: back fist, palm heel, hammer fist, short hook, short uppercut, forearm and elbow.
  • Build combinations from fixed to free, and from cooperative to resisting.
  • When a combination breaks down, flow into whatever is available, or cover, frame and reset.

Hiji-ate: The Elbow at Close Range

The elbow is one of the hardest, heaviest and most reliable weapons the human body possesses. It needs almost no room to work, it is very difficult to injure, and it can be thrown in at least six directions from positions where a punch would be useless. In Goju-Ryu the elbow appears throughout the kata, often disguised as something else. This chapter teaches the main elbow strikes, the mechanics that give them power, the targets they suit, and the way they fit into the trapping and combination work of the previous chapters.

There is an old saying in some Okinawan circles that the fist is for the young and the elbow is for the old. I first heard it from a teacher in his seventies who could no longer close his hands fully because of arthritis, but whose elbows were frighteningly effective. He was not saying that young people should not use their elbows. He was saying that the elbow is the weapon that remains when strength, speed and flexibility have begun to fade, because it relies on structure and position rather than athleticism.

There is another reason the elbow deserves special attention. It is the natural weapon of the close range. When the distance closes and your forearms are folded between you and the other person, a punch has no room to develop. An elbow, however, is already in position. You simply turn your body and it arrives.

7.1 The Problem: No Room to Punch

At close range, punching becomes difficult. A straight punch needs about the length of your arm to reach full power. At elbow distance, there is perhaps half that, and at chest-to-chest distance there is almost none. Many people, when they find themselves this close, try to punch anyway. The punches come out short, weak and smothered, and the other person barely notices them.

Hooks and uppercuts help, because they are shorter and curved, but they still need some room. And they put your hand, with all its small, breakable bones, into contact with hard parts of the other person’s head.

What you need is a weapon that is strong, that needs very little space, and that is unlikely to break when it hits something hard. That is the elbow.

7.2 The Principle: Short Lever, Whole Body

The elbow works on a simple mechanical principle. When you punch, your fist is at the end of a long lever, your arm, and much of the force is lost along the way through the wrist, elbow and shoulder. When you strike with the elbow, the striking surface is much closer to your body. The lever is short, so very little force is lost, and the whole mass of your body is directly behind it.

This has an important consequence: elbow power comes from the body, not the arm. A good elbow strike is driven by the turning of the hips and shoulders, the dropping or rising of the body, or the step of the feet. The arm itself is just the shape that carries the elbow into position. If you try to throw an elbow with arm strength, it will be weak. If you turn your whole body behind it, it will be devastating.

The striking surface

There are several parts of the elbow and forearm that can be used for striking, and it is worth being precise.

The point of the elbow is the bony tip, the olecranon. This is the hardest and most concentrated surface, used for downward and backward elbows, and for some rising elbows.

The forearm side just below the point is broader and slightly softer. It is used for horizontal and some upward elbows, and it is more forgiving if your aim is slightly off.

The upper-arm side just above the point is used for some backward and sideways elbows, particularly when the arm is folded tightly.

As a general rule, strike with the area within a few centimetres of the point. It is the strongest and least likely to be injured.

Protecting your own head

One great advantage of the elbow is that, when thrown correctly, it protects your own head. The striking arm is folded, with the hand close to your head or ear, so the forearm and upper arm form a shield on that side. The other arm should also be up, guarding the other side. This makes the elbow one of the safest strikes to throw at close range, provided you keep your guard up.

7.3 The Six Elbows

I divide elbow strikes into six main directions. You will find different names and classifications in different schools, but these six cover almost everything.

1. The horizontal elbow (yoko mawashi hiji-ate)

The horizontal elbow travels sideways across the body, like a hook punch, with the forearm roughly parallel to the floor. It is usually aimed at the jaw, the temple or the side of the head.

To throw it, raise your arm so that your elbow is at about shoulder height, with your hand near your ear or the side of your head. Turn your hips and shoulders sharply towards the target, and let the elbow travel across in a flat arc. The striking surface is the forearm just below the point.

This is the most common and versatile elbow. It can be thrown with either arm, from either the lead or rear side, and it fits naturally after a trap that has opened the side of the head.

2. The rising elbow (tate hiji-ate or age hiji-ate)

The rising elbow travels upwards, with the elbow driving towards the chin or the underside of the jaw. It is the close-range equivalent of an uppercut.

To throw it, drop your weight slightly, then drive upwards from the legs as you lift the elbow vertically, with your hand passing close to your own ear. The striking surface is the point or the forearm just below it.

The rising elbow is especially effective when the other person’s head is coming forward or down, for example when they are trying to grab you round the waist, or when you have pulled their head down. It also comes up through the gap between their arms, which makes it hard to block.

3. The downward elbow (otoshi hiji-ate)

The downward elbow drops vertically onto the target, usually the back of the head, the back of the neck, the spine or the collarbone of someone who is bent forward. The striking surface is the point of the elbow.

To throw it, raise your elbow high, then drop your whole body weight behind it, bending your knees as the elbow falls. The power comes from gravity and the drop of the body, not from arm strength.

This elbow is mainly useful when the other person is bent over, for example after a strike to the body, after you have pulled their head down, or when they are trying to tackle you. In sparring it is almost always prohibited, because the targets are very dangerous.

4. The forward elbow (mae hiji-ate)

The forward elbow drives straight forward, with the point of the elbow leading, usually into the chest, the solar plexus or the face. It is sometimes thrown with a step, so that the whole body moves behind it.

To throw it, bend your arm fully so that your fist is near your shoulder, then step or lean forward, driving the point of the elbow straight ahead. The other hand often supports the striking arm by pressing on the fist, which adds power and stability.

This elbow appears in several kata as a supported strike, with one hand pressing behind the other. It is very powerful at chest-to-chest range.

5. The backward elbow (ushiro hiji-ate)

The backward elbow drives straight back, usually into the abdomen, ribs or face of someone behind you. The striking surface is the point.

To throw it, look over your shoulder at the target, then drive your elbow straight back as you turn slightly towards it, often with a small step backwards. The other hand can push the fist to add power.

This is mainly a defence against attacks from behind, such as bear hugs and grabs, and we will come back to it in Chapter 15. It also has uses when you have moved to the side of someone and they are slightly behind you.

6. The diagonal elbow

The diagonal elbow travels on a slant, either downwards across the body from high to low, or upwards across the body from low to high. It is a variation of the horizontal elbow and is often aimed at the collarbone, the side of the neck or the jaw.

The downward diagonal elbow, in particular, is very effective at chest-to-chest range, because it comes over the top of the other person’s arms and is hard to see.

Drill 7.1: Six-direction elbows on pads

Your partner holds a focus mitt or a Thai pad firmly against their body or in front of their face. Work through each of the six elbows in turn, ten repetitions each side, starting slowly and building to full power. Concentrate on driving each elbow with your body: turn for the horizontal, rise for the rising, drop for the downward, step for the forward, turn back for the backward.

Your partner should feel the difference between an elbow thrown with the arm and one thrown with the body. Ask them to tell you which is which.

7.4 Targets for the Elbow

Because the elbow is so hard and so powerful, the choice of target matters a great deal. Some targets are suitable for training and sparring with protection; others are dangerous and should be reserved for genuine self-defence. I will be honest about which is which.

The jaw is the classic target for horizontal and rising elbows. A strike to the side of the jaw can cause a knockout by rotating the head sharply. In training, use pads; in sparring with full head protection and appropriate rules, very light horizontal elbows to the side of the head guard can be allowed.

The temple is a very dangerous target. Elbows to the temple can cause serious injury and should never be thrown at any force in training.

The nose and face are vulnerable to forward and rising elbows. A strike here causes pain, watering eyes and a strong flinch, which makes it a good opening strike in self-defence.

The collarbone is a target for downward and diagonal elbows. It breaks relatively easily, and a broken collarbone disables the arm on that side. This is a self-defence target only.

The ribs and solar plexus are targets for forward and horizontal elbows at chest-to-chest range. They are reasonably safe targets in training if thrown with control and with body protection.

The back of the head and neck are targets for downward elbows on someone bent over. They are extremely dangerous and should never be struck in training.

The thigh can be struck with a downward elbow when someone is trying to tackle you. It is painful but rarely seriously injurious.

The arm itself is an often overlooked target. A downward or horizontal elbow into the bicep, the forearm or the inside of the elbow of an arm that is grabbing or punching you can cause pain and numbness that makes the arm useless for a few moments. This is sometimes called destruction, and it is a very practical way to deal with a grabbing hand.

Street note: elbows and the law

Elbows can cause severe injuries, including fractured skulls, broken jaws and brain injuries. In a self-defence situation in the United Kingdom, the force you use must be reasonable in the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be. An elbow to the face of someone who is attacking you with a weapon may well be reasonable. A downward elbow to the back of the neck of someone who is already on the ground and no longer a threat almost certainly is not. Know what your weapons can do, and use them only when you must.

7.5 Elbows from Traps

The elbow fits naturally into the trapping and combination work of Part Two. In fact, many traps lead more naturally to elbows than to punches, because the trap brings you close.

Palm pin to horizontal elbow

You pin the other person’s lead arm against their body with your lead hand, as in Chapter 5. Instead of striking over the pin with a rear-hand punch, you step in slightly and throw a horizontal elbow with your rear arm. Because you are already close, the elbow is the natural weapon. The pinned arm cannot rise to block it.

Hooking pull to rising elbow

You hook the other person’s arm and pull it down towards your hip. Their head comes forward and down. As it does, you drive a rising elbow up with your other arm into their face or chin. The pull and the elbow happen together, so their head moves onto the elbow just as it rises.

Elbow lift to forward elbow

You lift the other person’s arm by pushing up under their elbow, exposing their ribs. As the arm goes up, you step in and drive a forward elbow into the ribs or solar plexus with your other arm.

Cross trap to downward diagonal elbow

From the outside line, you pull the other person’s lead arm across their body, tying up both arms. You then throw a downward diagonal elbow over the top of the trapped arms into the side of the neck or the collarbone.

Kata link: Seiyunchin and Seipai

Seiyunchin contains a sequence in which one hand pulls while the other rises in a forward-and-upward movement. As a block, it makes little sense. As a pull into a rising elbow, it makes very good sense, especially from the low stance, which drops the other person’s posture.

Seipai contains elbow strikes thrown while the other hand holds or pulls, and a turning movement that can be read as a horizontal elbow followed by a throw. Both kata reward close study for elbow applications.

7.6 Elbows as Defence

The elbow is not only a weapon. It is also a very effective defensive tool, and Goju-Ryu makes great use of it in this way.

The elbow cover

When someone throws a hooking punch at your head, bringing your elbow up so that your forearm covers the side of your head, with your hand near your ear or the back of your head, creates a solid shield. The punch lands on your forearm or the point of your elbow rather than your head.

This cover has an added benefit: a punch that lands on the point of your elbow often injures the puncher’s hand. Boxers know this well, and many are cautious of hooking into a tight elbow guard.

The elbow block

The point of the elbow can also be used to block kicks and knees, particularly to the body. Dropping your elbow onto an incoming knee or kick protects your ribs and can injure the shin or foot of the attacker.

The elbow frame

When someone is pressing into you at close range, placing your elbow and forearm against their chest or collarbone creates a strong frame that stops them closing further. Because the elbow is close to your body and backed by your structure, it is much harder to collapse than an extended arm.

Drill 7.2: Cover and return

Your partner throws slow, light hooking punches at your head with gloves on, alternating sides. You cover each one with an elbow cover, keeping your forearm tight to your head. As soon as the punch lands on your cover, you return immediately with a horizontal elbow from the same arm to a pad your partner holds with their other hand.

The idea is that the cover becomes the chamber for the strike. Your arm is already folded and raised; all you need to do is turn. Build up speed gradually over several sessions.

7.7 Developing Elbow Power

Elbow power depends on three things: body mechanics, structure and conditioning.

Body mechanics means turning, rising, dropping or stepping behind the elbow. Practise each elbow slowly in front of a mirror until the movement of the body feels natural. Then practise on pads, concentrating on the body rather than the arm.

Structure means having a stable base and a connected body at the moment of impact. An elbow thrown while off balance will bounce off the target. Use the Sanchin principles from Chapter 2: grip the floor, connect through the trunk, and breathe out sharply on impact.

Conditioning means preparing the elbow and forearm for impact. Fortunately, the elbow needs much less conditioning than the hands, because it is naturally hard. Regular work on pads and a heavy bag is usually enough. Avoid striking hard, unpadded surfaces with your elbows; the joint can be injured, and there is no need.

Drill 7.3: The heavy bag elbow round

Set a round timer for two minutes. Stand close to a heavy bag, so close that you could touch it with your forehead. Throw only elbows, in any direction, for the whole round. Move around the bag, change angles, and use your other hand to hold or push the bag as if it were an opponent’s arm or head.

Rest for one minute and repeat for three to five rounds. This builds elbow power, close-range footwork and the specific endurance needed for clinch work.

7.8 Elbows in Sparring

Most karate rule sets prohibit elbow strikes, and for good reason: they are very dangerous. Even with head guards, a hard elbow can cause concussion or cuts.

However, there are safe ways to include elbows in sparring if you train in a club that allows close-range work.

The first is to use very light, controlled elbows to the body only, with body protection. This lets you practise the timing and positioning without much risk.

The second is to use touch elbows to the head guard, where the elbow is placed rather than struck. This teaches you to recognise elbow opportunities without causing harm.

The third is to use pads in sparring. One partner wears a body shield or holds a pad, and the other may throw elbows only to the pad. This is surprisingly realistic, because the pad holder is moving and resisting, but the target is safe.

In all cases, both partners must agree on the rules beforehand, and an instructor should supervise.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My elbows feel weak on the pads.”

Solution: You are almost certainly throwing them with your arm. Practise slowly with your hand placed on your ear and kept there, so that the arm cannot move independently of the body. Now the only way to deliver the elbow is to turn, rise or drop. Once the body movement feels natural, let the arm move a little more freely again.

Problem: “I keep hurting my elbow on the pads.”

Solution: You are probably hitting with the very point of the elbow at an angle, or hyperextending the joint. Use the forearm just below the point for horizontal elbows, keep the arm fully folded, and check that your pads are thick enough. If the pain persists, rest and see a physiotherapist.

Problem: “I can never get close enough to use my elbows.”

Solution: Elbows are close-range weapons, and you need to get to close range first. Go back to the bridging work in Chapter 3 and the traps in Chapter 5. Also look for moments when the other person comes close to you, such as when they rush in, grab you or try to clinch. Those are elbow moments.

Problem: “My partner covers up and my elbows just hit their arms.”

Solution: Use the traps. An elbow thrown into a guard is a waste; an elbow thrown over a trapped arm lands. Also use the elbow on the arm itself, to break the guard down, and change direction: if they cover high, a forward elbow to the body often lands.

7.9 Bringing It Together

The elbow is the close-range weapon par excellence. It needs very little room, it is hard to injure, it protects your head while you use it, and it can be thrown in six directions from almost any position. It fits naturally after the traps and combinations of Part Two, and it is a weapon that you will be able to use long after your punches and kicks have begun to fade.

Learn the six elbows. Drive them with your body. Know the targets and respect their danger. And practise them from traps, so that when the arm is pinned and the head is open, the elbow arrives without thought.

Key Points

  • The elbow is the natural weapon of the close range: strong, hard to injure, and needing very little space.
  • Elbow power comes from the body: turning, rising, dropping or stepping. The arm only carries the elbow into position.
  • Strike with the area within a few centimetres of the point of the elbow.
  • The six main elbows are horizontal, rising, downward, forward, backward and diagonal.
  • Elbows to the head, neck and collarbone are dangerous. Use pads for power training and very controlled elbows, if any, in sparring.
  • Elbows fit naturally after traps: pin to horizontal, pull to rising, lift to forward, cross trap to diagonal.
  • The elbow is also a defensive tool: as a cover against hooks, a block against knees and a frame against pressure.

Training Questions

  1. Throw ten horizontal elbows on a pad with your arm only, then ten with your whole body. Ask your partner to rate the difference on a scale of one to ten.
  2. Find three places in the kata you know where an elbow strike might be hidden. Test each against the four bunkai tests.
  3. In kakie, look for moments when your partner’s head comes within elbow range. How often does it happen? What causes it?

Hiza-geri: Knees and the Low-Line Weapons

If the elbow is the close-range weapon of the upper body, the knee is its partner below. It is the heaviest strike most people can throw, it needs almost no room, and at close range it is very hard to see coming. Alongside the knee, Goju-Ryu uses a family of low kicks, stamps and sweeps that attack the legs from distances where a high kick would be impossible. This chapter covers them all.

Goju-Ryu has a reputation as a style without much kicking. People who know only competition karate sometimes look at the kata, see the absence of high kicks, and conclude that Goju-Ryu practitioners cannot kick. They are wrong, but in an interesting way.

Goju-Ryu does kick. It just kicks low and close. The front kicks in the kata are aimed at the groin, the knee and the shin. There are stamping kicks, knee strikes, and sweeping and hooking movements of the feet. These are not the kicks of the sport fighter, who needs distance and flexibility. They are the kicks of someone who expects the other person to be close, who wants to keep their own balance, and who knows that a strike to the leg can end a fight as surely as a strike to the head.

Of all these leg techniques, the knee is the most important at close range, and it is where we begin.

8.1 The Problem: The Lower Body Is Unguarded

When people fight at close range, they instinctively protect their heads. Their hands come up, their chins tuck in, their elbows close in front of their faces. What they rarely protect is everything below the chest: the abdomen, the groin, the thighs, the knees and the shins.

This is partly instinct and partly training. Most people who have done any boxing or karate are used to defending against punches to the head, but not against knees and low kicks. And at close range the lower body is simply hard to see. Your eyes are on the other person’s face and hands, and their legs are below your field of view, hidden by the bodies between you.

At the same time, your own legs are free. While your hands are busy trapping, covering and striking, your legs are doing nothing except holding you up. A close-range fighter who can use their legs as well as their arms has twice as many weapons as one who cannot.

8.2 The Principle: The Hips Are the Engine

Just as elbow power comes from the body rather than the arm, knee power comes from the hips rather than the leg. A knee thrown by lifting the leg is weak. A knee thrown by driving the hips forward, so that the whole weight of the body goes into the strike, is extremely powerful.

To feel this, stand with your hands on a partner’s shoulders. Lift your knee towards a pad they hold against their thigh, using only your leg. Then try again, this time pulling yourself towards your partner with your hands as you drive your hips forward and up, rising onto the ball of your standing foot. The second knee will be many times stronger.

This is why the knee works so well with the clinch. When you have hold of the other person, you can pull them onto your knee while you drive your hips into it. The pull and the drive combine.

8.3 The Knee Strikes

There are four main knee strikes, each suited to different positions and targets.

The straight knee (mae hiza-geri)

The straight knee drives forward and upward into the target, usually the abdomen, the groin, the thigh or, if the head has been pulled down, the face. The point of the knee, just above the kneecap, is the striking surface.

To throw it, step or shift so that your striking leg is behind you. Drive the knee forward and up while pushing your hips forward and rising onto the ball of your standing foot. Pull the other person towards the knee with your hands if you have hold of them. Lean back very slightly to keep your balance, but not so much that you lose the forward drive.

The rising knee to the head

When the other person’s head has been pulled down, a straight knee becomes a rising knee to the face. This is one of the most devastating strikes in close combat, and one of the most dangerous. It is never used in karate sparring, and it should be practised only on pads.

The curved knee (mawashi hiza-geri)

The curved knee travels in an arc from the side, like a roundhouse kick folded up. It is usually aimed at the ribs, the side of the abdomen or the outside of the thigh.

To throw it, lift your knee out to the side, turn your hips over and drive the knee across into the target. The striking surface is the inside of the knee or the point. The curved knee goes around the other person’s guard, so it is useful when they are covering their front.

The knee to the thigh

A straight or slightly curved knee into the front or outside of the thigh, aimed at the large muscles, causes intense pain and a temporary loss of strength in the leg, known in British playgrounds as a “dead leg”. It is safe enough to practise with control and with thigh protection, and it is one of the most practical knees for self-defence because it is very effective but unlikely to cause lasting injury.

Drill 8.1: Hip drive knees

Your partner holds a Thai pad or kick shield firmly against their abdomen. Place both hands on their shoulders. Throw ten straight knees with your right leg, concentrating entirely on driving your hips forward and pulling your partner onto the knee. Then ten with the left. Then alternate legs for twenty knees, switching your stance with a small hop between each.

Your partner should feel the difference between a knee that lifts and a knee that drives. Ask them to tell you when the knee feels heavy and when it feels light.

8.4 Targets for the Knee

As with the elbow, the choice of target matters, both for effectiveness and for safety.

The thigh is the safest and one of the most effective targets. Knees to the outside of the thigh (the peroneal area) and the front of the thigh cause pain and weakness without lasting harm in most cases.

The abdomen and solar plexus are effective targets for straight knees, causing winding and doubling over. They are reasonably safe in training with body protection.

The ribs are a target for curved knees. They can be broken, so control is needed in training.

The groin is a very effective self-defence target and is never struck in training except on pads or a groin-guard target used with great care.

The face and head are targets only when the head has been pulled down. Knees to the head can cause severe injury and are used only on pads.

8.5 Low Kicks and Stamps

Beyond the knee, Goju-Ryu uses a range of low kicks that work at close and middle range. These are simple, direct and hard to see.

The low front kick (mae geri gedan)

The low front kick is a snapping or thrusting kick with the ball of the foot or the toe of the shoe, aimed at the groin, the shin or the knee. In the kata, front kicks are usually low, and at close range they are often thrown with very little chambering, the leg simply snapping up from the floor.

The stamping kick (kansetsu geri or fumikomi)

The stamping kick drives the heel or the sole of the foot downwards and forwards into the knee, the shin or the instep. Against the knee, it is extremely dangerous: a stamp to the front of the knee can hyperextend it and tear the ligaments. Against the shin or instep, it is painful and disruptive.

Stamping movements appear throughout the Goju-Ryu kata, and they are often overlooked because they look like steps. Any time you see a heavy, deliberate step forward in a kata, it is worth asking whether it might be a stamp on the other person’s foot or knee.

The shin kick

A short kick with the shin into the outside or inside of the other person’s thigh or calf, similar to a Muay Thai low kick but thrown from closer range and with less wind-up. It is very effective at disrupting balance and causing pain, and it can be practised safely with shin guards.

The heel kick and back stamp

When someone is behind you or at your side, a stamp backwards with the heel onto their foot or shin, or a short back kick into their knee, can break a hold or create space.

Kata link: the stamps of Seisan and Suparinpei

Seisan and Suparinpei both contain heavy stepping and stamping movements that are often performed as simple stance changes. Read as stamps, they become powerful leg attacks: the step lands on the other person’s foot or knee as your hands pull or strike their upper body. This combination, upper body controlled while the leg is attacked, is one of the most effective in all of close combat, because the person cannot easily move their leg away while you have hold of their upper body.

8.6 Combining Hands and Legs

The real power of knees and low kicks at close range comes from combining them with hand techniques. While the other person’s attention is on your hands, your legs can attack freely, and vice versa.

High–low

The simplest combination is high–low: strike or threaten the head, and as the other person covers, attack the legs or body. A back fist to the face, followed instantly by a knee to the thigh, is a classic close-range combination. The back fist brings their hands and attention up; the knee lands below.

Low–high

The reverse also works: attack the legs or body first, and as the other person drops their hands or bends forward, attack the head. A knee to the abdomen bends them forward, and a downward elbow or a rising knee to the head follows. A stamp on the foot pins them in place, and a strike to the head lands while they cannot step back.

Pull and knee

The strongest knee combinations involve pulling. When you have hold of the other person’s arm, collar, neck or shoulders, you pull them onto the knee as you drive it. This combines their forward movement with yours, and it also stops them stepping away.

Trap and kick

A trap that ties up the other person’s arms also ties up their attention. While they are struggling to free their arm, a low kick to the shin or a knee to the thigh is very hard for them to notice or defend.

Drill 8.2: High–low at the pads

Your partner wears focus mitts on their hands and a thigh pad strapped to the outside of one thigh. They hold the mitts in front of their face like a guard. You throw a back fist to one mitt, then immediately drive a knee into the thigh pad. Then a palm heel to the other mitt and a shin kick to the thigh pad. Then a knee to the thigh pad and a hook to a mitt.

Twenty combinations, alternating sides. The aim is to make the hand and the leg feel like a single action, with no pause between them.

8.7 Defending Against Knees and Low Kicks

If knees and low kicks are so effective, you need to be able to defend against them too. Fortunately, the defences are relatively simple, and they are worth practising alongside the attacks.

Distance and posture

The best defence against a knee is to keep your hips back slightly and your elbows down, so that there is a little space between your body and the other person’s. A knee needs the other person’s hips to be close to yours. If you keep a slight gap and a forearm frame, their knees have little room to develop.

The elbow block

As mentioned in Chapter 7, dropping your elbow onto an incoming knee protects your ribs and abdomen and can injure the attacker’s leg. Keep your elbows tight to your body in the clinch, and drop them sharply as a knee comes in.

The leg check

Against a low kick or a curved knee to the thigh, lifting your own knee so that your shin meets their kick is the standard defence. It hurts them as much as it hurts you, and it stops the kick reaching your thigh. At close range, a small lift of the knee is usually enough.

The jam

Against a straight knee, placing your hand or forearm on the other person’s hip as they start to drive it in can stop the knee before it develops, because the hip is the engine. Push the hip back and the knee goes nowhere.

Moving the target

Turning your hips so that a knee meets the side of your thigh rather than your groin or abdomen, or stepping slightly to the side so it misses, are simple and effective defences. Your Sanchin stance, with its slightly inward-turned knees, naturally protects the groin.

8.8 Balance and the Knee

There is one problem with knees and kicks that every close-range fighter must reckon with: whenever you strike with your leg, you are standing on one leg. That makes you vulnerable to being pushed, pulled or swept.

At long range this is less of a problem, because the other person cannot reach you. At close range it is a real danger. If you throw a knee and the other person pushes you as your leg is in the air, you will fall over.

There are three ways to manage this.

First, hold onto them. When you throw a knee from the clinch, your hands are holding the other person, and their body helps support you. They cannot easily push you because you are attached to them.

Second, keep it short. A knee or low kick at close range should be fast and should return quickly to the floor. The longer your leg is in the air, the longer you are vulnerable.

Third, strike when they cannot respond. Throw your knees when the other person’s arms are trapped, when they are off balance, or when they are reacting to a hand strike. If they are busy, they cannot push you.

Fault: leaning back on the knee

Many people, when they throw a knee, lean back to keep their balance. This takes all the power out of the strike, because the hips go back instead of forward, and it also puts them off balance.

Correction: Keep your upper body upright or leaning very slightly forward, and drive your hips into the knee. Use your hands on your partner to stabilise you. If you find yourself falling backwards, you are leaning back.

8.9 Knees and Low Kicks in Sparring

Most karate rule sets prohibit knees and low kicks, although some allow foot sweeps and low kicks to the thigh. If your club allows close-range sparring, knees to the thigh with thigh protection, and low kicks with shin guards, are relatively safe to include. Knees to the body should be very light and used only with body protection. Knees to the head, stamps to the knee and kicks to the groin should never be used in sparring.

A useful sparring game is to allow only touch knees: a knee that touches the opponent’s thigh or body scores, but must not strike. This teaches the timing and positioning of knees without the risk of injury.

Drill 8.3: Touch-knee clinch rounds

Partners start in a light clinch, each with one hand behind the other’s neck and one hand on the other’s arm. On the signal, each tries to touch the other’s thigh with their knee while preventing their partner doing the same. Touches only, no strikes. One-minute rounds.

You will quickly discover the value of posture, hip position and control of the other person’s upper body, and you will learn to feel when a knee is coming.

Street note

On the street, the knee to the thigh is one of the most useful techniques you can have. It is very effective, it is hard to see, and it is unlikely to cause the kind of lasting injury that would be difficult to justify legally. A couple of hard knees to the thigh can take the fight out of the other person’s legs, making it hard for them to chase you as you leave. Knees to the groin and head, and stamps to the knee, are much more dangerous and should be reserved for situations where you genuinely fear serious harm.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My knees have no power.”

Solution: You are lifting your leg rather than driving your hips. Practise Drill 8.1 with your hands on your partner’s shoulders, focusing entirely on the hips. Rise onto the ball of your standing foot. Pull your partner towards you as you drive. If you still feel weak, practise standing knee drives against a wall: hands on the wall, drive your knee up and your hips forward towards the wall, twenty times each side.

Problem: “I keep losing my balance when I throw knees.”

Solution: You are leaning back or throwing from too far away. Get closer, hold onto your partner, and keep your upper body upright. Also check your standing foot: it should be turned slightly outward and planted firmly, with the heel lifting only as you drive.

Problem: “My partner keeps catching my low kicks.”

Solution: Your kicks are too slow or too high, or you are throwing them without setting them up. Keep low kicks very low, below the knee or into the lower thigh, so they are hard to catch. Throw them immediately after a hand technique, when your partner’s attention is high. And return your leg to the floor as fast as you threw it.

Problem: “I can never see the knees coming in the clinch.”

Solution: You will rarely see them. Learn to feel them instead. When the other person’s hips move towards you, a knee is coming. Keep a hand or forearm near their hip so you can feel the movement and jam it. Drill 8.3 develops exactly this sensitivity.

8.10 Bringing It Together

Knees and low kicks give you a second set of weapons at close range, aimed at targets the other person is rarely protecting. Combined with hand techniques, they create combinations that are very hard to defend, because the other person cannot protect high and low at once.

Like the elbow, the knee gets its power from the body, specifically the drive of the hips. Like the elbow, it is most effective after a trap or from the clinch. And like the elbow, it must be treated with respect in training, because it can cause serious injury.

The next chapter brings elbows, knees and the hands together in the clinch, where the two bodies are locked together and the fight is decided by control of the head, the arms and the posture.

Key Points

  • At close range, the lower body is usually unguarded and hard to see. Attack it.
  • Knee power comes from driving the hips, not lifting the leg. Pull the other person onto the knee.
  • The four main knees are the straight knee, the rising knee to the head, the curved knee and the knee to the thigh.
  • Goju-Ryu low kicks include the low front kick, the stamp, the shin kick and the back stamp. Stamps are hidden throughout the kata.
  • Combine hands and legs: high–low, low–high, pull and knee, trap and kick.
  • Defend knees with posture and distance, the elbow drop, the leg check, the hip jam and moving the target.
  • Manage balance by holding on, keeping strikes short, and striking when the other person is busy.

Training Questions

  1. Look through the kata you know for every step that could be a stamp. How many did you find? Which seem most plausible?
  2. Throw ten knees to a pad with your hands at your sides, then ten with your hands on your partner’s shoulders. What difference do the hands make?
  3. During kakie or clinch work, notice how often your partner’s thigh is within range of your knee. What are you usually doing with your legs at those moments?

The Clinch: Head Control, Posture and the Short Weapons Combined

Sooner or later, close-range exchanges become clinches. The bodies come together, hands grab necks, arms and clothing, and the fight becomes a contest of posture and control. Whoever controls the head and the arms in the clinch usually controls the fight. This chapter shows you how to win that contest, how to use elbows and knees from it, how to deal with walls, and how to get out of a clinch that is going badly.

A friend of mine who works door security in a busy city-centre venue once told me that in fifteen years he had never seen a fight that did not end up in some kind of clinch. “People think it’ll be like the films,” he said. “Two blokes squaring up and trading punches. It never is. Two or three swings and they’re hanging onto each other, pulling each other’s shirts over their heads and falling over chairs.”

That has been my experience too, both on the mat and in the few real incidents I have witnessed. The clinch is not an unusual situation that you might occasionally find yourself in. It is where most fights go, and where many of them are decided.

The good news is that Goju-Ryu prepares you for it extremely well, provided you train its close-range material properly. Sanchin gives you the structure to resist being pulled and pushed. Kakie gives you the sensitivity to feel what the other person is doing. Trapping gives you the ability to control their arms. And the elbows and knees give you the weapons to finish the exchange.

9.1 The Problem: Being Controlled

In the clinch, the biggest danger is being controlled: having your head pulled down, your arms tied up, your posture broken and your balance taken. Once you are controlled, the other person can knee you, elbow you, throw you or hold you while their friends hit you.

The most common way to be controlled is through the head. Where the head goes, the body follows. If someone pulls your head down, your spine bends, your hips go back, your hands drop to protect your face, and your whole body is broken forward. In that position you cannot strike effectively, you cannot see, and you are wide open to knees to the face.

The second most common way is through the arms. If someone ties up your arms, by trapping them, gripping them or pinning them against your body, you cannot strike or defend, and they have free hands or knees to use.

The third is through posture and balance. If you are bent forward, leaning back or standing with your feet in a line, you can be thrown, pushed or swept.

9.2 The Principle: Head, Arms, Hips

The clinch is a contest for three things, in order of importance: the head, the arms and the hips.

Control of the head

If you control the other person’s head, you control their body. A hand on the back of their head or neck, pulling it down or to one side, breaks their posture and opens them to knees and elbows. A forearm across their jaw or neck pushes their head away and turns their body.

Equally, you must protect your own head. Keep your chin down, your neck strong and your head up, so that your spine stays straight and your posture stays strong. If someone grabs your head, the first priority is to straighten up again.

Control of the arms

The person who has the inside position on the arms usually controls the clinch. If your hands and forearms are inside theirs, closer to their body and neck, you can reach their head and push their arms away. If theirs are inside yours, they can reach your head and you cannot reach theirs.

The battle for the inside position is constant in the clinch. Each person is trying to get their arms inside, using swimming movements in which one arm circles inside the other person’s arm. This is very similar to the double-arm kakie in Chapter 4, and if you have practised that, you will find the clinch much more familiar.

Control of the hips

The hips are the link to throws and takedowns. If your hips are close to the other person’s and slightly lower, you are in a strong position to throw them. If their hips are close to yours, you are in danger. In the clinch, you usually want to keep your hips slightly back, out of their reach, unless you are about to throw.

9.3 The Main Clinch Positions

There are many clinch positions, but a few appear again and again. Each has particular strengths and weaknesses.

The double collar tie

Both your hands are behind the other person’s head or neck, with your forearms on their collarbones and your elbows close together. This is the classic Muay Thai clinch, and it gives you very strong control of the head. From here you can pull the head down into knees, turn the person from side to side to break their balance, and frame them away with your forearms.

To make it strong, keep your elbows close together and press your forearms into the other person’s collarbones. Place one hand on top of the other behind their head, not with fingers interlocked, which risks injury to your fingers. Pull down with your body weight, not with your arms.

The collar and elbow

One hand is behind the other person’s neck and the other is on their arm, usually just above the elbow. This is the most common position in many grappling arts, and it is very versatile. You can pull the head down, control the arm, and move between strikes and throws.

Underhooks

One or both of your arms are under the other person’s arms, around their body. Underhooks give strong control of the upper body and the hips, and they are the basis for many throws. However, they leave your head somewhat exposed, because your arms are low.

Overhooks

One or both of your arms are over the other person’s arms, wrapping them. Overhooks control the arms well and can be used to apply joint pressure, but they give less control of the body than underhooks.

The arm tie

You have hold of one or both of the other person’s arms, at the wrist, forearm or elbow, controlling them without any contact with the head or body. This is less a clinch than an extended trap, but it often leads into one.

9.4 Strikes from the Clinch

The clinch is not a resting place. It is a position from which to strike, throw or escape. Here are the main strikes available from each position.

From the double collar tie, the main weapons are knees, especially to the body and thighs, and, if you pull the head down, to the face. You can also turn the head and throw a horizontal elbow by releasing one hand.

From the collar and elbow, you can throw knees to the thigh and body, short hooks and uppercuts with the hand on the arm, and elbows by releasing the collar hand.

From underhooks, the main weapons are knees, particularly to the thighs, and headbutts. Throws are often more natural than strikes from here.

From overhooks, you can strike with the free hand while the overhook ties up the other person’s arm, and throw knees.

From the arm tie, your free hand can strike while the tie controls their arm, and knees are available.

Drill 9.1: Clinch strike rotation

Your partner wears a body shield or holds a Thai pad against their thigh. Take each clinch position in turn, and from each, throw five strikes of your choice into the pad. Your partner moves and turns slightly, so that you have to keep adjusting your control. Then swap roles.

After a few rounds, your partner may begin to resist gently, trying to break your posture or improve their position. You must keep control while striking.

9.5 The Headbutt

The headbutt deserves a short section of its own, because it is one of the most effective and least discussed weapons in close combat. It is not found in most karate syllabi, but it is undeniably part of real violence, and anyone who trains for self-defence should at least understand it.

The headbutt uses the top of the forehead, just at the hairline, to strike the other person’s face, especially the nose and the cheekbones. It is driven by the whole body, usually by pulling the other person’s head towards you while driving your forehead forward with your legs.

The danger with the headbutt is that you can hurt yourself badly if you strike with the wrong part of your head or hit a hard part of theirs. Striking with the face, the temple or the top of the skull, or hitting their forehead with yours, can cause serious injury to you.

In training, headbutts should be practised only on pads, slowly, to learn the movement. In sparring they are never used. In self-defence, they are a serious use of force and must be justified accordingly.

The headbutt is also important to understand defensively. In the clinch, keep your head close to the other person’s head, with your forehead against their cheek or the side of their head. This takes away their space for a headbutt and also makes it hard for them to pull your head down.

9.6 Posture: Staying Upright

Whatever position you are in, the most important rule of the clinch is: keep your posture. A straight spine, a strong neck and a head that stays up and over your hips make you very hard to control. A bent spine and a lowered head make you very easy to control.

If your head is pulled down, the priority is to straighten up immediately. There are three ways to do this.

Drive the head up. Press your head up into the other person’s hands or chest while straightening your back. Use your legs, not your neck. This works if you catch it early.

Frame and lift. Place both your forearms against the other person’s chest or collarbones and push them away as you lift your head. This creates space and breaks their grip.

Swim inside. Bring one of your arms inside theirs and up to their head or neck. This breaks their grip on your head and gives you inside control.

Fault: fighting the grip with the neck

When someone grabs the back of your head, the instinctive reaction is to try to lift your head with your neck muscles. This rarely works against a strong person, and it can strain your neck.

Correction: Use your legs and your arms. Straighten your back by pushing through your legs, frame with your forearms, and swim inside to break the grip. Your neck should be strong and still, not fighting.

9.7 The Wall

I promised in Chapters 2 and 3 that we would look at wall work, and the clinch is where it belongs. Walls change everything about the clinch.

When your back is to the wall

Your back against a wall takes away your ability to step back and makes it easy for the other person to pin you. It also, however, stops you being thrown or pushed over backwards, and gives you something solid to push off.

The danger is being pinned square-on, with the other person pressing into you and striking freely. Your priorities are:

  1. Protect your head, with your forearms up and your elbows in.
  2. Get an underhook or a frame, so that you have some control of their upper body.
  3. Turn, so that your shoulder rather than your back is against the wall.
  4. Move along the wall, at an angle, using the wall to push off. Never try to push straight out.
  5. Reverse the position if you can, by turning so that their back is to the wall.

When their back is to the wall

If you have pressed the other person against a wall, you are in a strong position. They cannot step back, their strikes are cramped, and your knees and elbows have a solid backstop. Pin them with your forearm across their collarbones or with a hand on their chest, keep your hips back so they cannot grab your waist, and strike.

However, be careful. Someone pinned against a wall may panic and fight very hard, and a good fighter will try to turn and reverse the position. Do not let them get an underhook.

Drill 9.2: Off the wall

One partner stands with their back to a padded wall or a mat stood against a wall. The other partner presses them with a light collar-and-elbow clinch. On the signal, the person on the wall tries to turn and move along the wall to escape, or to reverse the position so that their partner’s back is to the wall. The presser tries to keep them pinned.

No strikes. Thirty-second rounds. Swap roles after each round. This is exhausting and very instructive, and it teaches you more about real-world clinch work than almost any other drill.

9.8 Getting Out of the Clinch

On the street, you usually want to get out of the clinch as quickly as possible, because the clinch makes it hard to escape and easy for other people to join in. In sparring, you might want to leave a clinch that is going badly.

The three methods from Chapter 3 all apply: push and pivot, frame and step, strike and step. In the clinch, a fourth method is often useful: break the grip and turn. If the other person has hold of your neck or arm, break the grip, usually by attacking the thumb side of their grip or by swimming your arm inside, and then turn away from them at an angle as their grip releases.

Whatever method you use, do not simply let go and step back. The other person will usually follow you and strike as you separate. Leave behind a strike, a frame or a push, so that they are busy dealing with that as you go.

Kata link: Seipai and Kururunfa

Seipai contains a movement in which both hands rise up and then drive down while the body turns, which makes very good sense as a way of breaking a double collar tie and turning out of it. Kururunfa contains a sequence in which the arms circle and press while the body drops, which can be read as breaking a clinch and turning the other person’s posture. Both kata reward study for clinch applications.

9.9 The Clinch in Sparring

Most karate rule sets break up the clinch quickly, but some, particularly in knockdown and mixed-rules karate, allow limited clinching. If your club allows close-range sparring, clinch work should be introduced carefully, with clear rules.

A good starting point is positional clinch sparring: no strikes, only grips, posture and position. The aim is to get inside control and break the other person’s posture, or to pin them against a wall. Once this is comfortable, add touch knees to the thighs and body. Only later, and with full protection and supervision, add light strikes.

Street note: never stay

On the street, the clinch is a dangerous place to stay. It ties you to one person while others may be joining in, it makes it easy to be taken to the ground, and it makes escape difficult. Use the clinch to strike, to control briefly, and to create an exit. Then leave. If you find yourself in a clinch and you do not know what to do, frame, strike, break the grip and turn away.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “Bigger people just pull my head down and I can’t get back up.”

Solution: You are probably letting them get inside control, and then trying to lift your head with your neck. Fight for the inside position early, swimming your arms inside theirs before they settle their grip. If your head is pulled down, frame with your forearms and straighten with your legs. And keep your forehead pressed against their cheek or chest, so they cannot pull your head down in the first place.

Problem: “In the clinch I just end up hugging and doing nothing.”

Solution: You have no plan. Before each clinch round, choose one strike, one throw and one escape that you will look for. As soon as you feel an opportunity for any of them, take it. The clinch should always be leading somewhere.

Problem: “I get pinned against the wall and can’t move.”

Solution: Practise Drill 9.2 regularly. Remember the priorities: protect, underhook, turn, move along the wall, reverse. Never push straight out. And try not to get there in the first place, by watching your position relative to walls before the clinch begins.

Problem: “My partner keeps throwing me from the clinch.”

Solution: Your hips are too close to theirs, or your posture is too upright and stiff. Keep your hips slightly back and your knees bent, so they cannot get under your centre of gravity. The next part of the book covers throws in detail; studying them will help you see what your partner is doing and how to defend against it.

9.10 Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Three. We have looked at the elbows and knees, the short weapons of close combat, and at the clinch, where they are most often used.

The clinch is a contest for the head, the arms and the hips. Whoever controls them controls the fight. Keep your posture, fight for the inside position, protect your head, and use the clinch as a platform for strikes, throws and escapes rather than a place to rest.

In Part Four, we turn to the throws and takedowns that emerge so naturally from the clinch, and to the skills of falling safely and getting back up.

Key Points

  • Most real fights end up in some kind of clinch. Train for it.
  • The clinch is a contest for the head, the arms and the hips, in that order.
  • Keep your own posture straight and strong. Where the head goes, the body follows.
  • Fight for inside control of the arms, using swimming movements like double-arm kakie.
  • The main clinch positions are the double collar tie, the collar and elbow, underhooks, overhooks and the arm tie. Each offers different strikes.
  • Against a wall, protect, underhook, turn, move along the wall and reverse. Never push straight out.
  • On the street, use the clinch to strike and create an exit, then leave.

  2. Try Drill 9.2 with your eyes closed (with a trusted partner and a padded wall). What do you feel that you did not notice with your eyes open?
  3. Watch footage of a Muay Thai bout and a judo match. How does each sport use the clinch differently? What could Goju-Ryu borrow from each?

Kuzushi: The Art of Breaking Balance

No throw works on a person who is balanced, rooted and ready. Every successful throw begins with a moment in which the other person’s balance has been broken, however slightly, so that they cannot resist. The Japanese call this kuzushi. This chapter explains where balance comes from, how to take it away, and how the pushes, pulls and turns hidden in the Goju-Ryu kata do exactly that.

When I first started trying to throw people, I made the mistake almost everyone makes. I treated the throw as a feat of strength. I grabbed hold, turned, heaved and hoped. Against smaller or less experienced partners it sometimes worked, in an ugly sort of way. Against anyone bigger or more experienced, it failed completely. They simply stood there, solid as a tree, while I strained against them.

The person who finally showed me what I was doing wrong was a judo coach in his sixties who weighed perhaps sixty-five kilograms. He let me grab hold of him and try my best throw. Nothing happened. Then he took hold of me, and without any apparent effort, I was on my back looking at the ceiling. “You tried to throw me while I was standing,” he said. “I threw you while you were falling. You just didn’t know you were falling yet.”

That is kuzushi. The throw itself is almost an afterthought. The real work is done in the moment before, when the other person’s balance is taken away.

10.1 The Problem: People Resist

The fundamental problem with throwing someone is that they do not want to be thrown. If you try to throw a balanced person, they will resist with their whole body, and unless you are much stronger than them, you will fail.

Worse, a failed throw at close range is dangerous. You are tangled up with the other person, often with your back partly turned to them, and your own balance is compromised by the effort. A good opponent will use your failed throw to counter-throw you.

The answer is not to try harder. It is to throw at the moment when they cannot resist, because their balance has been broken and their body is busy trying to recover it.

10.2 The Principle: The Base and the Centre

To understand how balance is broken, you need two simple ideas: the base and the centre of gravity.

The base

Your base is the area on the floor enclosed by your feet. If you stand with your feet together, your base is small. If you stand with your feet apart, it is larger. The base is not just the area under your feet; it is the whole area between them.

The centre of gravity

Your centre of gravity is the point at which your body’s weight is concentrated. For most people standing upright, it lies roughly behind the navel, in the lower abdomen. When you move, bend or lean, your centre of gravity moves too.

Balance and its loss

You are balanced when your centre of gravity is directly above your base. The further it is from the edges of your base, the more stable you are. When your centre of gravity moves outside your base, you begin to fall, and you must either step to create a new base under it or go down.

Breaking someone’s balance means moving their centre of gravity towards the edge of their base, or moving their base out from under their centre of gravity. Either way, they are forced to react, and while they are reacting they cannot resist your throw.

10.3 The Eight Directions

Judo teaches that a person’s balance can be broken in eight directions: forward, backward, left, right and the four diagonals between them. This is a useful framework for karateka too.

The important insight is that a person in a particular stance is strong in some directions and weak in others. This is the triangle idea from Chapter 3. A person standing with one foot forward is strong along the line of their feet, forwards and backwards along that line. They are weak across that line, towards the third corner of the triangle.

In practice, for a person in a typical fighting stance with the left foot forward, the weakest directions are usually diagonally forward to their right, past their lead foot, and diagonally backward to their left, behind their lead foot. These are the directions in which there is no foot to catch them.

10.4 How to Break Balance

There are four basic ways to break someone’s balance. Most real throws use a combination of them.

Pulling

Pulling the other person towards you moves their centre of gravity forward. If you pull them in a direction where there is no foot to catch them, they will lose balance forward.

Pulls are most effective when they are sudden and when they follow a push. If someone is pushing towards you, a pull in the same direction adds to their own momentum and draws them off balance very easily.

The key to pulling is to use your body, not your arms. Step back or turn as you pull, so that your weight goes into it. A pull with the arms alone is weak and easily resisted.

Pushing

Pushing the other person away from you moves their centre of gravity backward. Like pulling, pushing is most effective when it is sudden, when it follows a pull, and when it is directed towards a weak direction.

A push directly into someone’s chest along the line of their feet will usually be resisted. A push diagonally across their stance, towards the point behind their lead foot, is much harder to resist.

Turning

Turning the other person, by twisting their shoulders or head, moves their centre of gravity sideways and disrupts their base, because their feet do not turn as quickly as their upper body. A turn of the head is particularly effective, because where the head goes the body follows.

Lifting and lowering

Lifting the other person, even slightly, takes weight off their feet and makes them much easier to move. Lowering them, by pulling down on their head or arms, bends them forward and puts their centre of gravity in front of their feet.

Both lifting and lowering work well with pulling and pushing. A pull that also lifts is much more effective than a pull alone. A push that also lowers can collapse someone’s posture completely.

10.5 Action and Reaction Again

In Chapter 6 we saw how every action causes a reaction, and how combinations exploit those reactions. The same principle is at the heart of kuzushi.

When you push someone, they push back. When you pull them, they pull back. When you push their head down, they lift it. These reactions are almost automatic, and they are the key to breaking balance against a resisting opponent.

The classic method is the push–pull: push the other person, wait for them to push back, and then pull suddenly in the same direction as their push. Their own force, combined with your pull, takes them off balance far more easily than any pull on its own could.

The reverse, pull–push, works just as well. Pull them towards you, feel them resist by leaning back, and then push suddenly in the direction they are already leaning.

Drill 10.1: Push–pull

Stand facing a partner, each with hands on the other’s shoulders or upper arms. Push your partner gently backwards. Ask them to resist by pushing back. The moment you feel them push back, step back and turn slightly, pulling them forward in the direction they are pushing. They should stumble forward.

Then reverse: pull them gently, feel them lean back, and push suddenly as they lean. They should stumble backward.

Do this slowly at first, with your partner cooperating. Over several sessions, ask your partner to resist more naturally, and try to feel the exact moment their reaction begins. That moment is the moment to act.

10.6 Kuzushi Through the Arms and Head

At close range, you usually break balance through the points of contact you have with the other person: their arms, their head, their shoulders or their clothing.

Through the arms

Pulling on the other person’s arm, particularly at the wrist or forearm, draws their shoulder forward and turns their body. If you pull down as well as towards you, their posture bends forward. This is the hooking pull from Chapter 5, used now for balance rather than for opening a target.

Pushing on the other person’s elbow, particularly upwards and across their body, turns them away from you and lifts their weight onto one foot. This is the elbow lift from Chapter 5.

Through the head

The head is the most powerful handle on the body. Turning someone’s head turns their whole body. Pulling it down bends them forward. Pushing it back bends them backward.

A palm on the chin, pushing up and back, is one of the most effective balance-breaks in close combat. It tips the head back, which arches the spine and moves the centre of gravity behind the heels. Combined with a leg sweep or a step behind the other person, it becomes a powerful throw.

A hand on the back of the head, pulling down and towards you, breaks the posture forward. Combined with a knee to the body or face, or with a step and turn, it is equally powerful.

Through the shoulders

Pushing one shoulder while pulling the other turns the other person, as if you were turning a steering wheel. This is very effective for moving them in a circle, which disrupts their footwork and often leaves their feet crossed or in a line.

10.7 Kuzushi Through the Legs

Balance can also be broken by attacking the base directly: moving, blocking or striking the other person’s legs.

Blocking a foot stops the other person from stepping to recover their balance. If you have pulled them forward and they try to step forward to catch themselves, a foot placed in front of theirs means they cannot step, and they fall.

Sweeping a foot moves their base out from under them. A sweep is most effective when the foot being swept is carrying little weight, for example as they step, or when their weight has been shifted to the other foot by a pull or push.

Striking a leg, for example with a knee to the thigh or a low kick, causes the leg to buckle or withdraw, which takes away part of their base.

Stepping behind the other person, with your leg behind theirs, means that if they move backward they will trip over your leg. This is the basis of many of the most effective throws.

10.8 Timing: The Moment of Weakness

The best moment to break someone’s balance is when they are already moving. A person standing still with their weight evenly distributed is at their most stable. A person in the middle of a step, a strike or a reaction is much less stable.

There are several moments of weakness worth looking for.

Mid-step. When the other person steps, their weight is briefly on one foot. A push or pull at that moment, towards the foot that is moving, is very hard to resist.

Mid-strike. When they throw a punch or kick, their weight is committed in the direction of the strike. A pull in that direction carries them past their own balance point.

During a reaction. When they react to one of your actions, by flinching, pushing back or pulling away, their balance is committed to that reaction.

During a recovery. When they have been slightly unbalanced and are trying to recover, they are vulnerable to being unbalanced again in a different direction.

Learning to feel these moments is a matter of sensitivity, and once again kakie is the foundation. In kakie you are constantly feeling your partner’s pressure and timing. The same sense, applied to the whole body, tells you when their balance is vulnerable.

Kata link: the turns of Seipai and Shisochin

Many Goju-Ryu kata contain turns of ninety or one hundred and eighty degrees, often combined with grabbing or pulling hand movements. These turns are often taught as turning to face a new attacker. Read as close-range applications, they are frequently balance-breaks: you have hold of the other person, and as you turn, your body weight pulls them in a circle, breaking their balance and swinging them off their feet. Shisochin’s opening movements and the turning sequences of Seipai are good places to explore this.

10.9 Kuzushi and Striking

Balance-breaking is not only for throws. It is equally useful for strikes.

A person whose balance is broken cannot strike effectively, because they need their legs for recovery rather than for generating power. They also cannot defend well, because their arms are often reaching out to catch themselves. A strike landed on someone who is off balance is much more effective than one landed on someone who is balanced, because they cannot absorb it with their structure.

This is why so many of the combinations in this book involve pulling or pushing before striking. The pull breaks the balance; the strike lands on someone who cannot defend or absorb it.

Drill 10.2: Pull and strike

Your partner holds a focus mitt near their face with one hand and offers you their other arm. Hook their arm and pull suddenly, stepping back and turning as you do. As they stumble forward, strike the mitt with your free hand.

Then the same with a push: push their shoulder suddenly, and as they stumble backward, follow and strike the mitt.

Do ten each way, on both sides. Notice how much harder you can strike when your partner is off balance, and how little they can do to stop it.

10.10 Protecting Your Own Balance

If you can break the other person’s balance, they can break yours. Here are the principles for protecting it.

Keep your base wide enough. A stance that is too narrow, or with the feet in a line, is easy to break. Sanchin dachi, with its slightly staggered feet and moderate width, is a good balance between stability and mobility.

Keep your centre low. Bent knees lower your centre of gravity and make you harder to move.

Keep your posture upright. A bent spine or a forward head moves your centre of gravity towards the edge of your base.

Do not over-commit. Every time you push, pull or strike hard, you commit your balance in that direction. If the other person is skilled, they will use that commitment against you. Keep your actions short and controlled.

Move your feet. When your balance is threatened, the simplest answer is to step and create a new base under your centre of gravity. Small, quick steps are better than big ones.

Do not fight the force; redirect it. If someone is pulling you strongly, do not pull back. Step towards them, turn, and redirect their pull past you. This is the essence of the soft side of Goju-Ryu.

Fault: resisting with stiffness

When their balance is threatened, many people go stiff, trying to resist with muscle. This makes them easier to throw, not harder, because a stiff body moves as a single block, and a single block is easy to tip over.

Correction: When you feel your balance going, relax and move your feet. A relaxed body can adapt; a stiff one cannot.

Street note: uneven ground

On the street, balance is affected by the ground: wet pavements, ice, gravel, kerbs, steps and debris. A small push near a kerb can send someone sprawling into the road. A pull on ice can bring two people down together. Be very aware of the ground beneath you, and take it into account both for your own balance and for anyone you might need to unbalance. Throwing someone onto concrete can cause catastrophic head injuries, which is one reason we will discuss control and responsibility so carefully in the next chapter.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My partner just stands there and I can’t move them.”

Solution: You are trying to move a balanced person directly. Use the push–pull or pull–push principle: provoke a reaction first, then use it. Also check the direction of your force. If you are pushing or pulling along the line of their feet, you are working against their strongest direction. Go across it.

Problem: “When I pull, my partner just pulls back harder and I end up being pulled myself.”

Solution: You are pulling with your arms and not committing your body. Step back and turn as you pull, so that your weight goes into it. And if they pull back hard, use it: go with their pull, step in, and push them in the direction they are leaning.

Problem: “I can feel when my partner is off balance but by the time I act they’ve recovered.”

Solution: Your timing is slightly late, which is very normal at this stage. Work on Drill 10.1 slowly, and try to act at the very beginning of your partner’s reaction rather than when it is fully developed. Over time, you will start to feel the reaction before it happens.

Problem: “I’m always the one who gets thrown.”

Solution: Look at your own posture and base. Most people who are thrown frequently have their feet too close together, their knees too straight, or their head too far forward. Practise the protection principles above, and spend time in the pushing square from Chapter 2.

10.11 Bringing It Together

Kuzushi is the hidden foundation of every throw and many strikes. Without it, throws become contests of strength, which the stronger person usually wins. With it, a smaller person can throw a larger one with surprising ease.

The principles are simple. Balance depends on the relationship between the centre of gravity and the base. You can break it by pulling, pushing, turning, lifting or lowering, through the arms, the head, the shoulders or the legs. You do it best by using the other person’s own reactions and by acting at the moment when they are already moving.

The next chapter uses these principles to build the throws themselves, drawing on the rich supply of throwing techniques hidden in the Goju-Ryu kata.

Key Points

  • Every successful throw begins with kuzushi: breaking the other person’s balance.
  • Balance depends on keeping the centre of gravity above the base. Break it by moving one away from the other.
  • A person in a stance is strong along the line of their feet and weak across it.
  • Break balance by pulling, pushing, turning, lifting and lowering, using the body rather than the arms.
  • Use reactions: push–pull and pull–push are far more effective than a single pull or push.
  • The head is the most powerful handle on the body. Where the head goes, the body follows.
  • The best moments to break balance are mid-step, mid-strike, during a reaction and during a recovery.
  • Protect your own balance with a sound base, a low centre, an upright posture and relaxed, quick feet.

Training Questions

  1. Stand on one leg and ask a partner to push you very gently in eight directions. Which directions are hardest to resist? Now stand in sanchin dachi and repeat. What changed?
  2. In kakie, try to feel the moment your partner’s balance shifts. Describe what you feel in a few words.
  3. Find three turns in the kata you know that could be read as balance-breaks. Test them with a partner.

Nage-waza: The Throws Hidden in the Kata

Goju-Ryu is not usually thought of as a throwing art, yet its kata contain a remarkable number of throws and takedowns. They are hidden inside movements that look like blocks, turns and changes of stance, and they make sense only when you know that the other person is already in your hands. This chapter brings out the most practical of them, shows you how to perform them safely, and explains how each grows from the balance-breaking principles of the previous chapter.

The first time someone showed me a throw from a Goju-Ryu kata, I was sceptical. I had performed Seiyunchin hundreds of times and never once thought of it as anything but a series of low-stance blocks and strikes. Then a visiting instructor took my arm, dropped into the low straddle stance from the kata, slipped his shoulder under my body and lifted me across his back. I landed on the mat with a thump and a new respect for the people who designed the form.

Since then, I have found throws in every one of the kaishu kata. Some are obvious once you see them; others take years to recognise. Not all the interpretations I have been shown pass the four bunkai tests from Chapter 1, but many do, and the best of them are as effective as anything taught in judo or wrestling.

Before we begin, a word of warning. Throws are dangerous. Throwing a training partner onto a hard floor, or throwing someone who does not know how to fall, can cause serious injury, particularly to the head, neck and shoulders. Do not practise any throw in this chapter until both you and your partner can breakfall safely, which is covered in Chapter 12. If you have not yet learned to breakfall, read Chapter 12 first and practise its drills for several weeks before attempting these throws. And always practise on proper mats.

11.1 The Problem: Ending Up on the Floor

The problem throws solve is simple: you want the other person on the floor and yourself on your feet. On the street, a person on the floor cannot easily chase you, and you have time to escape. In sparring rules that allow throws, a clean throw scores and often ends the exchange.

But throws have their own problem: if they fail, you may end up on the floor yourself, often underneath the other person. And if you succeed but go down with them, you may find yourself in a ground fight you did not want, possibly on concrete, possibly with other people around.

This means that the best throws for Goju-Ryu purposes are those that keep you on your feet. Many judo throws involve the thrower falling with the person being thrown, which is fine on a mat in a competition but much less desirable on a pavement. The throws in this chapter have been chosen because, done correctly, they leave you standing.

11.2 The Principle: Kuzushi, Entry, Execution

Every throw has three stages, and judo has names for each of them that are worth knowing.

Kuzushi is the balance-break, which we studied in Chapter 10. Before the throw can work, the other person’s balance must be compromised.

Tsukuri is the entry, or fitting in. You move your body into the right position relative to the other person: your hips, your legs, your arms and your head all have to be in particular places for the throw to work.

Kake is the execution: the actual throwing action, in which you use the position you have created to send the other person to the ground.

In practice, these three stages overlap and happen very quickly. But when you are learning a throw, it helps to separate them. If a throw is not working, ask yourself which stage is failing. Usually it is the first: the other person was not off balance when you tried to throw them.

11.3 Throw One: The Outer Reap

The outer reap is one of the simplest and most effective throws there is. It appears in judo as osoto-gari, and something very like it can be found in several Goju-Ryu kata, where a step forward behind the other person’s leg is combined with a strike or push to the upper body.

The movement

You are facing the other person at close range, with your left foot forward and your left hand controlling their right arm.

Kuzushi. Push their upper body back and to their right, so that their weight goes onto their right heel. The push can come from a palm under the chin, a forearm across the collarbone, or a pull on their right arm combined with a push on their left shoulder.

Tsukuri. Step forward with your left foot to the outside of their right foot, so that your left leg is level with theirs. Your weight is on your left leg. Your right leg is free.

Kake. Swing your right leg forward past their right leg, then back, sweeping your thigh against the back of their right thigh. At the same time, drive their upper body backwards and down. Their right leg is taken away just as their weight is pushed onto it, and they fall backward.

You remain standing on your left leg. As they fall, you can control their arm to slow their fall in training, or release them and move away.

Kata link: Saifa and Seisan

Both Saifa and Seisan contain movements in which a step forward is combined with a strike or push to head height, often with the other hand pulling back to the hip. Read as an outer reap, the step places your leg beside or behind the other person’s, the high hand drives their head back, and the pulling hand controls their arm. The kick that follows in some versions can be understood as the reaping leg itself.

Common errors

The most common error is trying to reap the leg before the other person’s weight is on it. If their weight is on their other foot, you will simply lift their leg and they will hop away. Make sure the kuzushi has put their weight on the leg you are reaping.

The second error is reaping with the foot rather than the thigh. A foot sweep at this distance has little power. Reap with the back of your thigh against the back of theirs, close to the knee.

The third error is leaning back as you reap, which takes your weight away from the throw. Keep your upper body forward, driving into them.

11.4 Throw Two: The Shoulder Wheel

The shoulder wheel, kata guruma in judo, is the throw I mentioned at the start of this chapter. It is one of the most commonly cited applications of Seiyunchin, and it is extremely effective when done well.

The movement

You are facing the other person, and you have hold of their right arm with your left hand.

Kuzushi. Pull their right arm forward and up, drawing them towards you and onto their toes.

Tsukuri. Drop into a low straddle stance, sliding your head under their right armpit and your right arm between their legs, wrapping around the back of their right thigh. Your right shoulder is in their abdomen. Your body is low, and your back is straight.

Kake. Stand up by driving through your legs, lifting them across your shoulders. Pull their arm across your body with your left hand and lift their leg with your right, so that they rotate over your shoulders. Turn and drop them to the floor beside you.

This throw involves lifting, and it requires good technique to do safely. The lift must come from the legs, with the back straight. Do not attempt it with a partner much heavier than you until you have mastered it with lighter partners.

A safer variation

A much safer variation, suitable for most training and more useful in many real situations, is the shoulder wheel takedown. Instead of lifting the person across your shoulders, you enter in the same way but, instead of standing up, you drive forward and turn, pulling their arm and driving their leg so that they topple sideways to the floor. You never lift them off the ground, which is much safer for both of you.

Kata link: Seiyunchin

Seiyunchin is performed almost entirely in shiko dachi, the low straddle stance, with one arm reaching high and the other low in several of its key movements. As a block, the high–low arm position is hard to explain. As a shoulder wheel, it is exactly the position you need: one hand holding the other person’s arm high, the other arm wrapped around their leg low, your stance dropped beneath their centre of gravity.

11.5 Throw Three: The Head Twist

The head-twist takedown uses the principle from Chapter 10 that where the head goes, the body follows. By turning the other person’s head sharply, you turn their whole body and topple them.

The movement

You are in close range, with your right hand able to reach the back of the other person’s head and your left hand able to reach their chin.

Kuzushi and tsukuri. Place your left palm on their chin and your right hand on the back of their head. Step forward with your right foot slightly to their left side.

Kake. Turn their head as if you were turning a steering wheel: chin pushed to their right and up, back of the head pulled to their left and down. Step around in a circle to your right as you turn. Their body follows the head and they topple to the floor.

This must be done slowly and very carefully in training, because neck injuries are serious. The turn should be a smooth rotation of the whole body, not a sharp twist of the neck. In training, your partner should begin to fall as soon as they feel the rotation, rather than resisting it.

Street note: neck techniques

Any technique that twists the neck can cause serious injury or death. In a real self-defence situation, a head-twist takedown may be justified against a serious threat, but it should be applied as a turn of the whole body rather than a twist of the neck, and released as soon as the person is falling. In training, it must always be performed slowly and with complete cooperation.

11.6 Throw Four: The Arm-Bar Takedown

The arm-bar takedown uses pressure on a straightened arm to drive the other person face down to the floor. It is one of the most useful takedowns for self-defence and restraint, because it leaves you standing over a controlled person, and it links directly to the joint-control work of Part Five.

The movement

The other person reaches for you with their right hand, perhaps grabbing your clothing or throwing a punch.

Kuzushi and tsukuri. Take their right wrist with your right hand and pull it forward along its line of travel, straightening their arm. Step forward with your left foot to their right side, so that you are beside their right arm, facing the same way as them. Place your left forearm or palm on the back of their right arm, just above the elbow.

Kake. Press down on their elbow with your left arm while you maintain the pull on their wrist with your right. Their arm is straight, and the pressure on the elbow forces their shoulder down. Step forward in a circle as you press, so that they spiral down to the floor face first.

Once they are on the floor, you can maintain control of the arm, as we will discuss in Chapter 14, or release them and move away.

Kata link: Shisochin

The opening sequence of Shisochin contains a repeated pressing, opening movement of the hands, followed later by a turning movement in which one arm presses down while the other pulls. Many instructors read parts of this as an arm-bar takedown, with the open hands seizing the wrist and the pressing arm driving the elbow down. Practise it slowly with a partner and see whether you agree.

11.7 Throw Five: The Inner Sweep

The inner sweep is a foot sweep that takes away the other person’s leg from the inside. It is useful in sparring, where many rule sets allow sweeps, and it is simple enough to work under pressure.

The movement

You are facing the other person with your left foot forward, and their left foot is forward too.

Kuzushi. Pull or push their upper body so that their weight shifts onto their left foot. The easiest way is to pull their right arm towards you, which turns them and puts weight on their left leg.

Kake. With the sole of your left foot, sweep the inside of their left ankle outward and forward, in the direction their weight is moving. At the same time, continue the pull on their arm. Their foot slides away and they fall.

The sweep must be timed to the moment their weight arrives on the swept foot, or just before. Too early, and the foot is too heavy to move; too late, and they have stepped. Sweeps are all about timing.

11.8 Throw Six: The Leg Catch

The leg catch is a defence against a kick that turns into a takedown. It is useful in sparring against round kicks and front kicks, and in self-defence against a kick to the body.

The movement

The other person throws a right round kick to your body.

Tsukuri. Step slightly towards the kick to jam it before it develops full power, and scoop the kicking leg with your left arm, trapping it against your side. Your right hand comes up to protect your head.

Kuzushi and kake. Lift the trapped leg slightly, which puts all their weight on their standing leg and makes them hop. Then either sweep the standing leg with your right foot, or step forward and push their upper body backward with your right hand, so that they fall back.

This is a very effective technique, but it is also very dangerous for the person being thrown, because they fall backwards with one leg held and cannot breakfall properly. In training, release the leg as they fall, or support their fall.

11.9 Throw Seven: The Turning Throw

The turning throw uses a circular movement of your whole body to swing the other person off their feet. It is the throw most directly linked to the many turns in the Goju-Ryu kata.

The movement

You have hold of the other person, perhaps with one hand on their arm and the other on their shoulder or collar.

Kuzushi. Push them slightly, and as they push back, step back and begin to turn. Your pull, combined with their push, draws them forward and around.

Tsukuri and kake. Continue turning, pivoting on one foot so that your body rotates through ninety degrees or more. As you turn, drop your weight slightly and pull them around you in a circle. Their feet cannot keep up with their upper body, and they are swung off balance and down to the floor, often landing on the side or back.

The turning throw requires no strength at all if the timing is right. It uses the momentum of both bodies and the centrifugal effect of the turn. It is also very safe for the thrower, because you never turn your back on the other person or go to the floor yourself.

Kata link: the turns

Almost every Goju-Ryu kata contains turns, and many of them are combined with grabbing or pulling hand movements. Seipai, Kururunfa, Shisochin, Seisan and Suparinpei all have turns that can be read as turning throws. When you practise these kata, imagine that you are holding someone as you turn, and feel how your turn would swing them.

11.10 Training Throws Safely

Throwing practice has a higher injury risk than almost anything else in this book. These are the safety rules I insist on in my own classes.

Mats. Never throw on a hard floor. Use proper judo-style mats, at least four centimetres thick.

Breakfalls first. Both partners must be able to breakfall competently before throwing practice begins. See Chapter 12.

Control the fall. As the thrower, keep hold of your partner’s arm or clothing as they fall, so that you can slow their descent and keep their head off the mat. This is called supporting the fall, and it is the thrower’s responsibility.

Start slowly. Learn each throw at walking pace, with your partner cooperating fully, before adding any speed or resistance.

No neck techniques at speed. Head twists and any throw that involves pressure on the neck must always be done slowly.

Space. Make sure there is enough space around you. Throws can carry people further than you expect.

Stop on request. If either partner says stop, stop.

Drill 11.1: Uchikomi (fitting practice)

Choose one throw. With a cooperative partner, practise only the kuzushi and tsukuri: break their balance and fit into position, then stop without throwing. Return to the start and repeat. Do twenty repetitions on each side.

This drill, called uchikomi in judo, builds the entry movement without the strain of throwing. It is how judoka learn throws, and it is a safe and efficient way to develop yours. Do it for every throw in this chapter before you start throwing.

Drill 11.2: Throw on the reaction

Your partner pushes you gently with both hands. You respond with a push–pull: let them push, then pull them forward as they push and enter into a throw of your choice. Begin with the turning throw, which suits a pushing partner perfectly.

Then reverse: your partner pulls you, and you respond by stepping in and pushing as they pull, entering into an outer reap.

This drill links the throws to the action–reaction principle and teaches you to throw on what you feel rather than on what you planned.

11.11 Throws in Sparring

Different rule sets treat throws very differently. Many traditional karate competitions allow foot sweeps and takedowns if followed immediately by a controlled strike. Some knockdown and mixed-rules formats allow a wide range of throws. Most sport karate rule sets prohibit throws that involve lifting or that drop the opponent on their head.

If your club allows throws in sparring, start with foot sweeps and the turning throw, which are safe and effective. Add the outer reap with care. Leave the shoulder wheel and the head twist for controlled drills only.

When you throw someone in sparring, follow up with a controlled strike, usually a punch stopped short of the head, to show that you could have finished the exchange. Then step back and let them get up.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My throws only work on beginners.”

Solution: Beginners do not know how to resist, so you can throw them without kuzushi. Experienced partners do, so you cannot. Go back to Chapter 10 and work on breaking balance through action and reaction. Then practise uchikomi until your entry is fast and smooth. Most throws fail at the kuzushi stage.

Problem: “I end up on the floor with my partner.”

Solution: You are committing your own balance too far into the throw. Keep your weight over your own feet, particularly your standing leg in the outer reap and the inner sweep. Let the other person fall; do not fall with them. Practise the throws slowly and check where your weight is at the end.

Problem: “The shoulder wheel hurts my back.”

Solution: You are lifting with your back rather than your legs, or your partner is too heavy. Use the takedown variation instead of the full lift, and work on your squat strength separately. If you have any history of back problems, do not attempt the full shoulder wheel.

Problem: “My sweeps never work.”

Solution: It is almost always timing. Sweep the foot as the weight arrives on it, not when it is fully loaded or after it has left. Practise with a partner who walks slowly towards you, and sweep each foot just as it touches the floor. Once you can feel that moment, add a pull to bring their weight onto the foot.

11.12 Bringing It Together

The Goju-Ryu kata are full of throws, and once you have learned to see them, the kata take on a new depth. The outer reap, the shoulder wheel, the head twist, the arm-bar takedown, the inner sweep, the leg catch and the turning throw give you a practical repertoire for both sparring and self-defence.

Every throw rests on kuzushi. If you break the balance first, the throw is easy. If you do not, it is a contest of strength. Learn the entries through uchikomi, train them on reactions, and always practise safely.

The next chapter covers the other side of throwing: how to fall without being hurt, how to survive on the ground, and how to get back to your feet as quickly as possible.

Key Points

  • Every throw has three stages: kuzushi (balance-breaking), tsukuri (entry) and kake (execution). When a throw fails, check the kuzushi first.
  • For Goju-Ryu purposes, the best throws leave you standing.
  • The outer reap takes away a leg that is carrying the weight, while the upper body is driven back.
  • The shoulder wheel from Seiyunchin is powerful but demanding; the takedown variation is safer.
  • The head twist and arm-bar takedown use the head and arm as handles; both demand great care in training.
  • Sweeps are all about timing: catch the foot as the weight arrives.
  • The turning throw uses the turns of the kata to swing the other person around you.
  • Never practise throws without mats and competent breakfalls.

Training Questions

  2. Perform Seiyunchin slowly and mark each place where a shoulder wheel or other throw might fit. Test the most plausible with a partner.
  3. Video yourself doing uchikomi for the outer reap. Where is your weight at the moment of entry? Is your back straight?

Ukemi and Ground Survival: Falling Well and Getting Back Up

If you train close combat, you will fall. You will be thrown in the dojo, you may slip on ice or be pushed in the street, and one day you may go to the ground in a struggle you did not choose. Knowing how to fall without injury, how to protect yourself once you are down, and how to get back to your feet quickly are among the most valuable skills in this book. They are also the most often neglected.

Of all the skills I have learned in martial arts, the one that has protected me most often has nothing to do with fighting. It is breakfalling. I have slipped on wet steps, been knocked off a bicycle, tripped over a dog lead in the dark and fallen down a short flight of stairs carrying a box. Each time, my body did what it had been trained to do thousands of times on the mat: tucked the chin, rounded the back, spread the impact, and rolled. Each time, I walked away with a bruise rather than a broken wrist or a head injury.

Falls are one of the leading causes of injury in everyday life, particularly as people get older. A skill that teaches you to fall safely is worth learning for that reason alone, whatever your interest in martial arts. For close combat, it is essential. You cannot practise throws safely without it, and in a real confrontation the ground is where many people are seriously hurt.

12.1 The Problem: The Ground Is Hard

The danger of going to the ground comes from three sources.

The first is the impact itself. A fall from standing height onto a hard surface generates a great deal of force. If that force is concentrated on a small area, such as an outstretched wrist, an elbow, a shoulder or the back of the head, the result can be a fracture, a dislocation or a serious head injury. Head injuries from falls onto concrete are one of the most common causes of death in street violence. Many of them happen not from a punch but from the fall that follows it.

The second is the position you end up in. On the ground, you are much more vulnerable to kicks, stamps and being held down. If there are other people involved, the ground is a very dangerous place to be.

The third is the difficulty of getting up. Getting up from the ground in a hurry, especially while someone is trying to keep you down or strike you, is surprisingly hard. Many people, trying to stand up, expose their heads and become even more vulnerable.

12.2 The Principle: Spread, Roll, Protect

Safe falling rests on three principles.

Spread the impact. The force of a fall is the same whatever you do; what you can change is how it is distributed. Spread it over as large an area of your body as possible, especially the large, padded areas such as the side of the thigh, the buttocks and the back of the shoulders, rather than concentrating it on a joint or the head.

Roll with the force. A body that rolls converts some of the downward force into rotation, reducing the impact. A body that lands flat and stops absorbs all of it at once.

Protect the head. Above all, keep your head off the ground. Tuck your chin to your chest so that the back of your head cannot strike the floor. This single habit prevents more serious injuries than any other.

There is also a fourth principle, specific to close combat: do not reach out with a straight arm. The instinct when falling is to put a hand out to catch yourself. On a hard surface, a straight arm catching your whole body weight is a common cause of broken wrists, elbows and collarbones. Breakfall training replaces this instinct with a better one.

12.3 The Basic Breakfalls

There are four basic breakfalls, known in Japanese as ukemi. Learn them in order, and practise them on mats until they are automatic.

The back breakfall (ushiro ukemi)

The back breakfall is used when you fall straight backwards.

Start by sitting on the mat with your legs out in front of you and your arms crossed in front of your chest. Tuck your chin firmly to your chest. Roll backwards onto your rounded back, and as your shoulders touch the mat, strike the mat with both arms, palms down, at about forty-five degrees from your body. Your head must not touch the mat. Roll forward again and repeat.

When this is comfortable, progress to a squatting position, then to a standing position, lowering yourself into a squat as you fall back so that you roll rather than drop.

The arm slap is not essential for protection, but it helps spread the impact and, more importantly, trains you to keep your arms out of harm’s way. Instead of reaching back to catch yourself, your arms strike the mat beside you.

The side breakfall (yoko ukemi)

The side breakfall is used when you fall to the side, which is how most throws land you.

Start by lying on your back with your legs raised slightly and your chin tucked. Roll onto your left side, and as you do, strike the mat with your left arm, palm down, at about forty-five degrees from your body. Your legs should be slightly apart, with the left leg straighter and the right leg bent, so that you land on the side of your thigh and buttock rather than on your hip bone. Roll onto your right side and repeat.

Progress to falling from a squat, then from standing, by sliding one leg across in front of the other and lowering yourself to the side.

The forward roll (mae mawari ukemi)

The forward roll is used when you are thrown or fall forward with momentum. It is the most useful breakfall in daily life, because it lets you roll through a forward fall and come back up to your feet.

Start in a kneeling position with your right knee up and your left knee on the mat. Place your right hand on the mat in front of you, fingers pointing back towards you, and your left hand beside it. Look over your left shoulder. Roll forward over your right arm, shoulder and diagonally across your back to your left hip, keeping your body rounded throughout. Your head should not touch the mat. As you complete the roll, either come up to a kneeling or standing position, or finish in a side breakfall.

The forward roll takes some time to learn. Go slowly, and have an experienced person check that you are rolling across the diagonal of your back rather than straight over your spine or your head.

The forward breakfall (mae ukemi)

The forward breakfall is used when you fall straight forward without the possibility of rolling.

Start kneeling. Fall forward, and as you do, bring your forearms down in front of you so that they strike the mat together, from elbows to palms, forming a triangle with your hands near your face. Turn your head to one side. Your forearms and the balls of your feet take the impact, and your body stays off the mat.

This breakfall needs particular care to avoid jarring the wrists or elbows. Practise from kneeling for several weeks before trying it from a crouch.

Drill 12.1: Daily breakfalls

Five minutes of breakfall practice at the start of every session: ten back breakfalls, ten side breakfalls each side, ten forward rolls each side, and five forward breakfalls. Begin from low positions and gradually progress to standing as your confidence grows.

Breakfalls are like scales for a musician. They are not exciting, but without them nothing else works. After a year of daily practice, they will be automatic.

12.4 Falling Outside the Dojo

Breakfalls on a mat are one thing. Falls in the real world are another. The ground is hard, you are wearing clothes and shoes, you may be carrying things, and the fall is unexpected.

The principles stay the same, but the emphasis changes.

On hard surfaces, rolling is better than slapping. Slapping concrete with your palm hurts and can injure the hand. Instead, aim to roll through the fall, spreading the impact along your arm, shoulder and back.

Tuck the chin always. This is the single most important habit. On concrete, the back of the head striking the ground is a potentially fatal injury.

Keep the arms bent. If you must use your arms, keep them bent and use them to guide yourself into a roll, not to stop yourself dead.

Protect your face when falling forward, by turning your head and taking the impact on bent forearms.

For older readers, or anyone with fragile bones, breakfall practice should be gentle and progressive, but it is especially valuable. Many people find that even a few months of practice changes their confidence and their instinctive reaction to a trip or slip.

12.5 On the Ground

Despite your best efforts, you may end up on the ground in a confrontation. When that happens, your priorities change completely.

Priority one: protect the head

If you are on the ground and the other person is standing, they can kick and stamp on you. The head is the most vulnerable target. Keep your arms up around your head, your knees up and your feet towards them, so that they have to get past your legs to reach your head.

Priority two: keep them at bay with your legs

On your back, with your knees bent and your feet towards the other person, you can use your legs to keep them away. Turn on your back so that your feet always face them. If they come close, kick at their knees and shins. This is sometimes called the ground guard or defensive position.

Priority three: get up safely

You must get up as soon as you can, but you must do it safely. Getting up in the wrong way exposes your head to kicks and lets the other person push you back down.

12.6 Getting Up Safely: The Technical Stand-Up

The technical stand-up, sometimes called the tactical get-up, is the safest way to get from the ground to your feet when someone is near you. It is taught in many martial arts and in police and military training, and it is worth knowing.

From sitting on the ground, facing the other person:

  1. Post your right hand on the floor behind you, beside your right hip.
  2. Plant your left foot flat on the floor, knee bent.
  3. Raise your left hand in front of your face as a guard, or use it to keep the other person at a distance.
  4. Push off your right hand and left foot to lift your hips off the floor.
  5. Swing your right leg back underneath you, through the gap between your right hand and left foot, and place it on the floor behind you.
  6. You are now in a low, balanced stance, facing the other person, with your guard up. Stand up and step back.

The beauty of the technical stand-up is that at every stage your head is back, your guard is up, and your legs are between you and the other person. You never turn your back on them or put your head forward.

Drill 12.2: Get-up circuits

Lie on your back. On the signal, perform a technical stand-up, step back into a guard, then lie back down. Ten repetitions on each side.

Progress: a partner stands near you holding a kick shield. Each time you get up, they push the shield gently towards you, and you must keep your guard up and your feet under you. Later, the partner may try to push you back down with the shield, and you must use the defensive position and the stand-up to get to your feet safely.

12.7 Ground Work in Goju-Ryu

Goju-Ryu is not a ground-fighting system. There is very little ground work in the traditional syllabus, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. If you want to learn to fight on the ground, you should train with people who specialise in it, such as judoka, wrestlers or Brazilian jiu-jitsu practitioners.

However, there are some things a Goju-Ryu practitioner should know about the ground, and they follow directly from the principles of this book.

Structure still matters. On the ground, as on your feet, good alignment and connecting tension let you resist pressure and generate force. Your elbows should stay close to your body, your chin tucked, your spine strong.

Frames still work. A forearm across someone’s neck or collarbone, with your elbow anchored on the floor or your own body, creates space and stops them pressing down on you.

Strikes still work, though they are shorter and weaker. Elbows, knees, headbutts and short punches can all be used from the ground.

Escape is still the priority. On the street, the aim on the ground is always to get back up and away. Do not try to win a ground fight; try to end it by getting up.

When you are underneath

If someone is on top of you, the most important thing is to stop them striking your head and to create enough space to escape. Bring your arms up to protect your head, keep your elbows tight to your body, and try to get your knees between you and them. A bridge (driving your hips up explosively from the floor) combined with a roll to one side can often dislodge someone sitting on you, especially if you trap one of their arms on that side first.

When you are on top

If you have taken someone down and are on top of them, you are in a position of advantage, but also of considerable legal responsibility. A person on the ground is generally much less of a threat than a person on their feet. Striking someone who is on the ground, particularly around the head, is very difficult to justify as reasonable force unless they remain a serious danger. In most self-defence situations, the right thing to do once you have someone on the ground is to get up and leave.

Street note: the ground and the law

Many of the most serious injuries in street violence happen to people on the ground. Kicking or stamping on someone who is down, particularly to the head, can kill. In the United Kingdom, continuing to attack someone who is no longer a threat is very likely to fall outside reasonable self-defence. If you have taken someone down, use that moment to escape. If you have been taken down, your priority is to protect your head and get up.

Kata link: Kururunfa and Suparinpei

Although Goju-Ryu kata are performed standing, several contain dropping movements in which the body sinks very low, sometimes almost to kneeling. Kururunfa contains a movement in which the body drops while the arms circle and press, and Suparinpei has deep stance changes with downward strikes. Some practitioners read these as applications against someone who has been taken to the ground, such as a downward strike or a control maintained as you descend with them. They are also a reminder that the kata assume you may need to change level dramatically.

12.8 Falling with a Partner

There is one more falling skill that is especially relevant to close combat: falling with someone. When two people are clinched together and one is thrown, the thrower may be pulled down too. When someone grabs you and falls, they may take you with them.

The key skills are:

Let go if you can. If you feel yourself being pulled down, release your grip and step away, unless holding on is protecting you.

Land on top if you must go down. If you cannot avoid going down, try to turn so that the other person lands first and you land on them, which cushions your fall.

Do not put your hands out. Even when falling with someone, the instinct to catch yourself with a straight arm is dangerous.

Get up immediately. As soon as you land, get up using the technical stand-up or a quick roll to your feet.

Fault: the reaching hand

The most common fault in breakfall practice is reaching back or down with a straight arm to catch the fall. This is the instinct that causes broken wrists and dislocated elbows.

Correction: Practise the back breakfall from sitting with a towel held between your hands across your chest. When you roll back, release the towel and slap the mat. Your hands are kept busy with the towel until the right moment, which retrains the instinct.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “I’m frightened of falling.”

Solution: That is very common, especially for adults who have not fallen deliberately since childhood. Start from the lowest positions, sitting and lying, and progress very slowly. Use thick mats. Practise for a few minutes every session rather than for long periods occasionally. Fear reduces with familiarity, and most people find that within a few months the fear has largely gone.

Problem: “I keep hitting my head on the back breakfall.”

Solution: You are not tucking your chin firmly enough, or you are falling straight back rather than rolling. Practise from sitting, with your chin pressed to your chest and your eyes looking at your belt knot throughout. If you can see your belt, your head is safe.

Problem: “The forward roll hurts my shoulder.”

Solution: You are probably landing on the point of your shoulder rather than rolling across the back of it, or your arm is collapsing. Keep your leading arm curved, like a wheel, and roll along its outside edge onto the back of the shoulder blade. Ask an experienced person to check your technique.

Problem: “I’m older and worried about injury.”

Solution: Breakfall practice is especially valuable as you get older, because it improves your reaction to everyday falls. But progress gently. Stay with the low positions for longer, use thick mats, and avoid forward rolls and forward breakfalls if you have shoulder, neck or wrist problems. Even sitting back breakfalls and side rolls give real benefit. Check with your doctor if you have osteoporosis or any similar condition.

12.9 Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Four. We have studied balance-breaking, the throws hidden in the kata, and the skills of falling, ground survival and getting up.

These last skills are not glamorous, but they may be the most important in the book. Throws are useless if you cannot practise them safely, and the ground is where many people suffer the worst injuries. Learn to fall well. Learn to protect your head. Learn to get up safely. And remember that on the street, the ground is a place to leave as quickly as possible.

In Part Five, we turn to joint controls and restraint: the techniques that let you control another person without striking them.

Key Points

  • Breakfalling protects you in daily life as well as in close combat. It is worth learning for that reason alone.
  • Spread the impact, roll with the force, and above all protect the head by tucking the chin.
  • Never reach out with a straight arm to catch a fall.
  • Learn the back, side, forward roll and forward breakfalls in that order, from low positions first.
  • On hard surfaces, roll rather than slap.
  • On the ground, protect the head, keep your feet towards the other person, and get up as soon as it is safe.
  • Use the technical stand-up to rise with your guard up and your head back.
  • On the street, the ground is a place to leave, not a place to fight.

Training Questions

  1. Practise Drill 12.1 every training day for a month. Note how your confidence changes from week one to week four.
  2. Perform the technical stand-up slowly with your eyes closed. Which stage feels least stable? Why?
  3. Think about the surfaces you walk on every day: pavements, stairs, car parks, shop floors. Which would be most dangerous to fall on? How would you adapt your breakfall to each?

Kansetsu-waza: The Principles of Joint Control

Joint controls let you move, steer and restrain another person by applying pressure to their joints in directions the joints do not want to go. Done well, they need surprisingly little strength, and they offer an option between doing nothing and striking. Done badly, they fail under pressure or cause serious injury. This chapter sets out the principles that make joint controls work, and the safety rules that make them trainable.

There is a common belief, encouraged by a great many films and demonstrations, that joint locks are magical. A slight figure takes hold of a large attacker’s wrist, makes a small twisting movement, and the attacker drops to his knees in agony. It looks effortless, and it suggests that anyone who learns the secret can control anyone else with a flick of the wrist.

The reality is more complicated. Joint controls can be extremely effective, and they can indeed allow a smaller person to control a larger one. But they work only when certain conditions are met: when the joint is isolated, when the other person’s structure is broken, when the lever is long and the pressure is in the right direction, and above all when the other person is distracted, off balance or hurt. Against a calm, balanced, resisting person who is expecting it, a wrist lock is extremely hard to apply.

The old Okinawan teachers knew this well. The joint-manipulation methods they practised, sometimes called tuidi or tuite, were usually applied after a strike, during a grab, or as part of a throw, not as a standalone technique against a ready opponent. In this chapter and the next, we will learn them the same way.

13.1 The Problem: When Striking Is Not the Answer

Most of this book has been about striking, trapping and throwing. These are effective tools, but they are not always the right ones.

Sometimes you need to control someone rather than hurt them. You may be dealing with a drunk friend, a confused or distressed person, a family member, or someone who is a nuisance rather than a serious threat. Striking them may be out of proportion, and the law expects you to use only as much force as is reasonable.

Sometimes you need to move someone: out of a doorway, away from another person, or towards an exit. A strike will not do that; a joint control can.

Sometimes you need to hold someone until help arrives. Again, a strike will not help.

And sometimes you are already in contact with someone, perhaps because they have grabbed you, and a joint control is simply the most natural response to the position you are in.

Joint controls give you options in all these situations. They form a middle ground between doing nothing and doing something drastic.

13.2 The Principle: Lever, Fulcrum, Direction

Every joint control is an application of simple mechanics. To understand them, you need three ideas.

The lever

A lever is a rigid bar that turns around a fixed point. The longer the lever, the less force is needed to turn it. In joint controls, the other person’s limb is the lever. The forearm is a lever for the wrist; the whole arm is a lever for the shoulder; the forearm and hand together are a lever for the elbow.

The practical lesson is to apply force as far from the joint as possible. To control the elbow, push or pull near the wrist, not near the elbow itself. To control the shoulder, use the whole arm, not just the upper arm.

The fulcrum

The fulcrum is the fixed point around which the lever turns. In joint controls, the fulcrum is usually the joint itself, or a point just beside it that you stabilise with your own body.

For the lever to work, the fulcrum must not move. If you push on someone’s wrist to bend their elbow and their whole arm simply moves, there is no fulcrum and no control. You must fix one end of the lever, usually by pinning the joint with your hand, forearm or body, while you apply force at the other end.

The direction

Every joint has directions in which it moves freely and directions in which it does not. The elbow bends and straightens freely, but it does not bend backwards or sideways. The wrist bends forwards and backwards and moves a little from side to side, and it rotates with the forearm, but it does not rotate beyond a certain point. The shoulder moves in many directions, but each has a limit.

Joint controls work by moving a joint towards the limit of its range in a direction it cannot escape from easily. The pressure must be in exactly the right direction. A few degrees off, and the other person will simply move with the pressure and escape.

13.3 Isolating the Joint

A joint can be controlled only if it is isolated: if the other person cannot relieve the pressure by moving another part of their body.

Consider a straight-arm lock on the elbow. If you push on the back of someone’s elbow while their arm is straight, they can relieve the pressure by bending the arm, by turning their shoulder, by stepping forward, or by turning their whole body. Each of these movements takes the elbow away from the limit of its range. For the lock to work, you must stop all of them.

This is why most effective joint controls involve more than one point of contact. One hand holds the wrist to stop it rotating; the other hand or forearm presses on the elbow; your body position stops their shoulder turning; and your footwork stops them stepping.

In practice, you cannot always isolate a joint completely, but you can make it very difficult to escape by combining the lock with balance-breaking. A person who is off balance cannot step or turn to relieve the pressure, because they need all their movement to stay on their feet.

13.4 Structure: Yours and Theirs

Just as in striking and throwing, structure is the key to joint control.

Breaking their structure. A person with good structure, with their elbows down and their shoulders connected to their back, is very hard to lock, because their whole body supports each joint. A person with broken structure, with their elbow raised and their shoulder disconnected, is much easier. Many joint controls begin by raising the other person’s elbow away from their body, which breaks the connection between the arm and the trunk.

Keeping your own structure. When you apply a joint control, use your whole body, not just your arms. Keep your elbows close to your body, turn your hips, and drop your weight into the control. A lock applied with arm strength alone is weak and can be resisted by a stronger person. A lock applied with your body weight behind it is much harder to fight.

13.5 Pain, Compliance and Damage

Joint controls work in three different ways, and it is important to understand the differences.

Pain compliance

At the first level, the joint is moved near the limit of its range, causing pain. The other person moves to relieve the pain, and in doing so goes where you want them to go. This is the level at which most joint controls are used for restraint and escort. It relies on the person feeling the pain and responding to it.

The weakness of pain compliance is that it does not work on everyone. People who are drunk, on drugs, highly agitated or simply very tough may not respond to pain at all. If pain compliance fails, you must be ready to do something else.

Mechanical control

At the second level, the joint is held at the limit of its range so that the other person cannot move in certain directions, regardless of pain. Their skeleton is locked, and their body must follow the lock. This is more reliable than pain compliance, because it does not depend on the other person feeling or responding to pain.

Mechanical control usually requires a combination of a joint lock and a body position, such as an arm held straight with pressure on the shoulder while the person is face down on the floor.

Damage

At the third level, the joint is forced beyond the limit of its range, causing a sprain, dislocation or fracture. This disables the limb. It is the most reliable level, but also the most serious, and it can only be justified in situations of grave danger.

In training, you work only at the first level, and very gently. You should learn where the second and third levels are, but you must never go there with a training partner.

Street note: proportionate control

In the United Kingdom, any force you use must be reasonable in the circumstances. Joint controls are often a more proportionate response than strikes in situations involving a nuisance or a low-level threat. But a joint control that dislocates an elbow or breaks a wrist is serious violence and must be justified as such. Apply only as much pressure as is needed to achieve control, and release the control as soon as the danger has passed.

13.6 The Safety Rules

Joint controls are the techniques in this book most likely to injure a training partner, because the line between pain and damage can be very thin, and because different people have very different ranges of movement. A wrist that can bend ninety degrees on one person may be injured at sixty degrees on another.

These are the rules I use in my own classes, and I strongly encourage you to adopt them.

Tap and release. The person receiving the lock taps clearly on their partner or on themselves, or says “tap” or “stop”, as soon as they feel pain. The person applying the lock releases immediately. This rule is absolute.

Slow application. All joint controls are applied slowly in training, over two or three seconds, so that the receiver has time to tap before the pressure becomes dangerous. Never snap a lock on.

No jerking. Joint controls are never applied with a sudden jerk or twist in training. Sudden force does not give the joint time to signal pain before damage occurs.

Know your partner. Ask about previous injuries before you start. People with old wrist, elbow or shoulder injuries need particular care.

Tap early. As a receiver, tap as soon as you feel genuine discomfort. There is no prize for enduring a lock, and joint injuries can take months or years to heal.

Supervision. Joint controls should be learned under the supervision of an experienced instructor.

Fault: the late tap

Pride makes many people tap late. They want to show they can take the pain, or they think they can escape. Then something goes wrong and a ligament tears.

Correction: Tap early, every time. You learn nothing by enduring a lock. You learn a great deal by feeling where the lock begins, and then working out how to escape before it gets there.

13.7 The Joints

Let us look briefly at the main joints and how they can be controlled. Specific techniques follow in Chapter 14.

The fingers

The fingers are small and weak, and they can be controlled very easily by bending them backwards or sideways. A single finger bent back can move a large person. However, finger locks are easy to slip out of, and fingers break very easily. They are most useful for breaking a grip.

The wrist

The wrist can be controlled by bending it forwards (flexion), bending it backwards (extension), turning it outwards or inwards beyond its normal range, or a combination. Wrist locks are the classic joint controls of aikido and jujutsu, and some of the most effective are found in Goju-Ryu kata. They work best when the elbow is bent and the arm is close to the body, because then the forearm forms a strong lever.

The elbow

The elbow can be controlled by straightening it beyond its normal range (hyperextension), usually by pressing on the back of the elbow while the arm is held straight, or by bending it and rotating the forearm. Elbow controls are among the most reliable, because the elbow is a simple hinge with a clear limit.

The shoulder

The shoulder can be controlled by rotating the arm inwards or outwards beyond its normal range, or by raising the arm behind the back. Shoulder controls are very powerful because the shoulder controls the whole upper body, and they are often the basis of restraint holds.

The neck

The neck is technically a set of joints, and it can be controlled by twisting or bending the head. Neck controls are extremely dangerous and can cause paralysis or death. In this book I discuss neck control only in the context of head control in the clinch (Chapter 9) and turning takedowns (Chapter 11), and escapes from neck holds (Chapter 15). I do not teach neck locks.

13.8 Setting Up Joint Controls

As I said at the beginning of this chapter, joint controls are very hard to apply against a calm, balanced, resisting person. They work best when they are set up. Here are the main ways to do that.

After a strike

A strike causes pain, distraction and a flinch. In the moment of distraction, the other person’s arm is often in a position where it can be locked, and their mind is elsewhere. Many traditional applications begin with a strike, often to the face or a nerve point, followed immediately by a lock.

During a grab

When someone grabs you, their hand is fixed in place, and their arm is extended towards you. This is the classic setup for wrist and elbow locks, because the grab provides the fulcrum for you. You simply have to supply the lever.

During a push or punch

When someone pushes or punches, their arm is extended, and their weight is moving towards you. A pull along the line of the push or punch, combined with a lock, uses their own force to apply the control.

From a trap

A trap in which you have hold of the other person’s arm is a natural setup for a joint control. If you have pinned their arm against their body or pulled it across their body, it is already partly controlled.

From a throw

Many throws, particularly the arm-bar takedown in Chapter 11, naturally lead into a joint control on the ground.

Kata link: Shisochin and Kururunfa

Shisochin contains movements in which one hand presses down while the other pulls up, sometimes with a turn of the body. These are often read as elbow or shoulder controls applied after a grab. Kururunfa, sometimes described as a kata of “holding on long and striking suddenly”, contains several sequences of grabbing, turning and pressing that suit joint control applications very well. Both kata reward close study with a careful partner.

13.9 Joint Controls and Sensitivity

Finally, joint controls depend heavily on sensitivity. To apply a lock effectively, you must feel where the other person’s structure is weak, which direction their joint is resisting, and when they are about to move. The best people at joint controls are almost always people with excellent sensitivity, developed through kakie or a similar practice.

This is another reason why the order of this book matters. If you jump straight to joint controls without the sensitivity of Part Two, you will find them very difficult. If you have spent months in kakie, you will find that you can feel the lock developing and adjust it moment by moment.

Drill 13.1: Finding the end of range

Work with a cooperative partner. Take their wrist gently and move it slowly through its full range in each direction: forward, back, side to side and rotation. Feel the point where the movement starts to resist. Do not go beyond it.

Then do the same for the elbow and shoulder. Your aim is to learn what the end of range feels like, so that you can recognise it in a lock. Swap roles, and notice how different people’s ranges are.

Drill 13.2: Lock on the grab

Your partner grabs your wrist firmly. Without trying any particular technique, explore the grab: turn your hand, move your elbow, step around. Find the positions in which you can feel pressure developing on their wrist, elbow or shoulder. When you find one, apply it slowly until they tap.

This exploratory drill teaches you that joint controls are not fixed techniques but natural consequences of positions. Once you understand that, the specific techniques in the next chapter will make much more sense.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My locks never work on resisting partners.”

Solution: That is normal. Locks rarely work on calm, balanced, resisting people. Set them up with a strike, a grab, a trap or a balance-break, and apply them while the other person is distracted or moving. Also check that you are isolating the joint and that your direction is precise.

Problem: “My partner says it doesn’t hurt.”

Solution: Your direction is slightly off, or there is no fulcrum, so the other person is simply moving with the pressure. Slow down, find the fulcrum, and adjust the angle by small amounts until your partner feels it. Different people feel locks at different points, so be patient.

Problem: “I’m worried about hurting my partner.”

Solution: That is a good worry to have. Follow the safety rules strictly: slow application, no jerking, immediate release on tap. Ask your partner about old injuries. And remember that most of the learning in joint controls happens at very low pressure.

Problem: “I’m much smaller than my partner. Can I really lock someone that big?”

Solution: Yes, if you use leverage and set the lock up well. A long lever and a well-fixed fulcrum multiply your force enormously. But do not expect locks to work on a large, calm person who is resisting. Set them up, and combine them with balance-breaking, so that the other person’s size and strength are working against them.

13.10 Bringing It Together

Joint controls are a valuable option between doing nothing and striking. They let you move, steer and hold another person, often with relatively little force.

They rest on simple mechanics: a lever, a fulcrum and a precise direction. They require the joint to be isolated and the other person’s structure to be broken. They work at three levels, pain compliance, mechanical control and damage, and in training you must stay firmly at the first. And they work best when set up by a strike, grab, push, trap or throw.

The next chapter applies these principles to specific controls of the wrist, elbow and shoulder.

Key Points

  • Joint controls work by moving a joint to the end of its range in a direction it cannot escape.
  • Every control needs a lever (as long as possible), a fulcrum (fixed) and a precise direction.
  • Isolate the joint by stopping the other person relieving the pressure through other movements.
  • Break their structure, keep yours, and apply locks with the body, not the arms.
  • Controls work at three levels: pain compliance, mechanical control and damage. Train only at the first.
  • Follow the safety rules absolutely: tap early, release immediately, apply slowly, never jerk.
  • Set controls up with strikes, grabs, pushes, traps and throws. Against a calm, resisting person, they rarely work alone.

Training Questions

  1. Using Drill 13.1, compare the wrist range of three different partners. How much does it vary? What does that tell you about safety?
  2. Choose one movement from Shisochin or Kururunfa and work out, with a partner, whether it could be a joint control. Where is the lever, the fulcrum and the direction?
  3. Think of three real situations in which a joint control would be more appropriate than a strike. What would make it the better choice in each?

Wrist, Elbow and Shoulder Controls

With the principles of Chapter 13 in place, we can look at specific controls. This chapter teaches six joint controls that I have found reliable under pressure, along with the restraint and escort positions that follow from them. Each is shown from a realistic starting point, usually a grab, a push or a punch, because that is where joint controls actually happen.

When I began studying joint controls, I collected them the way some people collect stamps. I learned dozens of wrist locks, elbow locks and shoulder locks, each with its own name and its own precise sequence of movements. Most of them worked beautifully in the dojo, against partners who stood still and offered their wrists.

Then I started working with people who did not cooperate: police officers on a self-defence course, security staff, and a few very strong judo players who thought my wrist locks were faintly ridiculous. Almost all of my collection failed. Only a handful survived, and those were the ones I have kept teaching ever since. They have three things in common. They use large movements rather than small ones. They work from realistic starting positions. And they flow naturally from or into strikes, traps and throws.

Those are the controls in this chapter.

14.1 The Problem: From Contact to Control

The starting point for every control in this chapter is contact. The other person has grabbed you, pushed you, thrown a punch that you have blocked, or been trapped by you. Their arm is in contact with yours, and you want to turn that contact into control.

The difficulty is that the contact lasts only a moment. A grab may be released, a push withdrawn, a punch retracted. You must move from contact to control very quickly, before the other person has time to react, and you must do it without letting them hit you with their other hand.

The solution is to use the movement that the contact already contains. A grab is pulling you; go with the pull. A push is pushing you; turn and let it go past. A punch is extending; pull it further. Joint controls applied in the direction of the other person’s own movement are much faster and much harder to resist than controls applied against it.

14.2 Control One: The Outward Wrist Turn

The outward wrist turn, known in many arts as kote gaeshi, is one of the most famous joint controls in the world. It rotates the wrist outward and backward, turning the other person’s arm and body and, if continued, throwing them to the floor.

From a wrist grab

The other person grabs your left wrist with their right hand.

Entry. Circle your left hand outward and upward, turning it so that your palm faces up and your fingers point towards their elbow. This loosens their grip and brings their hand to the outside. As you do, place your right hand over the back of their right hand, with your thumb on the back of their hand between the knuckles of the ring and little fingers and your fingers wrapping around the base of their thumb.

Control. Free your left hand and add it to the control, so that both your hands hold their right hand. Your thumbs are on the back of their hand. Turn their hand outward, rotating it away from them and bending the wrist back towards their forearm, as if you were turning a large key.

Takedown. Step back with your left foot, turning your body to your left, and continue the rotation. Their arm and body follow the rotation, and they fall backward and to the side. In training, they should breakfall to the side as they feel the turn.

The key detail is that the rotation must be in a large circle, driven by your body turning, not a small twist of the wrist driven by your hands. A small twist is easy to resist; a large turn of the whole body is not.

From a punch

The outward wrist turn can also be applied to a punch, by catching the punching hand as it withdraws and applying the turn. This is harder, because the hand is moving fast, but it is a classic application of the hooking and pulling hand movements in kata.

14.3 Control Two: The Inward Wrist Bend

The inward wrist bend, related to nikyo in aikido, bends the wrist forward and rotates it inward, creating intense pain at the wrist. It is a powerful pain-compliance control and a good basis for escorting someone.

From a lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing at chest height with their right hand.

Entry. Trap their right hand against your chest with your right hand, palm flat on the back of their hand. Their hand is now fixed: they cannot release it without pulling it out from under your palm.

Control. Bring your left forearm over their right forearm, close to their wrist, and press down. At the same time, bow slightly forward from the hips. Their wrist is bent forward between your chest and your forearm, and the pressure drives them downward.

Finish. Continue the pressure until they go down to their knees, or until they tap in training. From here you can release and leave, or move into an escort position.

The beauty of this control is that the grab provides the fulcrum. Their hand is pinned against your chest by your hand, and all you have to do is press their forearm down. It requires very little strength and is very hard to escape once it is on.

Kata link: the opening of Seipai and Shisochin

Several Goju-Ryu kata contain a movement in which one hand is drawn to the chest while the other forearm presses forward or down. Read as the inward wrist bend, the hand at the chest pins the grabbing hand, and the pressing forearm drives the wrist down. This is one of the clearest examples of a joint control hidden in an apparent block.

14.4 Control Three: The Straight-Arm Bar

The straight-arm bar controls the elbow by pressing on the back of a straight arm. We saw it as a takedown in Chapter 11; here we look at it as a control.

From a push

The other person pushes you with their right hand on your left shoulder or chest.

Entry. Let the push turn you slightly to your left, so that their arm slides past. Take their right wrist with your right hand and draw it forward, straightening the arm. Step forward with your left foot to their right side.

Control. Place your left forearm on the back of their right arm, just above the elbow. Pull their wrist slightly upward with your right hand while pressing down on the elbow with your left forearm. Their arm is straight and locked, and the pressure on the elbow drives their shoulder down.

Takedown or escort. Continue pressing and circle forward to take them to the floor, as in Chapter 11. Or hold the control and walk them in the direction you want, keeping the arm straight.

The straight-arm bar is one of the most reliable controls because the elbow has a very clear limit. However, it is also one of the most dangerous, because the elbow can be hyperextended and injured very quickly. In training, apply it very slowly.

14.5 Control Four: The Bent-Arm Lock

The bent-arm lock, sometimes called the figure-four or key lock, controls the shoulder by bending the arm and rotating it, either upward (an outward rotation) or behind the back (an inward rotation). It is very powerful and forms the basis of many restraint positions.

The rear version (behind the back)

The other person reaches for you with their right hand.

Entry. Take their right wrist with your right hand. Step to their right side and slide your left arm under their right arm from the front, between their arm and their body, so that your left forearm is in the crook of their elbow.

Control. Bend their right arm by pushing their wrist back and down with your right hand while lifting under their elbow with your left forearm. Their arm folds behind their back. Grip your own right wrist with your left hand, forming a figure four around their arm.

Finish. Lift their elbow while keeping their hand low, rotating their shoulder inward. This creates strong pressure on the shoulder and drives them forward and onto their toes.

This lock is the basis of the classic “hammerlock” escort, and it is very effective. It is also very dangerous to the shoulder. Apply it slowly and release on the tap.

14.6 Control Five: The Elbow Lift and Turn

The elbow lift and turn is less a lock than a way of steering someone by controlling their elbow. It is very useful for moving people without hurting them, and it is the gentlest control in this chapter.

From any contact

You have contact with the other person’s right arm, near the elbow.

Control. Place your left palm under their right elbow and your right hand on their right wrist or forearm. Lift the elbow slightly, which raises their shoulder and breaks their posture. At the same time, turn their forearm so that their palm faces up.

Steering. With their elbow lifted and their arm turned, you can steer them by turning their elbow in the direction you want them to go. Their body follows the elbow. Walk them where you want them, keeping the elbow lifted.

This is a very good control for dealing with someone who is a nuisance but not a serious threat, such as a friend who has had too much to drink. It is uncomfortable but not painful, and it gives you control of their movement without escalating the situation.

14.7 Control Six: The Wrist Lock Escort

The wrist lock escort, sometimes called the gooseneck, bends the other person’s wrist forward and holds it against your body, allowing you to walk them with very little effort.

From the elbow lift

You have lifted the other person’s right elbow and turned their arm, as in control five.

Entry. Bend their right arm so that their forearm is vertical and their hand is near your chest. Hold their elbow against your body with your left arm.

Control. With your right hand, bend their wrist forward so that their fingers point down and their palm faces them, like the neck of a goose. Press their fingers towards their forearm.

Escort. With their elbow trapped against your body and their wrist bent, you can walk them forward. If they resist, a small increase in the wrist pressure brings them up onto their toes.

This is the control used by many security staff and police officers for escorting a non-compliant but not violent person. It is very effective, but like all wrist locks it depends on pain compliance, and it may not work on someone who is very intoxicated or very determined.

14.8 Combining Controls

Joint controls rarely work perfectly the first time. The other person will move, resist and try to escape. The skill is to flow from one control to the next as they escape, so that each escape leads them into another control.

The classic pattern is that each control has a natural escape, and each escape leads naturally into another control.

Outward wrist turn and inward bend. If the other person resists an outward wrist turn by pulling their hand back towards themselves, their wrist bends forward, which is the inward bend. Follow their resistance into it.

Straight-arm bar and bent-arm lock. If they resist a straight-arm bar by bending their arm, the arm is now bent and can be folded behind their back into the bent-arm lock.

Elbow lift and wrist escort. The elbow lift leads naturally into the wrist escort if more control is needed.

This idea of following resistance from one control to another is the joint-control version of the action–reaction principle that runs through this book. It is also where sensitivity becomes essential, because you must feel the resistance as it begins and move with it.

Drill 14.1: Control chains

With a cooperative partner, apply each control in turn. Your partner offers a gentle, realistic resistance to each one, for example bending the arm against the straight-arm bar. You follow their resistance into the next control. Begin with pairs of controls, then build chains of three.

Go slowly, and stop on the tap. The aim is to learn the transitions, not to win.

14.9 Restraint and Release

Once you have someone under control, what then?

In most real situations, the answer is to release them and leave. A joint control has done its job if it has stopped an attack, moved someone out of your way, or given you a moment to escape. Holding someone in a lock for longer than necessary increases the risk of injury to them, legal risk to you, and the danger that their friends will intervene.

In some situations, such as a security role or a situation where you must protect someone else, you may need to hold someone for a time. If so, keep the following in mind.

Use the lowest effective level. Apply only as much pressure as is needed to maintain control.

Watch their breathing. Never put pressure on someone’s neck, chest or back that restricts their breathing. People have died from being held face down with weight on their backs. This is called positional asphyxia, and it is a real and serious danger.

Talk to them. Tell them calmly what you want them to do. Many people stop resisting once they understand that resistance is pointless and that you are not trying to hurt them.

Get help. Do not try to hold someone on your own for a long time. Call the police or ask others to help.

Release safely. When you let go, step away to a safe distance at an angle, so that they cannot immediately attack you as they get up.

Street note: restraint is risky

Holding someone down is far more dangerous than many people realise. Restraint deaths, usually through positional asphyxia, have occurred when people were held face down with weight on their backs, sometimes for only a few minutes. If you ever have to restrain someone on the ground, keep your weight off their back and chest, watch their breathing and colour, and release the moment they stop resisting. If they say they cannot breathe, take it seriously.

Fault: small and quick

The most common fault in joint controls is trying to make them small and quick, using the hands alone, with a sharp twist. This rarely works against a resisting person, and it is very dangerous in training.

Correction: Make controls large and slow. Use your whole body, turn your hips, step, and drop your weight. A big, smooth movement of the whole body is much harder to resist than a small, quick twist of the hands, and it is far safer for your partner.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My partner just pulls their hand away before I can apply the wrist turn.”

Solution: You are going for the lock too directly. Distract them first, with a strike or a pull, or use the moment their grab is fixed. Also make sure your first contact traps their hand: in the inward bend, for example, pin the hand against your chest before you try to lock it.

Problem: “The straight-arm bar just turns into a wrestling match.”

Solution: You are probably not controlling the wrist well enough, so they can bend the arm. Draw the wrist forward to straighten the arm before you apply pressure, and keep it drawn forward throughout. If they bend the arm anyway, follow it into the bent-arm lock.

Problem: “Wrist locks don’t seem to hurt my partner.”

Solution: Some people have very flexible wrists, and wrist locks are less effective on them. Use elbow and shoulder controls instead, which are less dependent on individual flexibility. Also check your fulcrum: a wrist lock without a fixed fulcrum just moves the arm.

Problem: “I can apply the controls but I don’t know what to do next.”

Solution: In most real situations, the next thing is to release and leave. If you need to keep control, practise the escort positions until you can walk a partner around the room comfortably. And practise the transitions between controls, so that if one fails you flow into another.

14.10 Bringing It Together

These six controls, the outward wrist turn, the inward wrist bend, the straight-arm bar, the bent-arm lock, the elbow lift and turn, and the wrist lock escort, give you a practical set of options for controlling, moving and restraining another person.

Each works best from a realistic starting point, uses the whole body rather than the hands, and flows naturally into the others. Each must be trained slowly and safely, with immediate release on the tap. And each should be used, in real life, only as much and as long as necessary.

The final chapter of Part Five turns the tables: what to do when someone else has hold of you.

Key Points

  • Use the movement the contact already contains: go with grabs, turn with pushes, pull punches further.
  • The outward wrist turn rotates the wrist outward in a large circle driven by the body.
  • The inward wrist bend uses a grab as its fulcrum, pinning the hand to your chest.
  • The straight-arm bar and bent-arm lock control the elbow and shoulder; both are powerful and must be trained very carefully.
  • The elbow lift and turn steers someone with minimal pain, ideal for low-level situations.
  • The wrist lock escort lets you walk someone with very little effort.
  • When a control fails, follow the resistance into another control.
  • In real life, release and leave as soon as you can. If you must restrain, never restrict breathing.

Releases: Escaping Grabs, Holds and Neck Attacks

Much of the real violence people face does not start with a punch. It starts with a grab: a hand on the wrist, a fist in the collar, an arm around the neck, a pair of arms around the body from behind. This chapter turns the joint-control and close-range principles of the book to defensive use, and shows how to escape the grabs and holds that people most often encounter.

When I teach self-defence courses for people with no martial arts background, the techniques they ask about most are not strikes or throws. They are escapes. “What do I do if someone grabs my wrist?” “What if someone grabs me from behind?” “What if someone puts their hands round my neck?”

These are good questions, because grabs and holds are common, frightening and often the first stage of something worse. A grab is a way of controlling you, holding you still while you are hit, moving you somewhere you do not want to go, or stopping you leaving. Being able to escape quickly and confidently is one of the most practical skills in close combat.

It is also, as it happens, the context for which many of the Goju-Ryu kata seem to have been designed. Saifa begins with an escape from a wrist grab. Several kata contain movements that make most sense as escapes from holds around the body or neck. The old teachers clearly expected their students to be grabbed.

15.1 The Problem: Being Held

Being held is dangerous for three reasons.

First, it fixes you in place, so that you can be struck more easily. A person holding your collar with one hand can hit you with the other, and you cannot step back out of range.

Second, it lets you be moved. A person holding your arm or clothing can drag you, push you against a wall, pull you off balance or take you somewhere more private.

Third, and often overlooked, it is frightening. Being grabbed triggers a powerful freeze response in most people. The mind goes blank, the body stiffens, and valuable seconds are lost.

15.2 The Principle: Weak Point, Leverage, Action

Escapes from grabs rest on three principles.

The weak point of a grip

Every grip has a weak point, and for a hand grip it is almost always the gap between the thumb and the fingers. Four fingers are much stronger than one thumb. If you pull against the fingers, you are fighting four; if you pull towards the thumb, you are fighting one. So the basic rule for escaping a hand grip is: move towards the thumb.

Leverage, not strength

Do not try to pull straight out of a grip with strength. Use leverage: rotate your arm, use your elbow as a lever, use your whole body. A small rotation of the forearm against the thumb is far more effective than a large pull against the fingers.

Act, do not just escape

An escape on its own often leaves you exactly where you started: facing someone who has just grabbed you and who will probably try again. Combine the escape with an action: a strike, a step to the outside, a trap or simply leaving. The escape is the beginning, not the end.

The order of things

In practice, the sequence is often: distract, escape, act. A strike or a sudden movement distracts the other person, the escape is made while they are distracted, and then you act, usually by moving away or following up. Against a strong person, the distraction is often what makes the escape possible.

15.3 Wrist Grabs

Wrist grabs are the simplest grabs and the best place to start.

The same-side wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with their left hand, so that their thumb is on top of your wrist.

Escape. Find their thumb. Rotate your forearm so that the narrow edge of your wrist turns towards the gap between their thumb and fingers. Bend your elbow and drive it towards their forearm, while turning your hand up and out in a small circle. Your wrist slides out through the gap.

Follow-up. As your hand comes free, it is already close to their face. Strike with a back fist or palm heel, or step to the outside of their arm.

The cross wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with their right hand, so their thumb is on the inside of your wrist.

Escape. Rotate your forearm towards their thumb and bring your elbow up and across towards them. Your wrist turns out through the gap. This is the movement at the start of Saifa: the free hand covers the trapped hand, and the body steps and pulls.

Follow-up. The step that releases the grip also takes you to their side. From there, a hammer fist or back fist, as in the kata, follows naturally.

The two-handed wrist grab

The other person grabs your right wrist with both hands.

Escape. Reach between their arms with your left hand and grasp your own right fist. Pull your right arm up sharply between their thumbs, using both your arms and the drive of your legs. Their two thumbs are the weak point; your two arms working together are stronger than their two thumbs.

Follow-up. Your arms finish high, close to their face, ready for a downward elbow or hammer fist.

Kata link: Saifa

The opening of Saifa is the classic wrist-grab escape. The free hand covers the gripped hand, the body steps out at an angle, and both hands are pulled sharply in a tearing movement. The grip is broken against the thumb, your body is moved off the line, and the next movements, a back fist and a hammer fist, are the follow-up. It is a complete escape, distraction and counter in a few seconds.

15.4 Clothing Grabs

Clothing grabs, at the collar, lapel, sleeve or shoulder, are very common in real violence, and they are often a prelude to a punch.

The single lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing at chest height with their right hand and draws back their left to punch.

Immediate action. The punch is the danger, not the grab. Raise your left forearm to cover your head on the punching side, and step to the outside of their grabbing arm, as described in Chapter 3. Their grabbing arm is now between you and their punch.

Escape and control. Pin their grabbing hand against your chest with your right hand, and apply the inward wrist bend from Chapter 14 by bringing your forearm over their wrist. Or strike over the grabbing arm with your left elbow into their face.

The double lapel grab

The other person grabs your clothing with both hands, often pushing you back against a wall.

Escape. Both their hands are committed, which leaves their head and body open. Strike first, with a knee to the thigh or groin, or a palm heel to the chin between their arms. Then bring both your arms up between theirs and drive them outward, or turn and pass one of their arms to escape to the side.

The rear collar grab

The other person grabs your collar or hood from behind.

Escape. Turn quickly towards the grabbing hand, so that you face them and their grip twists. As you turn, raise your arm on that side to cover your head, and strike with an elbow as you come round.

15.5 Holds from the Front

The front bear hug over the arms

The other person wraps their arms around your body from the front, pinning your arms to your sides.

Escape. You still have your head, your hips and your legs. Drop your weight suddenly by bending your knees, which loosens the hold. Drive your hips back to create space. Strike with a knee to the groin or thigh, or a headbutt if justified. As the hold loosens, bring your hands up to their hips or chest and push away, stepping back at an angle.

The front bear hug under the arms

The other person wraps their arms around your body under your arms, leaving your arms free.

Escape. Your arms are free, so use them. Push their head back with a palm under the chin, or strike their face with elbows and palm heels. As their head goes back, their posture breaks, and you can push them away or use the outer reap from Chapter 11.

15.6 Holds from Behind

Attacks from behind are among the most frightening, because you cannot see them coming and your weapons all face the wrong way.

The rear bear hug over the arms

The other person wraps their arms around you from behind, pinning your arms.

Escape. Drop your weight immediately, bending your knees and widening your stance. This makes you harder to lift and loosens the hold. Strike backwards: stamp on their foot with your heel, drive a backward elbow into their ribs if your arm is free enough, or strike with the back of your head if their face is close. As the hold loosens, step to one side and turn, bringing your elbow round into their head or body.

The rear bear hug under the arms

The other person holds you from behind under your arms.

Escape. Your arms are free. Strike backward with elbows into their head and ribs. Attack their hands: peel back a finger or strike the back of the hand with your knuckles. Drop your weight and stamp on their foot. As the hold loosens, turn into them and strike, or step away.

15.7 Neck Attacks

Attacks on the neck are the most dangerous holds of all, because they can cause unconsciousness within seconds and death within minutes. Every martial artist should know how to escape them, and should treat any attack on the neck as an emergency requiring immediate and decisive action.

I want to stress something here. In this section, I describe escapes from neck attacks. I do not teach how to apply neck restraints, chokes or strangles, because they are among the most dangerous techniques that exist, and they need to be learned, if at all, under close supervision from a qualified instructor.

The front strangle with two hands

The other person grips your throat with both hands from the front.

Immediate action. Tuck your chin hard to protect your windpipe. Your priority is to break the grip before you lose consciousness.

Escape. Bring one arm up between their arms and over the top, turning your body sharply as you do, so that your arm and shoulder break the grip of one of their hands. Step to the side as you turn. Alternatively, strike immediately and hard to the groin, eyes or throat to make them release. Then step away and escape.

Many self-defence instructors also teach plucking at the thumbs to loosen the grip, but under real pressure, a turn of the whole body breaking the grip is usually more reliable.

The rear forearm around the neck

The other person wraps their forearm around your neck from behind. This is one of the most dangerous attacks there is, because it can render you unconscious very quickly.

Immediate action. Tuck your chin into the crook of their elbow, turning your head towards their elbow if you can, to protect your windpipe and arteries. Grab their forearm with both hands and pull down hard to create space at your neck.

Escape. Drop your weight and turn your body towards their elbow, so that your head slides out through the gap between their arm and body. As you turn, strike their groin or ribs with your elbow, or stamp on their foot. Once your head is free, turn to face them and get away.

This escape must be practised slowly and carefully, with a partner who releases the hold the instant you tap. Never apply pressure to a partner’s neck in training.

Street note: neck attacks are an emergency

An attack on the neck can kill. If someone is strangling you or holding you round the neck, you are entitled to use a high level of force to make them stop, because you are in genuine danger of serious harm or death. Do not hesitate. Strike hard to vulnerable targets, break the hold, and escape. Equally, never apply a neck restraint to someone unless you are in genuine fear for your life, because the risk of killing them is real.

15.8 Training Escapes Safely and Realistically

Escapes are only useful if they work under pressure, and pressure is hard to create safely. Here is a progression I use.

Stage one: cooperative. Your partner applies the grab or hold with moderate pressure. You perform the escape slowly, learning the mechanics.

Stage two: committed. Your partner applies the grab or hold firmly, as they would in real life, but does not resist the escape actively. You must make the escape work against a real grip.

Stage three: resisting. Your partner applies the grab firmly and tries to maintain it, adjusting as you try to escape. You must use distraction, timing and follow-up to get free. Strikes are light and controlled.

Stage four: scenario. Your partner approaches with a realistic attack, such as a shove and a grab, a grab and a punch, or a grab from behind as you walk. You must respond without knowing exactly what is coming.

Never progress to resisting or scenario training with neck holds. Neck escapes are always trained at stage one or two, with immediate release on the tap.

Drill 15.1: Grab circuit

Your partner works through a sequence of grabs: same-side wrist, cross wrist, two-handed wrist, single lapel, double lapel, front bear hug, rear bear hug. For each, you escape and follow up with one light strike to a pad your partner holds, then step away at an angle. Then swap roles.

Do the circuit three times. The first time is cooperative, the second committed, the third with light resistance. Keep the neck holds out of the resisting round.

Drill 15.2: The surprise grab

You walk slowly around the mat. At a random moment, your partner, approaching from any direction, grabs you in one of the ways described in this chapter. You must escape and move away within three seconds.

This drill trains the most important skill of all: overcoming the freeze. The first few times, you will probably hesitate. After a few sessions, your response becomes faster and more automatic.

Fault: pulling against the fingers

The instinctive response to a wrist grab is to pull straight back, against the fingers. Against a strong person, this simply does not work, and it wastes energy and time.

Correction: Always find the thumb. Turn the narrow edge of your wrist towards it and use your elbow as a lever. If you find yourself pulling straight back, stop, find the thumb, and rotate.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My partner is much stronger than me and I can’t break their grip.”

Solution: Strength is not the answer. Use the weak point (the thumb), use leverage (rotate and use the elbow), and above all use distraction. A sharp strike to the face or a stamp on the foot makes almost anyone loosen their grip for a moment. Escape in that moment.

Problem: “When I’m grabbed from behind I panic.”

Solution: That is entirely normal, and the answer is repetition. Practise Drill 15.2 regularly, starting with gentle grabs and gradually making them firmer and more sudden. The freeze response reduces with familiarity. Also practise dropping your weight as your very first response to any rear grab; having one simple, automatic first move makes the rest easier.

Problem: “I escape the grab but then my partner just grabs me again.”

Solution: You are escaping without acting. Every escape should be followed immediately by a strike, a step to the outside, or leaving. Practise escapes with a follow-up every time, never on their own.

Problem: “I’m worried about practising neck escapes.”

Solution: Your caution is wise. Practise neck escapes only at the cooperative and committed stages, never with resistance. Your partner should apply very light pressure and release instantly when you tap or say stop. Most of the learning is in the movements, not in the pressure.

15.9 Bringing It Together

This chapter completes Part Five. We have studied the principles of joint control, the specific controls of the wrist, elbow and shoulder, and now the escapes from grabs, holds and neck attacks.

The thread running through all three chapters is leverage: using the weak points of a joint or a grip, the long lever of a limb, and the strength of the whole body rather than the arms alone. Joint controls and escapes are two sides of the same coin. Once you understand how to control a joint, you understand how to escape from someone controlling yours.

In Part Six, the final part of the book, we bring everything together: timing and counter-attacking, sparring at close range, and the realities of real-world encounters.

Key Points

  • Grabs and holds are among the most common forms of real violence, and often a prelude to something worse.
  • The weak point of a hand grip is the gap between the thumb and the fingers. Move towards the thumb.
  • Use leverage and rotation, not strength. Use the elbow as a lever.
  • Distract, escape, act. An escape without a follow-up leaves you where you started.
  • Against a lapel grab and punch, deal with the punch first by moving to the outside.
  • Against rear holds, drop your weight first, then strike and turn.
  • Neck attacks are emergencies. Tuck the chin, break the hold decisively and escape.
  • Train escapes progressively, but never with resistance on neck holds.

Training Questions

  1. Ask three partners of different sizes to grab your wrist firmly. Which grip is hardest to escape? What makes it hard?
  2. Perform the opening of Saifa slowly against a real wrist grab. Does the kata movement work as an escape? What adjustments do you need?
  3. After a session of Drill 15.2, write down how long you think you hesitated on each grab. How does this change over a month of practice?

Timing and the Counter-Attack

Everything in this book depends on timing. A trap applied a fraction too late meets an arm that has already moved. A throw attempted a moment too early meets a person who is still balanced. A counter launched at the right instant, however, can turn the other person’s attack into their undoing. This chapter is about the timing of close combat: when to act, when to wait, and how to make the other person’s own actions open the door for your counter-attack.

There is a story, told in several versions in different martial traditions, about a young student who asked an old master what the most important technique was. The master thought for a while and said, “The one that arrives at the right moment.” The student, disappointed, asked again: “But which technique is that?” And the master replied, “It changes every time.”

It sounds like the kind of riddle that martial arts teachers enjoy a little too much, but it contains a hard truth. Technique matters, but timing matters more. A mediocre technique at the perfect moment will beat a perfect technique at the wrong moment almost every time. And timing is the hardest thing in martial arts to teach, because it cannot be shown in a photograph or a diagram. It has to be felt.

What I can do in this chapter is give you a framework for understanding timing, a set of principles that make it easier to develop, and drills that train it. The rest comes from practice.

16.1 The Problem: Attacking a Ready Opponent

The fundamental problem of timing is this: a person who is ready for your attack can usually defend it. They are balanced, their guard is up, their attention is on you. Whatever you throw at them, they have a good chance of blocking, evading or countering.

There are moments, however, when the other person is not ready. They are in the middle of their own attack, committed to a movement, recovering from a previous action, distracted, tired, or simply between thoughts. In those moments, even a simple technique can land cleanly.

Timing is the art of finding and using those moments.

16.2 The Principle: The Three Initiatives

Japanese martial arts describe timing in terms of sen, a word usually translated as “initiative”. There are three classic forms, and they are worth understanding, because they describe three fundamentally different ways of beating an opponent in time.

Go no sen: responding after the attack

Go no sen, sometimes translated as “late initiative”, means responding to an attack after it has been launched. The other person attacks, you defend, and then you counter-attack.

This is the most basic form of timing and the one most karate training emphasises: block, then counter. It is relatively easy to learn, because you have time to see the attack and respond. Its weakness is that it gives the other person the first move, and if their attack is fast or unexpected, you may not have time to defend.

At close range, go no sen works best when your defence and counter are very close together in time, ideally overlapping. A block that is immediately followed by a strike, or a cover that turns straight into an elbow, as in Drill 7.2, is go no sen done well.

Sen no sen: meeting the attack

Sen no sen, sometimes translated as “simultaneous initiative”, means responding at the same moment as the attack. As the other person attacks, you counter-attack, so that your counter lands while their attack is still developing or as it misses.

This is much more effective than go no sen, because the other person is committed to their attack and cannot defend against your counter. It is also much harder, because you must read the attack early and act without hesitation.

At close range, sen no sen often takes the form of a counter that combines defence and attack in a single movement: an angled step that avoids a punch while striking, a forearm that deflects a hook while the elbow of the same arm strikes, or a trap on the attacking arm that pulls the attacker onto your strike.

Sen sen no sen: anticipating the attack

Sen sen no sen, sometimes translated as “pre-emptive initiative”, means acting before the attack has been launched, at the moment the other person has decided to attack but before their body has begun to move. You sense the intention and strike first.

This is the highest level of timing and the most effective, but it is also the most difficult. It requires reading the other person’s intention from very subtle signals: a shift of weight, a tightening of the shoulders, a change in breathing, a narrowing of the eyes. At close range, where you are in contact with them, the sensitivity from kakie makes this much more achievable than it would be at long range.

16.3 Reading the Attack

To respond in time, you must know an attack is coming. At close range, you rarely see it start, so you must learn to read the signals that come before it.

Visual signals

Even at close range, there are visual signals. A shoulder dipping or drawing back before a punch. An elbow lifting before a hook. The head dropping before a tackle or a headbutt. The weight shifting onto the back foot before a front kick or knee. Eyes glancing at a target. The face tightening before a strike.

Tactile signals

When you are in contact, through kakie, a trap, a clinch or a grab, you can feel signals before you see them. The other person’s arm tenses before it moves. Their weight shifts, which you can feel through the contact. Their grip tightens or loosens. Their body draws back slightly before driving forward.

Rhythm signals

Most people have a rhythm in their movements and attacks. They bounce, step, breathe and attack in patterns. Once you have felt someone’s rhythm, you can anticipate when the next attack is likely, and you can also disrupt their rhythm to create openings.

Verbal and behavioural signals

Outside the dojo, there are other signals: a change in tone of voice, a sudden silence, a glance at friends, clenched fists, a step forward, removing glasses or jewellery, a change from shouting to quiet. These pre-attack indicators are very important in real-world encounters, and we return to them in Chapter 18.

Drill 16.1: Reading the tell

Your partner stands in front of you at close range, with gloves on. They will throw a single, light punch at a moment of their choosing. Your task is to say “now” at the moment you sense the punch is coming, before it lands.

At first, you will usually say it too late. Over time, you will start to pick up the small signals that come before the punch. When you can reliably say “now” before the punch starts, replace the word with a cover. Then with a counter.

Do this drill in contact as well: start with your forearm touching your partner’s. You will find the tactile signals much easier to read than the visual ones.

16.4 The Moments of Opportunity

Beyond the attack itself, there are other moments when the other person is vulnerable. These are the moments to look for, whether you are counter-attacking or attacking first.

When they attack. Their weapon is committed and their target is open. This is the classic counter-attack moment.

When they finish an attack. After a punch or kick, there is a moment when the weapon is extended or returning and the guard is not yet back in place.

When they move. During a step, their weight is on one foot and their base is unstable.

When they breathe in. The body is slightly less braced during an in-breath than an out-breath. This is subtle, but experienced fighters notice it.

When they are surprised. A sudden noise, movement or change can distract them for a moment.

When they are hurt. After a successful strike, there is usually a brief moment of shock or pain. This is the moment to follow up, which is why combinations are so effective.

When they think. When someone is deciding what to do next, they are not fully ready. Long pauses, changes of plan and moments of hesitation are all openings.

16.5 Counter-Attacks at Close Range

Let us look at the main ways of counter-attacking at close range, organised by type of attack.

Against a straight punch

A straight punch at close range is short and fast, and it comes along the line of attack.

The angled counter combines an angled step with a strike. As the punch comes, step slightly to the outside, letting it pass close to your head, and strike at the same moment with your rear hand or an elbow. This is sen no sen.

The catch and pull traps the punching arm as it extends and pulls it further, drawing the attacker onto your strike or into a throw. This is the hooking pull from Chapter 5 used as a counter.

Against a hook

A hook comes from the side and is most dangerous at the end of its arc.

The cover and return covers the hook with an elbow guard and returns immediately with a horizontal elbow from the same arm, as in Drill 7.2. This is go no sen, but very close to sen no sen if done quickly.

The step-in jam steps into the hook, meeting the arm near the shoulder where it has little power, and strikes with the other hand or an elbow. This is sen no sen and is the counter described in Chapter 3.

Against a push

A push is committed and extended, and the pusher’s weight is moving forward.

The pivot and pass pivots off the line, letting the push go past, and strikes or throws from the side. This uses the push’s own momentum against the pusher.

The pull through takes the pushing arm and pulls it in the direction of the push, unbalancing the pusher and drawing them onto a knee or strike.

Against a grab

A grab fixes the grabber’s hand and commits one of their arms.

The strike over the grab strikes immediately with the free hand while the grabber’s hand is still occupied. Their grabbing arm cannot defend.

The lock the grab applies a joint control to the grabbing hand, as in Chapter 14.

Against a knee or kick

A knee or kick puts the attacker on one leg.

The jam and sweep jams the knee at the hip before it develops, then sweeps the standing leg or pushes the attacker over.

The catch and dump catches the kicking leg and takes the attacker down, as in the leg catch in Chapter 11.

Kata link: the opening of the kata

Almost every Goju-Ryu kata begins with a defensive movement. The common interpretation is that “there is no first attack in karate”, and that the kata teach you to respond rather than initiate. That is true as far as it goes, but look closely and you will find that many of the opening “blocks” are counter-attacks in themselves: they deflect and strike at the same moment, or they take control of the attacking limb in a way that leads directly to a counter. The kata are full of sen no sen.

16.6 Drawing the Attack

An experienced fighter does not simply wait for the other person to attack. They draw the attack, creating an opening that invites the other person to attack in a predictable way, and then counter-attack.

The deliberate opening

Lower your guard slightly on one side, so that the other person sees an opening. When they attack it, you are ready, because you know exactly where the attack is going.

The feint

Make a small movement that looks like the start of an attack. The other person reacts, by blocking, flinching or counter-attacking, and their reaction creates an opening or a predictable attack that you can counter.

The pressure

Move forward steadily, closing the distance and crowding the other person. Many people, when pressured, attack to relieve the pressure, and their attack is often rushed and predictable.

The rhythm break

Establish a rhythm with your movement and then break it suddenly. The other person adjusts to your rhythm and is caught out when it changes.

Drawing the attack is a sophisticated skill, and it carries risk: an opening that you offer deliberately is still an opening. But it is the bridge between go no sen and sen no sen, because it makes the attack predictable enough to meet as it comes.

Drill 16.2: Open the door

Your partner, wearing gloves, attacks with a single, light strike whenever they see an opening. You deliberately leave one opening at a time, for example by lowering your right hand, and counter-attack whenever they attack it.

Start by telling your partner where the opening will be. Then stop telling them, and let them find it. The aim is to learn how to make the other person attack where you want them to, and to be ready when they do.

16.7 Timing in Combinations

Timing is not just about the first action. It applies to every action in a combination.

The best combinations have a rhythm that breaks the other person’s rhythm. A fast, fast, slow combination, or a slow, fast, fast one, is often more effective than three actions at the same speed, because the change of pace catches the other person out.

Similarly, the moment between combinations matters. After a combination, the other person often expects you to pause or retreat. Continuing at that moment, or starting a new combination immediately, can catch them unprepared.

And the moment of their recovery is crucial. After you have landed a strike or broken their balance, there is a brief window before they recover. The next action must arrive within that window. If it arrives after, they will be ready for it.

16.8 Developing Timing

Timing cannot be learned from a book, but it can be developed systematically. Here are the most effective methods I know.

Partner drills with a single attack. Drills like 16.1 and 16.2, in which one partner attacks once and the other responds, build the ability to read and respond to attacks.

Focus mitt work. A skilled pad holder can call strikes and combinations with unpredictable timing, and can also throw light strikes at you to test your defence and counter. This is one of the best timing exercises there is.

Kakie. The constant flow of push and yield in kakie develops a sense of rhythm and an ability to feel the moment when your partner’s pressure changes. It is the ideal foundation for close-range timing.

Sparring. Ultimately, timing is developed in sparring, where you face a partner whose timing is unpredictable. We look at close-range sparring in the next chapter.

Patience. Timing improves slowly, over months and years. It is one of the last skills to mature, and it keeps improving long after technique has stopped developing.

Street note: the pre-emptive strike

In a real-world encounter, waiting for the other person to attack first can be very dangerous. If you genuinely believe an attack is imminent and that you are in danger, UK law allows you to act first, provided your action is reasonable. You do not have to wait to be hit. This is sen sen no sen in its most practical form. But the belief must be honest and the action proportionate, and leaving or de-escalating should always be your first choice if it is possible. We discuss this in detail in Chapter 18.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “I always react too late.”

Solution: You are probably watching the wrong thing. At close range, do not watch the hands; they move too fast. Watch the centre of the chest and shoulders, where the movement begins, and use contact wherever possible to feel it. Work through Drill 16.1 regularly.

Problem: “When I try to counter at the same time as the attack, I get hit.”

Solution: Your counter is not also defending you. At close range, a sen no sen counter must combine defence and attack: an angle that takes you off the line, a cover that protects your head, or a trap that controls the attacking arm. A counter that does not do one of these things is just a trade of blows.

Problem: “My partner keeps feinting and I fall for it every time.”

Solution: You are reacting to every movement. Keep your structure and guard, and react only to movements that are actually threatening you. A feint that does not come close to you can be ignored. Also, use feints as opportunities: when your partner feints, they are not defending, and that is a moment to attack.

Problem: “I can’t seem to draw attacks; my partner just waits.”

Solution: Some people are very patient. Use pressure instead: step forward and crowd them, so that they feel they must act. Or switch to attacking first, using feints to make them react and countering their reaction.

16.9 Bringing It Together

Timing is the art of acting at the moment when the other person cannot respond. It can be understood through the three initiatives, go no sen, sen no sen and sen sen no sen, which describe responding after, during and before the attack.

To time your actions, you must read the attack through visual, tactile, rhythmic and behavioural signals. You must know the moments of opportunity and use them. You must learn to counter each type of attack in a way that combines defence and attack. And you must learn to draw the attack, so that it comes where and when you expect.

None of this can be learned quickly. But with patient practice, especially in kakie and partner drills, it develops steadily, and it transforms everything else you do.

Key Points

  • Timing matters more than technique. A simple technique at the right moment beats a perfect one at the wrong moment.
  • Go no sen responds after the attack; sen no sen meets it; sen sen no sen anticipates it.
  • Read attacks through visual, tactile, rhythmic and behavioural signals. At close range, touch is faster than sight.
  • The moments of opportunity include attacks, recoveries, steps, breaths, surprises, pain and hesitation.
  • Close-range counters must combine defence and attack: angle, cover or trap as you strike.
  • Draw the attack with deliberate openings, feints, pressure and rhythm breaks.
  • Develop timing through single-attack drills, focus mitts, kakie and sparring, and be patient.

Training Questions

  1. In Drill 16.1, what was the earliest signal you were able to detect? Was it visual or tactile?
  2. Choose the opening movement of three kata you know. Is each best understood as go no sen, sen no sen or sen sen no sen?
  3. In your next sparring session, try to identify your partner’s rhythm. When do they usually attack? What do they do just before?

Bringing It to Sparring: Progressive Close-Range Kumite

Drills build skills. Sparring tests them. Without sparring, close-range skill remains a set of cooperative patterns that may or may not survive contact with a resisting person. But close-range sparring is also where people get hurt, where egos flare, and where training partners are lost. This chapter sets out a progressive, safe method for bringing everything in this book into live practice, so that you can pressure-test your skills without losing your partners or your health.

I have seen close-range sparring done very badly. In one club I visited, the instructor’s idea of close-range training was to put two people in a corner and tell them to “sort it out”. The result was a red-faced brawl, a bloody nose, a twisted knee and two people who did not speak to each other for the rest of the evening. Nobody learned anything, except perhaps not to come back.

I have also seen it done very well. In the best clubs, close-range sparring is a structured, progressive, intelligent process. Partners start with very limited goals and very light contact, and gradually add techniques, intensity and freedom as their skill and trust develop. People come out of these sessions tired, sweaty, grinning and better than they were an hour before.

The difference is not the techniques. It is the method. This chapter gives you the method.

17.1 The Problem: Pressure Without Injury

The problem with sparring is that it must provide realistic pressure without causing injury. Too little pressure, and the sparring becomes a cooperative drill that teaches nothing new. Too much, and people get hurt, become frightened, and stop learning.

At close range, this problem is particularly acute. The techniques of close combat, elbows, knees, throws, joint controls, headbutts, are among the most dangerous in martial arts. They cannot be used at full power on a training partner. Yet if they are never tested against resistance, you will never know whether you can use them.

The solution is to limit sparring in carefully chosen ways, so that each kind of sparring tests a particular skill under realistic pressure while removing the most dangerous elements.

17.2 The Principle: Constrain, Then Free

The key idea behind progressive sparring is that every sparring game has constraints: limits on what techniques are allowed, how hard they can be applied, what the goals are, how long a round lasts, and so on. By choosing the constraints carefully, you can create a game that tests a specific skill with realistic pressure and acceptable risk.

As skill develops, the constraints are relaxed one at a time. A game that allows only pushing becomes a game that allows pushing and light strikes. A game with one target becomes a game with three. A game at quarter intensity becomes a game at half intensity.

This approach has several advantages. It keeps risk low, because dangerous techniques are introduced only when people are ready for them. It focuses learning, because each game tests a particular skill. And it builds trust, because partners know what to expect.

17.3 The Agreement

Before any close-range sparring, partners must agree on the following. I insist on this in my classes, even between people who have trained together for years.

Allowed techniques. Exactly which techniques may be used.

Targets. Exactly which targets may be struck.

Contact level. How hard strikes may land, usually described as a percentage: touch, light (around a quarter of full power), medium (around half). Full contact is never used in close-range sparring in my classes.

Protection. What equipment is worn: gloves, head guards, mouthguards, groin guards, shin guards, body protectors.

The tap. A clear signal for stopping, usually a tap on the partner or the floor, or the word “stop”. On the tap, both partners stop immediately.

The goal. What each person is trying to achieve.

The round length. Usually between thirty seconds and two minutes.

If either partner is unhappy with any part of the agreement, it is changed. If either partner breaks the agreement, for example by hitting harder than agreed, the round stops immediately and the agreement is restated.

Fault: escalation

The most common problem in sparring is escalation. One partner hits slightly harder than agreed, the other responds slightly harder still, and within a minute both are hitting much harder than either intended. Nobody decides to escalate; it just happens.

Correction: Stop the round as soon as you notice it, restate the agreement, and start again. If it keeps happening with a particular partner, train with someone else for a while. Experienced partners can help by deliberately keeping their contact slightly below the agreed level.

17.4 The Progression

Here is the progression I use. Each stage is a family of games, and you should spend weeks or months at each before moving on. You will also return to earlier stages regularly, as warm-ups and to sharpen particular skills.

Stage one: positional games

At the first stage, there is no striking at all. The games are about position: structure, balance, inside control and angles.

The pushing square (Drill 2.2): push your partner out of a square.

The shoulder game (Drill 4.2): touch your partner’s shoulder in kakie.

Owning the outside (Drill 3.2): gain the outside of your partner’s lead arm.

Clinch position (Chapter 9): gain inside control and break your partner’s posture.

Off the wall (Drill 9.2): escape from a wall or keep your partner pinned.

These games are safe, very tiring and extremely instructive. They develop the foundations without the stress of being hit.

Stage two: touch games

At the second stage, strikes are introduced, but only as touches. A touch scores; a strike that lands harder than a touch is a foul.

Touch kakie: free kakie in which an open-hand touch to the face or body scores.

Touch knees (Drill 8.3): touch your partner’s thigh or body with your knee from the clinch.

Trap and touch: trap your partner’s arm and touch them with the free hand.

Touch games teach timing and targeting without fear. They also teach control, which is essential for everything that follows.

Stage three: light contact with limited techniques

At the third stage, strikes are allowed at light contact, but the range of techniques is limited. Each game focuses on a particular group of techniques.

Hands only: short punches, palm heels and back fists to the body and head guard, at light contact.

Hands and knees: as above, plus knees to the thigh with thigh protection.

Traps and strikes: strikes that score only if preceded by a trap.

Clinch and knees: clinch control and knees to the thigh and body.

Stage four: throws and takedowns

At the fourth stage, throws are introduced, on mats, with competent breakfalls. Strikes may or may not be included.

Throwing games: start in a clinch and try to throw your partner. No strikes. A throw scores; you then reset.

Sweep games: try to sweep your partner’s foot. No strikes, no other throws.

Strike to throw: light strikes are allowed, and a throw that follows a strike scores extra.

Stage five: integrated close-range sparring

At the fifth stage, most of the techniques in this book are allowed, at light to medium contact, with full protection. The goal is to integrate structure, sensitivity, trapping, short strikes, clinch work and throws into a continuous exchange.

Even at this stage, some techniques are never used at speed or force: elbows to the head (allowed only as touches to the head guard, if at all), knees to the head, headbutts, stamps to the knee, strikes to the groin, joint controls applied quickly, and neck attacks of any kind.

Stage six: scenario training

At the sixth stage, sparring is placed in realistic scenarios: an aggressive person approaching, a grab from behind, a push against a wall, two attackers, a confined space. The goal is to test your skills under the stress of a realistic situation, and to practise the awareness, de-escalation and escape skills of Chapter 18.

Scenario training must be very carefully controlled, with clear rules, plenty of protection and close supervision, because the stress of the scenario makes people more likely to lose control.

Drill 17.1: The ten-minute ladder

A short session that climbs the ladder. Two minutes of the pushing square. Two minutes of touch kakie. Two minutes of hands-only light sparring at close range. Two minutes of clinch and touch knees. Two minutes of throwing games on mats.

Rest for thirty seconds between each section. This drill is an excellent end to a session, because it revisits every stage in a short time and shows you where your skills are strongest and weakest.

17.5 Starting Positions

One problem with close-range sparring is that, if both partners start at a distance, much of the round is spent getting to close range, and some people will avoid it altogether. Using close-range starting positions solves this.

Kakie start: both partners begin with forearms in contact, in the kakie position.

Clinch start: both partners begin in a collar-and-elbow clinch or a double underhook position.

Grab start: one partner begins holding the other’s wrist, lapel or shoulder.

Wall start: one partner begins with their back to a padded wall, the other pressing.

Fence start: one partner holds a fence (hands up, open, in front), the other at close range in front of them. This is particularly useful for self-defence scenarios.

By choosing the starting position, you can focus the sparring on the skills you want to develop.

17.6 Equipment

For close-range sparring, appropriate protective equipment is essential.

Gloves. MMA-style open-fingered gloves allow grabbing and trapping while offering some protection to the hands and the partner’s face. Boxing gloves offer more protection but make trapping and grabbing difficult.

Head guard. A good head guard, ideally with cheek protection, reduces the risk of cuts and some impacts, though it does not prevent concussion.

Mouthguard. Always. A properly fitted mouthguard protects the teeth and reduces the risk of jaw injuries.

Groin guard. Always, for both men and women.

Shin and thigh protection. For any game involving knees or low kicks.

Body protector. For games involving knees or elbows to the body.

Mats. For any game involving throws, sweeps or takedowns.

17.7 Partners

Your choice of training partners matters enormously. Good partners make you better; bad partners make you worse, or injured.

Train with a variety of partners: big and small, strong and fast, experienced and new, men and women. Each will teach you something different.

Train with people you trust. At close range, you are putting your safety in your partner’s hands. Choose people who keep to the agreement and look after you.

Be a good partner yourself. Keep to the agreement, control your contact, tap and release promptly, and give your partner the chance to succeed sometimes. The best training happens when both partners are working at the edge of their ability, not when one is crushing the other.

Match intensity to experience. Experienced practitioners working with beginners should keep their intensity well below their maximum and give the beginner room to learn.

17.8 Learning from Sparring

Sparring is only useful if you learn from it. Here are some ways to make sure you do.

Have a focus. Before each round, choose one thing to work on: a particular trap, a counter, a position. You will learn far more from a focused round than from a round in which you try everything.

Review afterwards. After each round, take a moment to ask yourself what worked, what did not, and why. Talk to your partner about it.

Film yourself. Video is brutally honest. You will see habits you never knew you had.

Go back to drills. When sparring reveals a weakness, go back to the drills in this book that address it. Sparring tells you what to practise; drills are how you practise it.

Keep a training diary. Write down what you worked on, what you noticed and what you want to improve. Over months, the diary will show you patterns.

Drill 17.2: Focused rounds

Before each round of light close-range sparring, each partner writes down one specific goal on a card, such as “land a palm pin followed by a strike” or “gain the outside line three times”. After the round, each partner reports whether they achieved it, and the partner gives feedback.

Swap goals and partners regularly. This simple structure makes sparring far more productive than open, unfocused rounds.

17.9 Sport Rules and Close Range

If you compete in sport karate, close-range sparring will help you even though many rule sets limit close-range work. You will be better at handling opponents who rush in, more comfortable when the exchange closes, and more able to use the few close-range techniques that are allowed, such as sweeps, short punches and quick traps.

In rule sets that allow more close-range work, such as knockdown karate, some traditional formats and mixed rules, the skills in this book become much more directly useful. Adapt the progression above to your rule set: if knees are allowed, include them at an appropriate stage; if throws are restricted, focus on the ones that are permitted.

17.10 Mental Skills in Sparring

Close-range sparring is psychologically demanding. Being close to someone who is trying to hit, throw or control you is stressful, and stress affects performance. Sparring is an opportunity to train the mental skills that will serve you in real encounters.

Breathing. Use the Sanchin breathing from Chapter 2 to stay calm. Breathe out on effort and keep breathing throughout.

Relaxation. Notice tension in your shoulders, jaw and hands, and release it. Tension slows you down and tires you out.

Attention. Keep your attention on your partner and on the contact between you, not on your own thoughts or fears.

Acceptance. You will be hit, thrown and controlled. That is part of learning. Accept it, learn from it and move on.

Enjoyment. Close-range sparring, done well, is enormously enjoyable. The intimacy, the problem-solving, the flow between partners can be a genuine pleasure. If you are not enjoying it at least some of the time, something needs to change.

Street note: sparring is not fighting

Sparring, however realistic, is not a real fight. There are rules, protection, a cooperative partner and no real intention to harm. A real encounter has none of these. Sparring builds skills and confidence, but it does not fully prepare you for the shock, chaos and fear of real violence. Scenario training helps bridge the gap, but the best preparation for real violence is the awareness and avoidance skills in the next chapter.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “My partner keeps hitting harder than we agreed.”

Solution: Stop the round immediately and restate the agreement. If it happens again, stop again. If it continues, stop training with that partner and speak to your instructor. A partner who cannot keep to an agreement is a danger to you.

Problem: “I freeze up in sparring and can’t do anything I’ve practised.”

Solution: You have moved up the ladder too quickly. Go back to the positional and touch games, where the stress is lower, and build your confidence there. Use the breathing and relaxation techniques. And make sure each round has a single, simple focus, so that you are not trying to think of everything at once.

Problem: “Close-range sparring always turns into wrestling.”

Solution: That is very common when the rules are vague. Use specific constraints: for example, a rule that the clinch can be held for only five seconds before the partners separate, or a rule that a throw must be attempted within ten seconds of clinching. Also make sure both partners have a clear goal for the round.

Problem: “I’m much smaller than most of my partners and I always lose.”

Solution: You may lose more often, but you are learning more. Use the constraints to give yourself a chance: for example, a game in which you score by touching your partner and they score only by pushing you out of a square. Ask larger partners to work at a lower intensity with you. And notice what does work for you; those techniques are your strengths.

17.11 Bringing It Together

Sparring is where close-range skill is tested and refined. Done well, it is safe, progressive and enjoyable, and it transforms cooperative patterns into genuine ability. Done badly, it is dangerous and teaches very little.

The method is simple: constrain, then free. Begin with positional games, move to touch games, then light contact with limited techniques, then throws, then integrated sparring, and finally scenarios. Agree the rules before every round. Wear the right equipment. Choose good partners and be a good partner. And learn from every round.

The final chapter steps outside the dojo, into the real world, where there are no agreements, no protection and no rules.

Key Points

  • Sparring must provide realistic pressure without injury. Constraints make this possible.
  • Agree techniques, targets, contact, protection, the tap, the goal and the round length before every round.
  • Climb the ladder: positional games, touch games, light contact with limited techniques, throws, integrated sparring, scenarios.
  • Use close-range starting positions to focus sparring on the skills you want.
  • Never use dangerous techniques at speed or force in sparring: elbows and knees to the head, headbutts, stamps to the knee, groin strikes, fast joint controls or neck attacks.
  • Choose trustworthy partners of all kinds, and be a good partner yourself.
  • Learn from sparring with focused rounds, review, video and a training diary.

Training Questions

  1. Write out a sparring agreement for your next session, covering all seven elements. Discuss it with your partner before you start.
  2. Which rung of the ladder are you currently on? What would you need to be able to do before moving to the next?
  3. After a month of focused rounds (Drill 17.2), review your cards. Which goals did you achieve most often? Which least? What does that tell you?

Real-World Encounters: Awareness, the Law and the Aftermath

Everything in this book has been leading here. But the real world is not a dojo, and the skills that matter most in a genuine encounter are not the ones we have spent most of our time on. Awareness, avoidance and de-escalation will keep you safe far more often than any trap, elbow or throw. This final chapter looks honestly at what real confrontations are like, how to stay out of them, how to manage them when you cannot, what the law in the United Kingdom expects of you, and how to cope with the aftermath.

I want to begin this chapter with a confession. In more than thirty years of training, I have used physical technique against another person outside the dojo only a handful of times. On each occasion it was brief, untidy and over within seconds, and on each occasion I could probably have avoided it entirely if I had been paying more attention a minute earlier.

Far more often, I have used the skills that come before technique: noticing that something was wrong, crossing the road, leaving a pub when the atmosphere changed, talking calmly to someone who was shouting, standing in a way that discouraged trouble without inviting it. None of these would look impressive in a demonstration. All of them worked.

Most experienced practitioners, and most people whose work brings them into contact with violence, such as door staff, police officers, paramedics and nurses, will tell you the same thing. The fight you avoid is the one you win most completely.

18.1 The Problem: Violence Is Not Like Training

Real violence differs from training in almost every way that matters.

It is sudden. There is often little or no warning, and the first blow may land before you have realised what is happening.

It is chaotic. People do not attack in neat, predictable ways. They grab, push, swing, fall, scream and flail.

It is frightening. The adrenaline response floods your body, narrowing your vision, distorting your sense of time, shaking your hands, emptying your mind and making fine skills almost impossible.

It is unfair. The other person may be bigger, armed, drunk, high or accompanied by friends. They will not stop when you tap.

It is legally consequential. Whatever happens, you may have to explain and justify your actions to the police and possibly a court.

And it is physically and emotionally costly, even when you win. Injuries, legal stress, fear and guilt can last for years.

None of this is meant to frighten you. It is meant to set your priorities. In the real world, the goal is not to win a fight but to get home safely, with as little harm as possible to you, to the people you care about and, as far as reasonable, to the other person.

18.2 The Principle: The Layers of Protection

I think of personal safety as a series of layers, like the walls of a castle. Each layer, if it holds, means you never need the next one. Physical technique is the last layer, not the first.

Layer one: awareness

Awareness means noticing what is going on around you. It is the foundation of everything else, because you cannot avoid a threat you have not seen.

Awareness does not mean being paranoid or constantly scanning for danger. It means being reasonably alert, especially in situations where trouble is more likely: late at night, around alcohol, in unfamiliar places, at times of tension. It means having your head up rather than buried in your phone. It means noticing when someone’s behaviour seems out of place, when an atmosphere changes, when a person is paying unusual attention to you.

A useful habit is to notice the exits whenever you enter a room, the people around you when you stop somewhere, and the route ahead when you are walking. This takes no more than a moment and quickly becomes automatic.

Layer two: avoidance

Avoidance means staying away from trouble once you have noticed it. Cross the road. Leave the pub. Take a different route. Get off the bus at the next stop. Walk into a shop. Avoidance costs you a little time and a little pride, and it is the most effective self-defence technique in existence.

Many people find avoidance hard because it feels like backing down. It is not. It is choosing not to take part in someone else’s problem. There is no shame in walking away from a situation that could have ended with someone in hospital or prison.

Layer three: de-escalation

De-escalation means calming a situation that has already begun. If someone is angry, shouting or threatening you, your words, tone and body language can often bring the temperature down enough for everyone to walk away.

Layer four: escape

If de-escalation fails, or is not possible, escape: create distance and leave. Physical technique at this layer is used only to create the opportunity to escape, not to win.

Layer five: physical defence

Only if all else has failed, and you or someone else is in genuine danger, do you use physical technique. And even then, it is used to stop the threat and create an opportunity to escape, not to punish or dominate.

18.3 Awareness in Practice

Awareness is a habit, and like all habits it can be trained.

Notice the environment. Exits, obstacles, lighting, other people, places where you could be cornered.

Notice people. Most people around you are going about their business. Pay attention to those who are not: people loitering without obvious purpose, people watching you, people whose behaviour changes as you approach, groups that separate as you pass.

Notice your own state. Are you tired, distracted, intoxicated, upset? Each of these reduces your awareness and your ability to respond. Adjust accordingly.

Notice changes. The atmosphere in a pub as the evening goes on. A conversation that is becoming heated. A group that has gone quiet. Changes are often more significant than any single observation.

Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, it probably is. You do not need to justify the feeling or prove it. Simply act on it, by leaving, moving, or becoming more alert.

18.4 Recognising the Build-Up

Most violence that is not a sudden ambush follows a recognisable build-up. Knowing the signs gives you time to act.

Verbal escalation. Raised voices, insults, challenges (“What are you looking at?”), repeated questions, swearing directed at you.

Physical posturing. Puffing up the chest, squaring the shoulders, standing very close, pointing or jabbing a finger, invading your space.

Pre-attack indicators. These are the signs that an attack may be imminent, and they are particularly important:

  • a sudden change from loud to quiet, or from moving to still;
  • looking around, as if checking for witnesses or police;
  • glancing at a target on your body;
  • clenching fists, or tensing the jaw;
  • blading the body, turning one shoulder forward;
  • shifting weight, or taking a step back to make room;
  • removing glasses, a hat, a jacket or jewellery;
  • a friend moving to your side or behind you;
  • the face going pale or flushed, the breathing becoming fast and shallow.

These signs are not proof that an attack is coming, but when you see several of them together, you should assume it may be and act accordingly.

18.5 De-escalation

De-escalation is a large subject, and many people whose work involves dealing with aggression receive specific training in it. Here are the essentials.

Stay calm. Your calm is contagious, and so is your agitation. Slow your breathing, lower your voice and relax your face.

Do not challenge. Avoid anything that might feel like a challenge: staring, raising your voice, arguing, insulting, making demands.

Acknowledge. Many angry people want to feel heard. Acknowledging their feelings (“I can see you’re really upset”) often lowers the temperature, without agreeing with them or accepting blame.

Offer a way out. People are more likely to back down if they can do so without losing face. Give them an exit: “Look, mate, I don’t want any trouble. Let’s both just get on with our night.”

Apologise if it costs nothing. If you have accidentally bumped into someone, spilled a drink or looked at them the wrong way, a sincere apology can end the matter. It is not weakness; it is good sense.

Keep your distance. While talking, keep a safe distance, ideally just outside arm’s reach, and keep your hands up in a non-threatening position (see the fence below).

Know when to stop. De-escalation does not always work. If the other person is not responding, or the pre-attack indicators are building, stop talking and prepare to leave or defend yourself.

18.6 The Fence

The fence is a concept developed and popularised by self-protection instructors, notably among those with door-security backgrounds. It is a way of standing and using your hands that controls distance, protects you and prepares you to act, while looking non-aggressive.

Stand in the settled stance from Chapter 2: feet about shoulder-width apart, one slightly forward, knees soft. Raise your hands in front of your chest, open, palms forward or slightly turned towards each other, as if to say “calm down” or “hold on”. Your lead hand is slightly forward, roughly at the distance where the other person would have to touch it to get closer.

The fence does several jobs at once.

It controls distance. If the other person moves forward, they touch your hand, which tells you they are closing. You can step back or to the side to restore the distance.

It protects you. Your hands are already up, between you and the other person, in a position to cover, block or strike.

It looks non-aggressive. To an onlooker, and to any camera, you appear to be trying to calm the situation, which is exactly what you are doing.

It prepares you to act. From the fence, every close-range technique in this book is available. A trap, a palm heel, an elbow, a push, a bridge: all start from where your hands already are.

Drill 18.1: Fence and talk

Your partner approaches you, acting angry and verbally aggressive, and tries to close the distance. You adopt the fence and talk to them calmly, using de-escalation language, while managing the distance with small steps. If they touch your lead hand, step back or to the side and restore the distance.

At a random moment, your partner either calms down or makes a pre-agreed attack signal (such as a sudden lunge with a pad). You must recognise which, and either continue de-escalating or respond with a single strike to the pad and a move to escape.

This drill trains the most realistic sequence of all: talking, managing distance, recognising the moment, and acting.

18.7 When Physical Defence Is Necessary

If all the outer layers have failed and you or someone else is in genuine danger, you may have to use physical technique. Here are the principles that should guide you.

Act decisively. Hesitation in a real attack is dangerous. If you have decided that you must act, act fully and immediately.

Use simple techniques. Under adrenaline, fine motor skills deteriorate. Simple, gross-motor techniques such as palm heels, elbows, knees, pushes and the basic traps work; complicated joint locks and intricate combinations usually do not.

Aim to stop the threat, not to win. Strike to create an opening, then use it to escape. Do not stay to finish the fight.

Keep moving. Do not stand still in front of an attacker. Move off the line, use angles, and head for your exit.

Beware of others. Assume there may be more than one attacker, even if you can only see one. Keep your head up and avoid going to the ground.

Beware of weapons. Assume the other person may have a weapon, even if you cannot see one. Many stabbings are not noticed by the victim until afterwards. If a weapon is visible, your priority is to escape; if you cannot, your priority is to control the weapon arm.

Stop when the threat stops. Once the other person is no longer a danger, stop. Continuing beyond that point is no longer self-defence.

Street note: multiple attackers and weapons

Nothing in this book will make you safe against several attackers or someone armed with a knife. Close-range skills improve your chances, but the risk of serious injury or death in these situations is high even for experienced martial artists. Awareness, avoidance and escape are your best defences. If you cannot escape, keep moving, use obstacles, keep one attacker between you and the others, and protect your head and vital organs. Run as soon as you can.

18.8 The Law in the United Kingdom

I am not a lawyer, and this section is not legal advice. The law changes, it varies between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and every case turns on its own facts. If you have been involved in an incident, take advice from a qualified solicitor. What follows is a broad, general outline of the principles as they are commonly understood.

The basic principle: reasonable force

In England and Wales, the law allows a person to use reasonable force to defend themselves or another person, to protect property, to prevent crime, or to assist in a lawful arrest. This comes from the common law and from statute, including the Criminal Law Act 1967 and section 76 of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008, which sets out how the question of reasonable force is approached.

There are, broadly, two questions.

Did you honestly believe it was necessary to use force? This is judged on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be, even if you were mistaken, provided the belief was genuinely held. (A mistaken belief caused by voluntary intoxication is treated differently.)

Was the amount of force reasonable in those circumstances? This is judged objectively, but the law recognises that a person defending themselves cannot weigh to a nicety the exact amount of force needed. If you did only what you honestly and instinctively thought was necessary in the heat of the moment, that is strong evidence that your actions were reasonable.

Force that is grossly disproportionate will not be regarded as reasonable. In householder cases, where someone is defending themselves against an intruder in their home, the law allows a wider margin, but grossly disproportionate force is still not permitted.

You do not have to wait to be hit

The law does not require you to wait to be attacked before you defend yourself. If you honestly believe an attack is imminent, you may act first, provided your response is reasonable. This is sometimes called a pre-emptive strike. However, the belief must be genuine, and you may be asked to explain what made you believe an attack was coming. This is where knowing and recognising pre-attack indicators matters.

Retreat

There is no absolute duty to retreat before defending yourself. However, whether you had an opportunity to retreat and did not take it is a factor that may be considered when deciding whether your force was reasonable. In practice, leaving when you safely can is both the safest and the most legally defensible course.

After the threat ends

Self-defence ends when the threat ends. Force used after the other person has stopped being a danger, for example striking someone who is on the ground and no longer resisting, or chasing someone who is running away, is very unlikely to be regarded as self-defence.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

The law in Scotland is based on different principles, including the idea that there must be an imminent danger, that retreat or escape was not reasonably possible, and that the force used was not excessive. Northern Ireland has its own statutory framework that broadly mirrors England and Wales. If you live or travel in these jurisdictions, take time to understand the differences.

Weapons

Carrying any article made or adapted for causing injury in a public place is a criminal offence in the United Kingdom, regardless of your intention. So is carrying a bladed or pointed article without good reason. This includes many items sold as “self-defence” tools. Martial artists should never carry weapons for self-defence. Your training is your protection.

18.9 After the Incident

What happens after a violent incident can be as important as the incident itself.

Immediate safety

Get to a safe place. Do not stay at the scene if there is any risk of further violence. Once you are safe, check yourself for injuries. Adrenaline can mask pain, including the pain of stab wounds, so look carefully.

Call the emergency services

If anyone is injured, or if there is a continuing danger, call 999 (or 112). Ask for an ambulance if anyone is hurt, including the other person, and the police. If you have the skills, give first aid to anyone who needs it, provided it is safe to do so.

Dealing with the police

The police will want to know what happened. You have the right to legal advice, free of charge, at the police station. It is generally sensible to take that advice before giving a detailed account, particularly if you may be suspected of an offence. When you do give an account, be honest. Explain what you saw, what you believed and why you acted as you did. If you recognised pre-attack indicators, say so. If you were frightened, say so.

Witnesses and evidence

If there were witnesses, try to get their contact details or ask the police to do so. Note the locations of any cameras. Write down your own recollection as soon as you can, because memory fades and is distorted by stress.

The emotional aftermath

Even a brief violent incident can have a lasting effect. It is very common to experience shaking, nausea, sleeplessness, intrusive memories, anxiety, irritability, guilt or anger in the days and weeks afterwards. These are normal responses to an abnormal event.

Talk to people you trust. Return to normal routines as soon as you can. Be patient with yourself. If the effects persist beyond a few weeks, or are severe, speak to your GP or seek support from organisations that help victims of crime. There is no weakness in this. Many experienced martial artists, police officers and soldiers have needed help after violent incidents.

Kata link: rei

Every Goju-Ryu kata begins and ends with a bow, rei. It is easy to treat the bow as a formality. But it carries a reminder that is especially relevant to this chapter: the techniques between the two bows are serious, and they are framed by respect, restraint and responsibility. The kata begin with a defensive movement, not an attack, and they end by returning to stillness. That is the spirit in which close-combat skill should be carried into the world.

Problem-Solving Clinic

Problem: “I find it hard to walk away. It feels cowardly.”

Solution: That feeling is very common, especially among people who train in martial arts. Remind yourself what you are protecting: your health, your freedom, your family, your future. Walking away from someone who wants a fight costs you nothing of value. Staying could cost you everything. Courage is doing the right thing when it is hard, and the right thing is usually to leave.

Problem: “I’m worried I’ll freeze if something happens.”

Solution: Almost everyone freezes, at least briefly, the first time. Scenario training (Chapter 17), the surprise grab drill (Drill 15.2) and the fence drill (Drill 18.1) all reduce the freeze by making realistic pressure familiar. Having one simple, automatic first response, such as raising the fence and stepping off the line, also helps, because the body can act while the mind catches up.

Problem: “How do I know whether my force would be considered reasonable?”

Solution: No one can know for certain in advance, because every case depends on its facts. As a guide: use force only when you genuinely believe you or someone else is in danger, use only as much as you need to stop the threat, stop as soon as the threat stops, and leave as soon as you safely can. If you can honestly say you did those things, you are on much firmer ground.

Problem: “I’ve been in an incident and I can’t stop thinking about it.”

Solution: That is a normal response. Talk to someone you trust, keep to your routines, and be patient. If it does not ease within a few weeks, or if it is affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please speak to your GP or a support organisation. Getting help is a sign of good sense, not weakness.

18.10 Bringing It Together

The real world is where all your training is ultimately tested, but not in the way most people imagine. The test is rarely a fight. It is far more often a moment of awareness, a decision to leave, a calm word, a well-judged distance. Physical technique is the last layer of protection, used only when everything else has failed.

When it is needed, keep it simple, decisive and brief. Use it to stop the threat and escape. Stop when the threat stops. Know the law well enough to act with confidence and restraint. And look after yourself afterwards.

That is the end of the technical part of this book. In the pages that follow you will find a twelve-week training programme, a map linking the techniques to the kata, answers to common questions and a glossary. I hope they help you put everything into practice.

Key Points

  • Real violence is sudden, chaotic, frightening, unfair and legally consequential. The goal is to get home safely.
  • Protection works in layers: awareness, avoidance, de-escalation, escape and, only last, physical defence.
  • Learn to recognise the build-up and the pre-attack indicators.
  • De-escalate with calm, acknowledgement, a way out and a safe distance.
  • The fence controls distance, protects you, looks non-aggressive and prepares every close-range technique.
  • If you must act, act decisively, keep it simple, aim to escape, and stop when the threat stops.
  • In the UK, you may use reasonable force based on the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be. You need not wait to be hit, but grossly disproportionate force is not reasonable.
  • After an incident: get safe, call for help, take legal advice, note evidence, and look after your mental health.

  1. Next time you go out, practise awareness for ten minutes: exits, people, changes. What did you notice that you would normally have missed?
  2. Practise Drill 18.1 with three different partners. Which de-escalation phrases felt most natural to you?
  3. Write a short, honest account of a time you walked away from trouble, or wish you had. What did you notice? What would you do differently now?

Appendix A: The Twelve-Week Close-Combat Programme

This programme takes you through the material in this book in a sensible order over twelve weeks. It assumes three sessions a week of about an hour each, two with a partner and one solo. If you train less often, simply take longer over each block; if you train more, add sessions rather than skipping ahead.

Each week has a focus, a partner session (done twice), a solo session and a check: something you should be able to do before moving on. If you cannot pass the check, repeat the week. There is no prize for finishing quickly.

How each session is structured

Every partner session follows the same shape.

Part Time Content
Warm-up 10 min Joint mobility, light cardio, breakfalls (Drill 12.1)
Foundation 10 min Sanchin or Tensho; kakie
Main skill 25 min The week’s focus drills, cooperative to resisting
Pressure 10 min A sparring game from the ladder in Chapter 17
Cool-down 5 min Stretching, breathing, diary notes

Every solo session includes the week’s kata work, conditioning from Chapter 2, footwork from Chapter 3 and any bag or pad work you can do alone.

Block One: Foundations (Weeks 1–3)

Week 1: Structure

Focus: Sanchin alignment, elbows down, breathing.

Partner session: Drill 1.1 (hard and soft); Drill 2.1 stages one and two (static and varying shime); pushing in sanchin dachi. Pressure: the pushing square (Drill 2.2) at low intensity.

Solo session: Sanchin kata three times slowly; pulse punching on a bag (Drill 2.3), fifty each hand; the conditioning routine from Chapter 2.

Check: Your partner can push your forearm with steady pressure and your elbow stays down and your feet stay still.

Week 2: Moving structure

Focus: Shime while moving; pulse tension.

Partner session: Drill 2.1 stages three and four (sudden pushes, moving shime); structure while striking a pad held against the chest. Pressure: pushing square, thirty-second rounds.

Solo session: Sanchin with relaxed transitions and pulsed tension; footwork: suri ashi and crescent step, ten minutes.

Check: You can step through the Sanchin pattern while your partner pushes, without losing your stance.

Week 3: Distance and angles

Focus: Pivots, angled entry, the outside line.

Partner session: Drill 3.1 (bridging on the line) with straight punches, then hooks; Drill 3.2 (owning the outside). Pressure: owning the outside with one light strike allowed.

Solo session: Pivots and angled steps, one hundred of each; Gekisai Dai Ichi and Ni, focusing on the angles.

Check: You can enter with a cover and make contact off the line against half-speed punches, eight times out of ten.

Block Two: Sensitivity and Trapping (Weeks 4–6)

Week 4: Kakie

Focus: Smooth single-arm kakie.

Partner session: Drill 4.1 (the silent circle), both sides; kakie stages two and three (the grab, the turn). Pressure: the shoulder game (Drill 4.2).

Solo session: Tensho slowly, imagining contact; grip conditioning.

Check: You can keep three minutes of kakie smooth and unbroken with a new partner.

Week 5: Traps

Focus: Palm pin, hooking pull, pass.

Partner session: Each trap from contact, ten times per side; Drill 5.1 (trap from any contact). Pressure: Drill 5.2 (pin and punch).

Solo session: Saifa slowly, identifying the traps; bag work: pin an imaginary arm and strike, fifty times.

Check: From a random contact, you apply a sensible trap and strike within half a second.

Week 6: Combinations

Focus: The three core combinations.

Partner session: Drill 6.1 (the three combinations) on mitts; Drill 6.2 (three actions). Pressure: touch kakie with traps.

Solo session: Short strikes on a bag: back fist, palm heel, hammer fist, short hook; three rounds of two minutes.

Check: You can complete Drill 6.2 with five actions and no pauses.

Block Three: The Short Weapons (Weeks 7–8)

Week 7: Elbows and knees

Focus: Body-driven elbows and hip-driven knees.

Partner session: Drill 7.1 (six-direction elbows); Drill 7.2 (cover and return); Drill 8.1 (hip-drive knees); Drill 8.2 (high–low). Pressure: touch-knee clinch rounds (Drill 8.3).

Solo session: Drill 7.3 (heavy-bag elbow rounds); knee drives against a wall, twenty per side; Seiyunchin.

Check: Your pad holder reports that your elbows and knees feel heavy rather than light.

Week 8: The clinch

Focus: Inside control, posture, wall work.

Partner session: Positional clinch work from each clinch position; Drill 9.1 (clinch strike rotation); Drill 9.2 (off the wall). Pressure: positional clinch rounds, then clinch with touch knees.

Solo session: Neck conditioning; Seipai and Kururunfa, looking for clinch applications.

Check: You can escape a wall pin with a resisting partner at least half the time.

Block Four: Throws and Falls (Weeks 9–10)

Week 9: Kuzushi and breakfalls

Focus: Balance-breaking; competent breakfalls from standing.

Partner session: Breakfalls (Drill 12.1) from standing; Drill 10.1 (push–pull); Drill 10.2 (pull and strike). Pressure: off-balance game (make your partner step).

Solo session: Breakfalls; the technical stand-up (Drill 12.2), twenty per side.

Check: You can perform all four breakfalls from standing safely, and you can make a cooperative partner stumble with push–pull on both sides.

Week 10: Throws

Focus: Outer reap, turning throw, arm-bar takedown, inner sweep.

Partner session: Uchikomi (Drill 11.1) for each throw; Drill 11.2 (throw on the reaction). Pressure: throwing game from a clinch, on mats.

Solo session: Shisochin and Seiyunchin, looking for throws; squat and hip-hinge strength work.

Check: You can complete the turning throw and outer reap against light resistance on both sides.

Block Five: Control and Release (Week 11)

Week 11: Joint controls and escapes

Focus: Inward wrist bend, straight-arm bar, elbow lift and turn; grab escapes.

Partner session: Drill 13.1 (finding the end of range); each control slowly from its starting grab; Drill 14.1 (control chains); Drill 15.1 (grab circuit). Pressure: Drill 15.2 (the surprise grab).

Solo session: Saifa with the wrist-grab escape in mind; grip and forearm conditioning.

Check: You can escape a firm wrist grab, lapel grab and rear bear hug and follow up, under committed (not yet resisting) pressure.

Block Six: Integration (Week 12)

Week 12: Timing, sparring and the real world

Focus: Counter-attack timing; climbing the sparring ladder; the fence.

Partner session: Drill 16.1 (reading the tell); Drill 16.2 (open the door); Drill 17.1 (the ten-minute ladder); Drill 18.1 (fence and talk). Pressure: focused rounds (Drill 17.2) of light close-range sparring.

Solo session: Your favourite kata, performed with full intent and imagined applications; a written review of your training diary for the whole twelve weeks.

Check: You can complete three two-minute rounds of light, integrated close-range sparring with a partner, keeping to the agreement and achieving your round goal at least once.

After the Twelve Weeks

When you finish, start again. The second time through, spend more time at the resisting end of each drill and climb one rung higher on the sparring ladder. The third time, choose the two blocks you found hardest and give each four weeks. Close-range skill grows in spirals: each time round, the same material goes deeper.

Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions I am asked most often, at seminars, after classes and by email. Some are answered in detail in the chapters; here I give the short version and point you to the right place.

Do I need to be a Goju-Ryu practitioner to use this book?

No. The kata links will mean most to Goju-Ryu students, but the principles of structure, sensitivity, trapping, short strikes, throws and joint control apply to any style. Practitioners of other karate styles, kung fu, kickboxing and self-defence systems have used this material successfully. You will need a partner, and ideally a teacher.

How long before I can use this in sparring?

Most students notice a difference in their sparring after about three months of regular close-range work, especially in their comfort when the distance closes. Real fluency takes years. Follow the twelve-week programme, then go round again.

I am over fifty. Is this suitable for me?

Yes, with sensible adjustments. Many of the most effective close-range methods rely on structure, timing and leverage rather than athleticism, which is why older practitioners are often very good at them. Take more time over breakfalls, avoid the full shoulder wheel and forward breakfalls if your joints or bones are fragile, and choose partners who will train at a sensible intensity. Speak to your doctor before starting if you have any health concerns.

I am small. Will this work for me?

Close range is the most dangerous distance for a smaller person, which is exactly why you need to be good there. Angles (Chapter 3), sensitivity (Chapter 4), kuzushi (Chapter 10) and joint controls (Chapter 13) all rely on skill rather than size. Train with bigger partners at controlled intensity so that you learn what works for you.

Is kakie really worth the time?

Yes. It is slow and often frustrating, and it is the single best exercise for close-range sensitivity. Ten minutes at the start of every session for six months will change the way you move. See Chapter 4.

Why are there no high kicks in this book?

Because high kicks are not close-range weapons. At close range, knees, stamps and low kicks are faster, safer and harder to see. See Chapter 8.

Can I use elbows and knees in competition?

Usually not. Most sport karate rules prohibit them. Some knockdown, traditional and mixed-rules formats allow knees to the body or legs. Check the rules of any event, and train elbows and knees on pads and in controlled sparring games (Chapter 17).

Isn’t trapping a myth? I heard it doesn’t work against resistance.

Long, elaborate trapping sequences do not work well against resistance. Short, simple traps made from contact and followed immediately by a strike work very well. See Chapter 5.

Is it safe to practise throws at home?

Only if you have proper mats, both of you can breakfall competently, and you start very slowly. Never throw on a hard floor or a thin carpet. A good dojo with an experienced teacher is a much better place to learn.

How do I find a partner?

Ask in your dojo. Many students are keen to explore bunkai and close-range work but have never been offered the chance. Judo, wrestling and jiu-jitsu clubs are also good places to find people comfortable with contact; many will happily swap skills with a karateka.

What equipment do I need?

For most of the book: a partner, a pair of focus mitts, a kick shield or Thai pad, gloves (MMA-style for trapping), a mouthguard and a groin guard. For throws: proper mats. For sparring: add a head guard, shin guards and, ideally, a body protector. See Chapter 17.

Can I really strike first under UK law?

You do not have to wait to be hit if you honestly believe an attack is imminent, provided your response is reasonable in the circumstances as you believed them to be. But the belief must be genuine, and leaving or de-escalating should always come first if possible. This is not legal advice; see Chapter 18 and consult a solicitor if you are involved in an incident.

Should I carry something for self-defence?

No. Carrying any article made or adapted for causing injury is a criminal offence in the United Kingdom, as is carrying a bladed or pointed article without good reason. Your training is your protection, and awareness is the most important part of it.

What if I freeze in a real situation?

Almost everyone freezes briefly. Scenario training, the surprise-grab drill and the fence drill (Drills 15.2 and 18.1) shorten the freeze. Having one simple first response, such as raising the fence and stepping off the line, helps the body act while the mind catches up.

Where should I go after this book?

Back to the beginning. Then find teachers who can watch you, partners who will push you, and, if you can, cross-train for a while in a grappling art such as judo. The close range rewards a lifetime of study.

A Closing Letter to the Reader

If you have read this far, thank you. If you have trained this far, thank you even more, because a book like this means very little until its pages are worn by use and its drills have left you sweating, bruised and grinning on a dojo floor.

I want to leave you with three thoughts.

The first is that the close range is not something to be feared. It is a place to be understood. Most people dread it because nobody ever showed them what to do there. Now you have a map. The territory will still surprise you, but you will no longer be lost in it.

The second is that the best close-range fighters I have known are also the gentlest people I have known. That is not a coincidence. When you know that you can deal with someone at arm’s length, you no longer need to prove it. You can afford to be patient, to listen, to step back and to let things go. The confidence that comes from real skill is quiet.

The third is that this art is bigger than any of us. Kanryo Higaonna, Chojun Miyagi and the countless teachers since have passed it from hand to hand, quite literally, through the forearms of their students in kakie and through the pushes and slaps of shime. You are now part of that line. Train well, look after your partners, and pass on what you know, honestly and generously, to the people who come after you.

Stand properly. Breathe properly. Do not be afraid to be close.

With warm wishes for your training,

Dr Beverly J. Stivers

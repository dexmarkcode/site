# Faults of Breath, Eyes and Mind

::: chapter-intro
The last of the fault chapters deals with the things a camera shows least clearly: the breath, the gaze, the face and the state of mind. These faults are often the last to be noticed and the slowest to change, but they shape everything else in the kata. This chapter catalogues them in the same format as Chapters 19 and 20, and adds specific practices for nerves, rushing and the wandering mind.
:::

A student once asked me why, after two years of work on her stance and arms, her teacher had started correcting her eyes. She had assumed that the eyes were a detail, something to tidy up at the end. In fact, her teacher had been waiting for the bigger faults to settle so that the eyes could be seen. Her gaze had been dropping to the floor before every turn for as long as she had done Sanchin, and with it her head tipped forward, her upper back rounded and her mind went somewhere else. Fixing it changed her turns more than any amount of footwork.

The faults in this chapter are like that. They are less visible than a long stance or a flared elbow, but they run underneath everything. A held breath stiffens the whole body. A dropping gaze rounds the back. A wandering mind lets the stance drift. Anxiety lifts the shoulders. When these are fixed, many of the faults of the previous two chapters become much easier to fix too.

## Faults of the Breath

::: fault
**Fault 21.1 – Holding the Breath**

*Looks like.* At the end of each technique, or during turns and tests, the breath stops. The face may redden, the neck tighten and the body freeze.

*Check.* The hum test (Chapter 7): try to hum at the moment you suspect you are holding. Ask a partner to listen; a held breath is silent where there should be sound.

*Causes.* Equating effort with breath-holding, as in lifting something heavy; anticipating a test; concentrating so hard on a technique that the breath is forgotten.

*Correct.* Return to Stage 2 breathing for two weeks. Breathe out through a soft "haa" with the mouth slightly open on every technique. Ask a partner to tap your shoulder whenever they hear the breath stop. Do not progress to firmer breathing until the hum test passes on every out-breath.

*Confirm.* Hum test throughout the kata; no redness or dizziness after.

*Safety.* This is the most important fault in the chapter. If you feel dizzy, see stars or develop a headache, stop at once (Before You Begin).
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.2 – Breathing Into the Upper Chest**

*Looks like.* The chest and shoulders rise on the in-breath; the belly may even draw in.

*Check.* One hand on the upper chest, one on the abdomen, during the in-breath. The upper hand should stay still.

*Causes.* Everyday habit; anxiety; a tight abdomen that does not allow the breath to descend.

*Correct.* Lying-down low breathing (Chapter 6 problem box) for five minutes daily; belt breathing (Drill 7.2). Allow the lower trunk to expand: the abdomen should be firm but not sucked in.

*Confirm.* Hand check during the kata's in-breaths.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.3 – Noisy or Gasping In-Breath**

*Looks like.* The in-breath is loud, sharp or sniffed, sometimes through the mouth.

*Check.* Record the kata with sound and listen to the in-breaths only.

*Causes.* The in-breath is too fast or too large; the previous out-breath emptied the lungs too far, so the body is short of air.

*Correct.* Slow the in-breath and make it smaller; finish each out-breath with air in reserve (Chapter 7).

*Confirm.* Listen again; the in-breaths should be quiet.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.4 – Running Out of Breath**

*Looks like.* The out-breath finishes before the technique does; the end of the punch or push is silent and soft.

*Check.* Video with sound; watch the moment the sound stops and the moment the technique arrives.

*Causes.* An out-breath that starts too forcefully and fades; an in-breath that is too small; a tempo too slow for current breathing capacity (dragging).

*Correct.* Practise a steady, even out-breath at the same volume from start to finish (Drill 7.1). Take a slightly fuller in-breath. If necessary, shorten the technique to fit the breath rather than the other way round.

*Confirm.* The sound should last until the technique arrives.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.5 – Breath and Movement Out of Step**

*Looks like.* The breath starts before or after the movement; the out-breath finishes early or late relative to the technique.

*Check.* Drill 9.1, the three-line check.

*Causes.* Learning the movement and the breath separately and never fully joining them; rushing; nerves.

*Correct.* Practise one technique at a time with a metronome (Drill 9.3), starting the breath and the movement on the same beat.

*Confirm.* Repeat the three-line check.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.6 – Strangled Out-Breath**

*Looks like.* The out-breath sounds tight, rasping or choked; the neck cords stand out.

*Check.* Hum test; look at the neck in a mirror during the out-breath.

*Causes.* Closing the throat too far to make a louder sound; tension in the neck and jaw.

*Correct.* Exhale with the mouth in a small "o" shape. Reduce the volume. Keep the jaw soft and the teeth slightly apart (Chapter 8).

*Confirm.* Hum test passes; the sound is steady rather than strained.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.7 – Theatrical Breathing**

*Looks like.* Exaggerated, loud breathing, sometimes with shaking and dramatic sounds, beyond what the technique requires.

*Check.* Ask yourself honestly: would my breathing be the same if nobody were watching?

*Causes.* Imitating demonstration performances; believing louder means stronger; nerves seeking an outlet.

*Correct.* Practise the kata alone, at Stage 3, attending only to the timing and the feeling of the breath in the trunk, not to its sound. Keep the theatrical version, if you need it, for demonstrations.

*Confirm.* Record yourself performing for an audience and alone; the two should sound similar.
:::

## Faults of the Eyes and Face

::: fault
**Fault 21.8 – Gaze Dropping**

*Looks like.* The eyes drop to the floor, especially before steps and turns, or during difficult moments. The head tips forward with them.

*Check.* Front-on video; or ask a partner to watch only your eyes for one kata.

*Causes.* Checking the feet; tiredness; anxiety; a habit of looking down when concentrating.

*Correct.* Choose gaze points at both ends of the room before the kata (Chapter 10). If you need to check your feet, do so in a separate drill, not in the kata. Practise the turns with a partner standing at each gaze point, holding up a finger to look at.

*Confirm.* Partner watches the eyes again.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.9 – Eyes Wandering**

*Looks like.* The eyes move around the room, glance at the tester, follow the hands or check the mirror.

*Check.* As Fault 21.8.

*Causes.* Distraction; self-consciousness; using the mirror during the kata.

*Correct.* Use the soft, steady gaze described in Chapter 6: look at the fixed point while staying aware of the edges of your vision. Practise Drill 3.1, the three-minute stand, focusing entirely on the gaze. Do not use the mirror during the whole kata; use it only for separate drills.

*Confirm.* As Fault 21.8.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.10 – Grimacing and Jaw Clenching**

*Looks like.* The face screws up, the forehead furrows, the jaw clenches or the mouth pulls into a grimace.

*Check.* Front-on video, close up, at the end of techniques. Feel your jaw after the kata: is it aching?

*Causes.* Tension spilling into the soft zone (Chapter 8); working above your spill point.

*Correct.* Reduce peak tension until the face stays calm. Practise the tension dial (Drill 8.1) with a mirror, watching only your face. Keep the teeth slightly apart and the tongue on the roof of the mouth.

*Confirm.* Video; no jaw ache.
:::

## Faults of the Mind

The faults below are not physical in the same way as the others, but they are just as real, and they show up just as clearly in the kata.

::: fault
**Fault 21.11 – Rushing**

*Looks like.* The kata gets faster as it goes on, especially at the steps and turns; the total time is short; transitions are hurried.

*Check.* The Stopwatch Test (Chapter 9).

*Causes.* Discomfort, which makes the mind want to finish; nerves; not knowing the next movement well enough, so that the student hurries to reach it before forgetting.

*Correct.* The metronome kata (Drill 9.3). Practise slowing down deliberately at the points where you rush. Know the sequence well enough that it is automatic (practise it without tension until it is). Recognise the urge to hurry as part of the inner battle, and answer it with a slower breath.

*Confirm.* Stopwatch Test.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.12 – Anticipation and Flinching**

*Looks like.* The student tenses before the tester touches them; flinches, blinks or looks round when tested.

*Check.* Ask your tester whether they feel you bracing before contact.

*Causes.* Fear of the test; past experience of testing that was too hard.

*Correct.* The approach in Chapter 16: Level 1 testing with the tester announcing each touch, gradually progressing. Keep breathing. Keep your eyes on your gaze point. Let the test come to you.

*Confirm.* The tester feels no pre-bracing.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.13 – The Wandering Mind**

*Looks like.* The student loses their place in the kata, repeats or skips a movement, or performs a section on "autopilot" with visible deterioration in posture.

*Check.* After the kata, ask yourself: at which frame did I stop paying attention? Most people can answer this surprisingly precisely.

*Causes.* Fatigue; boredom with a familiar kata; the mind running ahead to the end or back to something that went wrong.

*Correct.* Use the single-check practice from Chapter 6, choosing a different point of attention for each performance. Count the breaths silently. When you notice the mind has wandered, do not criticise yourself; simply return it to the breath and the next movement. That act of returning is the training.

*Confirm.* Note the frame at which attention first slips. Over weeks, it should come later and later.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.14 – Nerves Under Observation**

*Looks like.* The kata is good in private and poor in a grading, a competition or in front of a senior teacher: rushed, tense, with breath-holding and errors in the sequence.

*Check.* Compare a recording made alone with one made in front of others.

*Causes.* The ordinary stress response: raised heart rate, shallow breathing, tension in the shoulders and a narrowing of attention.

*Correct.* Practise under observation regularly, not just at gradings: ask a friend to watch, perform for the class, record yourself knowing others will see it. Before performing, take three slow low breaths with a long out-breath. During the kata, keep returning to the breath and the gaze. Accept that nerves will be present and perform anyway; the aim is not to remove them but to perform well alongside them.

*Confirm.* The gap between your private and observed performances narrows over time.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 21.15 – Trying Too Hard**

*Looks like.* Everything is overdone: tension too high, breath too loud, stance too low, face strained. The kata looks effortful and stiff.

*Check.* Compare a kata at your normal effort with one deliberately performed at about two-thirds effort. Which holds better under testing? Which looks better on video?

*Causes.* The belief that more effort is always better; wanting to impress; perfectionism.

*Correct.* For two weeks, perform every kata at two-thirds of your usual effort. Use the testing round to check that the structure still holds. It usually holds better.

*Confirm.* Compare testing scorecards.
:::

## Practices for the Mind

Several of the faults above respond to the same few practices. They are gathered here for convenience.

::: drill
**Drill 21.1 – Three Breaths Before**

Before every performance of Sanchin, whether in your living room or at a grading, stand at attention and take three slow breaths: in through the nose for four counts, out through the mouth for six. On each out-breath, let one area soften: first the face, then the shoulders, then the hands. Then begin.

*Why it matters:* A short, consistent ritual before the kata settles the nervous system and sets the tone for the breath. It also gives you something familiar to do at a grading, when everything else feels unfamiliar.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 21.2 – The Returning Practice**

Perform the kata once, very slowly, with a single instruction: every time you notice your attention has left the breath and the movement, make a small mark, such as a slight press of the thumb against the fingers, and bring your attention back. Do not count the marks or judge them. Simply return.

*Why it matters:* This is the inner battle of Chapter 3 practised directly. The skill being trained is not perfect attention, which nobody has, but quick return.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 21.3 – Observed Practice**

Once a week, perform the kata in front of someone: a training partner, a family member, your class. Treat it as a grading. Afterwards, write down one thing that went better than you expected and one thing that went worse.

*Why it matters:* Nerves under observation improve only with exposure. Regular, low-stakes observed practice is the most reliable way to close the gap between private and public performance.
:::

## Closing the Catalogue

You have now worked through the three fault chapters. Between them they list around forty-five faults. Nobody has all of them, and nobody has none.

If you have carried out the audits in Chapters 19 and 20 and the checks in this chapter, you should have a list of your own faults, grouped into chains, with the lowest links circled. That list is one of the most valuable things this book can give you. Keep it in your training notebook. Work on one root fault at a time. Re-audit every few months. And remember that the list will change: as one fault is fixed, others that were hidden beneath it will appear. That is not failure. It is progress, seen from the inside.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Faults of the breath, eyes, face and mind are less visible than physical faults but underlie many of them.
- Breath faults include holding, chest breathing, gasping, running out, being out of step, strangling and theatrical breathing. Holding the breath is the most important to fix, for safety.
- Eye and face faults include a dropping or wandering gaze and grimacing. Fixed gaze points and moderate tension help.
- Faults of the mind include rushing, anticipation, wandering attention, nerves under observation and trying too hard.
- Three practices help with most of them: three breaths before, the returning practice and regular observed practice.
- Keep your personal fault list, work on one root at a time and expect the list to change as you improve.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Record a kata with sound and listen to it with your eyes closed. Note every breath fault you hear.
2. Ask a partner to watch only your eyes for one complete kata and tell you every moment they moved.
3. Practise Drill 21.1 before every kata for a month.
4. Practise Drill 21.2 once a week.
5. Arrange to perform in front of someone once a week for a month (Drill 21.3).
6. Combine your audits from Chapters 19, 20 and 21 into a single list. Circle the lowest link of each chain, and choose one to start on.
:::

# Open Hands: The Spear-Hand and the Circular Block

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin ends with two open-hand techniques: the double spear-hand thrust and the circular block that finishes in the tiger's-mouth position. They change the character of the kata from straight and closed to curved and open. This chapter explains how to form the open hand, how to perform each technique with the same structure as the punch and the block, and how to practise the circular block until it is smooth on both sides.
:::

For most of Sanchin, the hands are closed. The fist holds the tension of the arm and concentrates it at the knuckles. Then, near the end of the kata, the hands open. The double spear-hand reaches forward with straight fingers, and the circular block sweeps both hands round in opposite directions before pushing forward with open palms.

Many students find this change disconcerting. The open hand feels weaker than the fist, and it is harder to hold tension in it without the fingers either going limp or becoming rigid and shaky. The circular block, in particular, asks for a smoothness and coordination that the earlier, straighter techniques do not. Students who are confident in the first part of the kata often lose their way in the last.

The answer, as so often in Sanchin, is to recognise that the principles do not change. The open hand is still driven from the ground and delivered by the back. The shoulders still stay down. The breath still supports the movement. What changes is the shape at the end of the arm and the path it travels.

## The Open Hand

There are several open-hand shapes in karate. The two used at the end of Sanchin are closely related.

### The spear-hand (*nukite*)

The spear-hand is formed with the fingers straight and pressed together, the thumb bent and tucked firmly against the side of the index finger or the palm. The tips of the middle three fingers are drawn level by slightly bending the middle finger, so that they form a flat, even striking edge. The wrist is straight, continuing the line of the forearm.

The fingers are firm, but not hyperextended. A common error is to bend the fingers backwards in an effort to make them strong, which leaves them stiff and vulnerable. They should be straight and braced, like the blade of a knife, with the strength coming from the forearm and hand muscles rather than from locking the finger joints.

### The palm (*shotei* and *tora guchi*)

In the circular block, the hands finish with the palms facing forward, the fingers pointing up on the upper hand and down on the lower hand, and the heels of the hands leading. The contact surface is the heel of the palm, *shotei*. The fingers are slightly curved and firm, as though gripping a large ball. The two hands, held with the wrists close together, form the shape called *tora guchi*, the tiger's mouth, because the open hands resemble the jaws of a tiger.

::: drill
**Drill 15.1 – Finger Strength**

1. **Fingertip press.** Kneel or stand at a table. Place the tips of your fingers of both hands on the table, fingers straight, and press down gradually until you feel the fingers and forearms working. Hold for ten seconds. Five times.
2. **Spear-hand hold.** Form a spear-hand with both hands and hold them in front of you at chest height, arms almost straight, for thirty seconds, breathing slowly. Keep the fingers straight but not bent back. Rest and repeat three times.
3. **Ball squeeze.** Hold a tennis ball or soft ball in your palm with the fingers spread around it, as in the tiger's-mouth hand. Squeeze with the fingertips and the heel of the hand for five seconds, release. Ten times each hand.

*Why it matters:* Open-hand techniques demand strength in the fingers and the small muscles of the hand that most people rarely train. A few minutes a day makes a marked difference. Chapter 23 describes the traditional Okinawan finger-strengthening methods.
:::

## The Double Spear-Hand

The double spear-hand, *morote nukite*, appears twice in the kata, each time after a step backwards (Frames 32 to 36). Both hands are drawn back to the hips, open, and then thrust forward together.

### The chamber

As the step back begins, both hands open and draw back and down to the sides of the body, just above the hips. The palms face up and slightly in, the fingers point forward and the elbows point back. This is a mirror of the pulling-hand position, performed with both hands at once and with the hands open.

The key points are the same as for the pulling hand. The shoulders stay down. The shoulder blades draw slightly back and down, not so far that the chest pushes out. The elbows stay close to the body. Both hands arrive together, at the same height.

### The thrust

From the chamber, both hands thrust forward together, along straight paths, to finish at about the height of the solar plexus or the lower ribs. The hands finish roughly shoulder-width apart or slightly narrower, so that the two spear-hands point at the two sides of an opponent's lower ribcage, in the area of the floating ribs. The palms face each other, or turn slightly downward; this varies between lineages.

The thrust is driven exactly as the punch is driven: rear foot pressing, hips square, back delivering the arms, shoulders down. Because both arms extend at once, there is no opposite arm in the block to help stabilise the trunk, and the back has to work harder to hold the shoulders in place. This is one reason the double spear-hand is a good test of the connection between the arms and the back.

### The return

After the thrust, both hands draw back to the chamber along the same paths, ready for the next step or for the circular block.

![**Figure 15.2** The double spear-hand from the side and from the front. The hands thrust from the hips along straight paths to finish at solar-plexus height, roughly shoulder-width apart. The shoulders stay down and the trunk upright.](figures/fig15_2.png)

::: fault
**Problem: "When I do the double spear-hand, I lean forward and my shoulders hunch."**

*Check.* Film from the side. At the end of the thrust, compare the position of your shoulders with your hips. Look at the space between the tops of your shoulders and your ears.

*Cause.* Both arms reaching at once pulls the shoulder blades forward around the ribcage and drags the upper body with them. This is the same fault as reaching with the shoulder in the punch, doubled.

*Correct.* Practise the thrust with your back against a wall, heels a foot's length from it, so that your shoulder blades stay in contact with the wall throughout. The thrust will feel shorter. That is correct. Ten slow repetitions, three times a week. Then step away from the wall and try to keep the same feeling.

*Confirm.* Film again from the side.
:::

## The Circular Block

The circular block, *mawashi uke*, is performed twice at the end of the kata: once with the right hand finishing on top and once with the left (Frames 37 to 46). It is the most complex technique in Sanchin and one of the most characteristic techniques of Goju-Ryu as a whole. It appears in several other kata, and many teachers regard it as one of the most useful techniques in the style.

It is best understood in four phases: the gather, the circles, the draw and the push.

### Phase 1: The gather

As the step back is taken, both hands draw in towards the centre of the chest, open, palms facing roughly towards each other or slightly forward. For the right circular block, the right hand is slightly above the left. The elbows are down and close to the body. This is the starting point of the circles.

### Phase 2: The circles

The two hands now trace circles in front of the body, moving at the same time and at the same speed but in opposite directions.

For the right circular block, seen from your own point of view, the right hand moves clockwise: up and out to the right, over the top, down the left side and back up through the centre. The left hand moves anticlockwise: down and out to the left, under the bottom, up the right side and back down through the centre. Because the hands move in opposite directions, they pass each other twice in each circle, once in front of the chest and once in front of the abdomen, without colliding. Each hand completes roughly one full circle.

The circles are made from the back and shoulders, not from the wrists. The elbows stay relatively low, and the hands stay within the width of the shoulders. The circles should not be huge sweeping movements but compact rings, roughly the diameter of a dinner plate to a small tray. They should be perfectly smooth, with no corners and no change of speed.

For the left circular block, everything is reversed: the left hand moves anticlockwise and finishes on top, the right hand moves clockwise and finishes below.

### Phase 3: The draw

As the circles finish, both hands draw back towards the chest, forming the tiger's mouth close to the body. For the right block, the right hand is above, at about chin or upper-chest height, fingers pointing up and palm facing forward; the left hand is below, at about the level of the lower abdomen, fingers pointing down and palm facing forward. The heels of the two hands are close together, almost touching, at the centre line. The elbows are down and close to the body.

### Phase 4: The push

From the draw, both hands push forward together, keeping the tiger's-mouth shape, until the arms are almost straight. The heels of the hands lead. The push is driven from the ground and delivered by the back, like the punch and the spear-hand, on a long, steady out-breath.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

The circular block is described differently by different teachers, and the precise number of circles, the size of the circles and the order in which the hands pass each other all vary. Some lines perform a smaller, tighter circle with the hands crossing at the wrists; others make a larger, more open circle. What is common to all is that the hands move in opposite directions, the movement is continuous and smooth, the elbows stay down and the technique finishes with a push into the tiger's mouth. If your teacher's version differs from the one described here, follow it, and use the principles in this chapter to refine it.
:::

### Breathing in the circular block

The breath in the circular block is longer than anywhere else in the kata. In the version described here, the in-breath covers the gather, the circles and the draw, and the out-breath covers the push. This means the in-breath may last four or five seconds. It must be slow and steady, not gulped at the start and then held. If you find you run out of in-breath before the circles are finished, make the circles slightly smaller or slightly faster until the breath fits.

Some lineages use a short out-breath partway through the circles, or a sharp exhalation at the end of the push. Follow your teacher.

### The tiger's mouth

The final position of the circular block deserves a closer look, because it is held at peak tension and is often tested.

The upper hand is at about chin or upper-chest height, palm forward, fingers up and slightly curved. The lower hand is at about the level of the lower abdomen, palm forward, fingers down and slightly curved. Both are on or close to the centre line. The heels of the hands are a few centimetres apart, the wrists close together. The arms are almost straight, the elbows slightly bent and pointing down. The shoulders are down, the back is wide, and the whole structure is rooted through the stance.

In application, the tiger's mouth is usually interpreted as a push or strike with the heels of the palms to the upper and lower body of an opponent, after the circles have deflected or trapped their attacking arms. Chapter 26 discusses this in more detail.

![**Figure 15.4** The tiger's-mouth position at the end of the right circular block, from the side and the front. The upper hand is at chin to upper-chest height, the lower hand at the lower abdomen, both palms forward, heels leading, wrists close on the centre line.](figures/fig15_4.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: Tiger's-Mouth Push-Back**

At the end of the circular block, hold the tiger's mouth. Ask a partner to place their palms against yours, one on each hand, and push back towards you slowly along the line of your forearms, building pressure over three seconds.

As with the punch, the push should be absorbed by your whole structure and felt in your rear foot. If your elbows fold, your arms are not braced. If your shoulders rise or are pushed back, your arms are not connected to your back. If your hands separate, your arms are not working together. If your whole body rocks back, your stance is not rooted.
:::

## Common Faults of the Open-Hand Techniques

**Limp fingers.** The fingers soften and curl in the spear-hand, or splay out in the tiger's mouth. Strengthen with Drill 15.1, and focus on the forearm muscles rather than the finger joints.

**Hyperextended fingers.** The fingers bend backwards in an attempt to look strong. This is stiff and vulnerable. Keep the fingers straight but not locked.

**Uneven hands.** The two hands arrive at different heights or times in the spear-hand, or separate in the tiger's mouth. Practise in front of a mirror with tape marks.

**Circles too large.** The hands sweep out beyond the width of the shoulders. This disconnects them from the back and leaves the body open. Keep the circles compact.

**Angular circles.** The circles have corners, so the hands move in jerks rather than smooth rings. Practise Drill 12.1, circles without feet, slowly.

**Elbows rising.** The elbows lift as the hands circle over the top. Keep the elbows low, and let the circle come from the forearm and the rotation of the upper arm rather than from lifting the elbow.

**Leaning into the push.** The upper body leans forward as the tiger's mouth pushes out. Use the stick test from Chapter 6.

**Losing the stance.** The stance softens during the circles, because all the attention is on the hands. Keep the baseline tension in the legs and hips throughout.

::: drill
**Drill 15.2 – Circular Block Builder**

Build the circular block in four stages, spending at least a few sessions on each.

1. **One hand.** In a parallel stance, perform the circle of the upper hand alone, ten times clockwise with the right hand, then ten times anticlockwise with the left. Smooth, compact circles, elbow low.
2. **Two hands, no push.** Perform both circles together, in opposite directions, ten times for the right block and ten for the left. Finish each with the draw to the chest but do not push.
3. **Full technique, parallel stance.** Add the push into the tiger's mouth, with the breath. Five times on each side.
4. **Full technique, sanchin dachi.** Perform the circular block in sanchin dachi, with the step back, as in the kata. Five times on each side.

*Why it matters:* The circular block has so many simultaneous elements that trying to learn it whole tends to produce a muddle. Building it in stages gives each element a chance to settle.
:::

## Why Open Hands at the End?

Students sometimes ask why Sanchin, which is so firmly a closed-fist kata, ends with open hands. Teachers give a number of answers.

Some point out that the open-hand techniques reflect the older open-hand version of Sanchin taught by Higaonna, a reminder of the kata's roots that Miyagi chose to keep.

Some see it as a deliberate change of character, from the hard, closed, linear first part of the kata to a softer, rounder close, preparing the student for the circular, flowing movements of Tensho and the other kata of the style.

Some emphasise the practical: the spear-hand and the circular block are among the most effective techniques in close-range fighting, and practising them under Sanchin tension builds the structure they need.

And some simply say that it is the way the kata has been passed down. All of these may be true. What matters for your practice is that you treat the open-hand techniques with the same care as the rest of the kata. They are not an afterthought. They are where the kata finishes, and how you finish matters.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The open-hand techniques use the same principles as the closed-hand ones: driven from the ground, delivered by the back, shoulders down, breath supporting the movement.
- The spear-hand has straight, firm fingers pressed together, the thumb tucked, the middle three fingertips level and the wrist straight. The fingers should not be bent backwards.
- The double spear-hand chambers both open hands at the hips and thrusts them forward together to solar-plexus height, roughly shoulder-width apart.
- The circular block has four phases: gather, circles, draw and push. The hands circle in opposite directions, compactly and smoothly, and push forward into the tiger's mouth.
- In the tiger's mouth, the upper hand is at chin to upper-chest height, the lower at the lower abdomen, palms forward, heels leading, wrists close on the centre line.
- The breath in the circular block is the longest in the kata: in through the gather, circles and draw; out through the push.
- Build the circular block in stages: one hand, two hands without push, full technique in a parallel stance, then in sanchin dachi.
:::

### Training Tasks

1. Practise Drill 15.1, finger strength, every day for three weeks.
2. Practise the double spear-hand against a wall, as described in the problem box, three times a week for two weeks.
3. Work through Drill 15.2, the circular block builder, over two to three weeks.
4. With a partner, carry out the Tiger's-Mouth Push-Back test on both sides.
5. Film both circular blocks from the front. Compare the size and smoothness of the circles on each side.
6. Repeat the Five-Point Baseline from Chapter 1 and compare it with your earlier scores.

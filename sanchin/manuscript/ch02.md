# From Fuzhou to Naha

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is older than Goju-Ryu and older than karate as we know it. This chapter follows the form from the martial traditions of southern China to the port city of Naha in Okinawa, and through the hands of the two men who shaped it most: Kanryo Higaonna and Chojun Miyagi. It also explains why the kata you learn today may look different from the one taught down the road, and what to do about it.
:::

History is not strictly necessary for doing a good Sanchin. Plenty of people have stood well in the stance without knowing anything about where it came from. But a little history saves a lot of confusion. Once you know that the form has passed through several pairs of hands, each of which changed it a little, you stop worrying that the version in your dojo is "wrong" because it differs from one you saw online. You also start to see why certain details matter so much to teachers. Some of those details were deliberate changes made for good reasons, and knowing the reasons helps you to perform them well.

A word of caution first. The early history of Okinawan karate was handed down mostly by word of mouth, and much of it was written down only decades later, sometimes by people with a strong interest in how the story was told. Dates differ between sources, names are spelt in several ways, and some well-known stories cannot be confirmed. In what follows I have tried to give the account that most historians of the style would accept, and to say plainly where the record is uncertain.

## A Form From Southern China

The word Sanchin is the Okinawan reading of a Chinese term, usually written in English as *san zhan*, meaning "three battles". Forms with this name, or something very close to it, are still practised today in several martial arts from the province of Fujian, on the south-east coast of China, particularly in the family of styles known as White Crane boxing and in related systems. These forms share a family resemblance with Okinawan Sanchin: a narrow, inward-gripping stance, slow or measured stepping, a strong connection between the arms and the trunk, and an emphasis on breathing and internal strength.

Fujian and the Ryukyu Kingdom, of which Okinawa was the main island, had close ties for centuries. Ryukyuan ships traded with Fuzhou, the provincial capital. Ryukyuan officials and students travelled there to study, and a community of Chinese families had long been settled in the village of Kume, close to Naha. It is not surprising, then, that Fujianese martial arts found their way to Okinawa, or that Okinawans went to Fuzhou to learn them.

Exactly which Fujianese style gave rise to the Okinawan Sanchin is one of the open questions of karate history. Several have been proposed, and research is ongoing. What can be said with confidence is that the form came from southern Chinese boxing, that it was already regarded there as a foundational exercise, and that it reached Okinawa during the nineteenth century through people who had trained in Fuzhou.

## Kanryo Higaonna and Naha-te

The central figure in the Okinawan story is Kanryo Higaonna, born in Naha in 1853. As a young man he travelled to Fuzhou, where he is said to have spent many years, often given as around thirteen or fourteen, though accounts vary. There he trained under a teacher remembered in Okinawan tradition as Ryu Ryu Ko. Who that teacher was in Chinese records has been debated for decades, and there is no settled answer. Different researchers have proposed different identities, each with some supporting evidence and some difficulties.

When Higaonna returned to Naha, probably in the early 1880s, he began to teach what became known as *Naha-te*, the "hand of Naha", to distinguish it from the styles practised in the old royal capital of Shuri and in the village of Tomari. Naha-te was characterised by close-range fighting, circular blocks, grabbing and pulling, strong stances and an emphasis on breathing and body conditioning. Sanchin sat at its heart.

Higaonna was, by all accounts, a demanding teacher. Stories passed down by his students describe long sessions spent on Sanchin alone, with the teacher checking every part of the student's body and correcting their posture by hand. One frequently repeated account says that students practised Sanchin for years before being taught any other kata. Whether or not that is literally true, it gives a good picture of the weight he placed on the form.

His Sanchin differed in several ways from the version most Goju-Ryu students learn today. The most commonly reported differences are these:

- **Open hands.** Higaonna is generally said to have taught Sanchin with open hands, the fingers extended, rather than closed fists. This is still the practice in some related styles.
- **No turns.** His version is usually described as moving forward and back along a straight line without turning around.
- **Breathing.** Some accounts suggest his breathing was quicker and less drawn out than the slow, audible breathing associated with later Goju-Ryu Sanchin, though this is harder to establish.

## Chojun Miyagi and the Birth of Goju-Ryu

Chojun Miyagi was born in Naha in 1888 into a well-off family, and began training with Higaonna in his early teens, around 1902. He trained with him until Higaonna's death in 1915, and is generally regarded as his most dedicated student. After his teacher died, Miyagi travelled to Fuzhou himself, partly to learn more about the roots of what he had been taught. He made more than one trip to China over the following years.

Miyagi was a methodical thinker, interested not only in fighting but in how karate could be taught to large numbers of people, including schoolchildren and police officers, and how it could improve their health. Over the next three decades he took what he had learned from Higaonna, added what he had found in China and his own experience, and organised it into a coherent system. That system became Goju-Ryu.

The name has a well-known origin story. In 1930, one of Miyagi's senior students, Jin'an Shinzato, gave a demonstration in Japan and was asked which style of karate he practised. He had no name to give, since Okinawan teachers of the time did not usually name their styles, and is said to have answered with something like "half-hard, half-soft". When he reported this to Miyagi, his teacher decided the style needed a proper name. He took it from a line in the Bubishi, an old Chinese text on martial arts and medicine that circulated among Okinawan karateka. The line is usually translated as "the way of inhaling and exhaling is hardness and softness". From *go*, hard, and *ju*, soft, he made Goju-Ryu, the hard-soft school.

The name says a great deal about what Miyagi thought karate was for. It is not hard, and it is not soft. It is the right combination of the two at the right moment. And the place where a student first learns that combination is Sanchin.

### What Miyagi changed

Miyagi made several changes to Sanchin as he received it. The best known are:

- **Closed fists.** He changed the hands from open to closed. A common explanation is that closed fists make it easier for a beginner to keep tension in the forearms and to connect the arm to the trunk. Some teachers also suggest that it suited the larger, more mixed classes he was teaching, where open hands tended to go limp.
- **Turns.** He introduced turns into the kata, so that the student steps forward, turns around, steps again, and turns back. This added the challenge of changing direction without losing the stance or the tension, which is one of the hardest things in the form.
- **A more deliberate breath.** He placed greater emphasis on slow, audible breathing coordinated with each movement, and on breathing that trained the lower abdomen.

He also composed Tensho, the soft partner to Sanchin, generally dated to the early 1920s, and in 1940 he and another senior teacher, Shoshin Nagamine of the Matsubayashi-Ryu, composed the introductory Gekisai kata for teaching karate in schools.

It is worth repeating that these accounts come to us mostly through Miyagi's students and their students. They are widely accepted, but they are not documented in the way a modern historian would like. Teachers within Goju-Ryu sometimes disagree about the details, and some say that Miyagi taught slightly different versions of Sanchin to different students at different times, adjusting the form to the person in front of him. That would be very much in keeping with what we know about him.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

Students sometimes ask whether the open-hand Sanchin is "more authentic" than the closed-fist version, since it is older. I do not find the question very useful. Both versions train the same structure. The closed-fist version is easier to teach to beginners and easier to test; the open-hand version demands more of the fingers and forearms and is sometimes easier to relate to the open-hand techniques of other kata. If you are a Goju-Ryu student, learn the version your teacher teaches. If you later have the chance to try the other, you will learn something from the comparison.
:::

## After Miyagi

Miyagi died in 1953. He did not appoint a single successor in the way that some later organisations would have liked, and his senior students went on to teach in their own ways. This is the main reason why Goju-Ryu today exists in several branches, each with its own emphasis and its own small variations in the kata.

Among the most influential of his Okinawan students were Seiko Higa, who taught in Naha and had trained with Higaonna before Miyagi; Meitoku Yagi, who founded the Meibukan; Eiichi Miyazato, who founded the Jundokan; Seikichi Toguchi, who founded the Shoreikan; and An'ichi Miyagi, a younger student who later taught Morio Higaonna, founder of the International Okinawan Goju-Ryu Karate-do Federation. In mainland Japan, Gogen Yamaguchi, who had learned from Miyagi during the founder's visits, built the Goju-kai, which became one of the largest karate organisations in the world and developed its own distinctive approach to Sanchin, with particularly strong emphasis on breathing.

Each of these lines teaches Sanchin, and each teaches it slightly differently. The differences are usually small: the number of steps, where the turns come, the exact path of the hands when they open into the double spear-hand, the length of the breath, whether the mouth is open or closed. They are not signs that one line is right and the others wrong. They are the natural result of a living tradition passing through many teachers.

## Sanchin Outside Goju-Ryu

Goju-Ryu is not the only style that practises Sanchin. Several others do, and it is useful to know about them, partly because you will see them performed and partly because the comparisons are instructive.

**Uechi-Ryu**, founded by Kanbun Uechi, who trained in Fuzhou a little later than Higaonna and under a different teacher, treats Sanchin as its central kata. Uechi-Ryu Sanchin is performed with open hands and with a quicker, more natural breath than Goju-Ryu's. Its stance is similar in principle but the arm actions are different, and its testing tradition is, if anything, even more vigorous. Uechi students often practise their Sanchin with three steps forward, a turn and three more steps.

**Shito-Ryu**, founded by Kenwa Mabuni, who trained with Higaonna as well as with Shuri-te teachers, includes Sanchin among its many kata, usually in a form close to the Goju-Ryu version.

**Kyokushin**, the full-contact style founded by Masutatsu Oyama, includes Sanchin and Tensho in its syllabus, taken from Goju-Ryu through Oyama's training in the Goju-kai.

Some other styles descended from Naha-te, and a number of independent schools, also practise forms called Sanchin. Some are close to the Goju-Ryu kata; others are quite different. The name has travelled further than any single version.

## Why Versions Differ

It helps to understand *why* the kata changes as it passes from teacher to teacher, because that tells you how to deal with the differences you meet.

**Teaching emphasis.** A teacher who believes the breathing is the most important element will tend to lengthen and emphasise it. One who believes the stance matters most will spend more time on stepping and may simplify the arm work. Over a generation, these emphases become visible in the kata itself.

**The student in front of them.** Good teachers adapt. A version taught to a heavy, powerful adult may differ in small ways from one taught to a light, flexible teenager. Some of those adaptations stick.

**Practical changes.** Turns, the number of steps and similar details are sometimes altered to suit the size of a training hall, the needs of a demonstration or the requirements of a grading syllabus.

**Memory.** Every generation remembers a little differently. When a kata is passed on by demonstration and correction rather than in writing, small changes are inevitable.

None of this makes the kata arbitrary. The core of Sanchin, the stance, the connection of the arm to the trunk, the coordinated breath, the controlled tension and the calm mind, is remarkably consistent across all the versions I have seen. What changes is the outer shell.

::: fault
**Problem: "My new dojo does Sanchin differently from my old one, and I keep getting it wrong."**

*What is happening.* You have learned one version thoroughly and your body defaults to it under pressure. The differences are probably small, but they show up at exactly the moments when you are concentrating on something else, such as your breath or a teacher's hand on your shoulder.

*Check.* Write down every difference you have noticed between the two versions: number of steps, direction of turns, hand paths, breathing pattern, finishing position. Ask a senior student to watch your kata and add anything you have missed.

*Cause.* Almost always habit. Occasionally there is a deeper difference in principle, for example a different way of tucking the hips or a different breathing method, that needs to be understood rather than simply memorised.

*Correct.* Practise only the points of difference, slowly and on their own, ten times each before every session, for at least three weeks. Then put them back into the whole kata. Do not try to perform the whole new version at full intensity until the differences have become automatic.

*Confirm.* Ask the same senior student to watch you again. Keep your list and tick off each difference as it settles.

*A final word.* Respect the version your new dojo teaches while you train there. You do not need to forget the old one, but you will learn more by committing fully to the new one than by holding back.
:::

## The Version in This Book

This book describes one version of Sanchin in detail. It is the closed-fist version with turns, as broadly taught in the Okinawan lines descended from Chojun Miyagi. I have chosen it because it is the most widely practised version in the world today, and because its elements, the stance, the step, the block, the punch, the pulling hand, the turns, the double spear-hand and the circular block, include almost everything found in the other versions.

The precise sequence I use, and the points where other lines commonly differ, are set out at the start of Chapter 10. Where your own dojo's version differs, you will find that nearly everything in this book still applies. The stance work in Part Two, the breathing in Part Three, the testing in Part Six, the faults in Part Seven and the conditioning in Part Eight are common to every Goju-Ryu Sanchin I know. Only the frame-by-frame sequence in Part Four is specific to one version, and even there, most frames will match yours.

## Lessons From the History

What does all this mean for your training today? I would draw four lessons from it.

**First, the kata is a means, not an end.** Higaonna and Miyagi both changed Sanchin when they thought a change would help their students. They were not preserving a fixed object; they were using a tool. Your job is to use the tool well, not to worship it.

**Second, the core matters more than the details.** The things every version shares are the things that matter most. When you are short of time, spend it on the stance, the structure and the breath, not on worrying whether your hand should travel two centimetres higher.

**Third, testing and correction by hand are part of the tradition.** Every account of Higaonna and Miyagi describes them correcting students by touch, checking posture with their hands and testing the body. This is not an optional extra added by later teachers. It is how the form was taught from the start, and Part Six of this book explains how to do it well.

**Fourth, the form was meant to make people healthier.** Miyagi in particular was interested in karate as a means of physical education and good health. Practising Sanchin in a way that leaves you dizzy, injured or exhausted goes against the intentions of the man who shaped it. The safety guidance in this book is not a modern dilution of the tradition. It is closer to its spirit than some of the more brutal practices that grew up later.

## Keeping Your Own Record

One more practical point. Because Sanchin varies, and because small details are easily forgotten, it is worth keeping your own written record of the version you are taught. This is not to replace your teacher's instruction but to support it. Many students only realise, years later, that a detail they were once taught has quietly dropped out of their kata.

The record does not need to be elaborate. A page in your training notebook with the sequence of movements, the breathing pattern, the direction of turns and any particular points your teacher has stressed is enough. Date it, and add to it whenever you receive a new correction. When you move dojos, train with a visiting teacher or come back after a break, you will be glad of it.

::: drill
**Drill 2.1 – Write Your Own Sanchin**

*You need:* your training notebook and about twenty minutes.

*What to do:* Without referring to this book, write down the sequence of your Sanchin from the opening bow to the closing bow, one movement per line. For each line, note the stance, which foot is forward, what each hand is doing and whether you breathe in or out. Then add any special points your teacher has emphasised.

*What to look for:* Gaps. If there are movements where you are not sure which hand moves first, or whether you breathe in or out, mark them with a question mark. Those are the points to ask about at your next class.

*Why it matters:* Writing the kata down forces you to know it consciously, not just physically. It also gives you a reference you can compare with the frame-by-frame account in Part Four.
:::

### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin comes from the martial traditions of Fujian in southern China, where forms called *san zhan* are still practised. The exact parent style is uncertain.
- Kanryo Higaonna (1853–1915) brought the form to Naha after years of study in Fuzhou and made it the centre of Naha-te. His version is generally said to have used open hands and no turns.
- Chojun Miyagi (1888–1953) developed Goju-Ryu from Higaonna's teaching, his own research in China and his experience as a teacher. He is credited with closing the hands, adding turns and emphasising a slow, deliberate breath. He also composed Tensho.
- The name Goju-Ryu, hard-soft school, comes from a line in the Bubishi about breathing being both hard and soft.
- After Miyagi's death his senior students taught in their own ways, which is why versions of Sanchin differ between associations. Uechi-Ryu, Shito-Ryu and Kyokushin also practise Sanchin.
- The core of the kata is consistent across versions. When you change dojos, practise the differences in isolation until they become automatic.
- This book describes the closed-fist version with turns. Nearly everything outside Part Four applies equally to other versions.
:::

# The Sanchin Block

::: chapter-intro
The middle-level block is held for most of the kata and returned to after every punch. This chapter looks closely at its shape, how it is formed, how it connects to the back, what it is for, how it is tested and the faults that most often creep into it. By the end you should be able to set your block accurately without a mirror and check it with a partner in under a minute.
:::

If you add up the time spent in each position during a performance of Sanchin, the double middle block wins easily. The arms are in it during every step, every turn and every settle, and each punch begins and ends there. A student who performs the kata for three minutes spends perhaps two of them with at least one arm in the block.

That makes the block the single most practised arm position in Goju-Ryu. It also makes it the place where small faults have the greatest cumulative effect. An elbow that drifts out by two centimetres in the block will drift out in every block of every kata for years, and it will carry that disconnection into the punches that start from it. It is worth getting exactly right.

## What the Block Is

The Sanchin block is a form of *chudan uke*, the middle-level block. In Goju-Ryu it is often called *uchi uke* or *chudan uchi uke*, since the forearm moves from the inside outwards, although terminology varies between associations. Whatever it is called, in Sanchin it is performed with both arms together as *morote chudan uke* at the opening and after each turn, and with one arm at a time as the punching arm returns.

The finished position has the following features:

- **The forearm is upright,** roughly vertical when seen from the side, or tilted very slightly inwards towards the centre line when seen from the front.
- **The fist is at about shoulder height,** in front of the shoulder and slightly outside it, roughly in line with the outer edge of the shoulder.
- **The elbow is down,** about a fist's width away from the side of the body, in front of the lowest ribs. It points down and slightly in.
- **The angle at the elbow** is a little less than a right angle, perhaps seventy to eighty-five degrees, so that the fist is slightly closer to the face than the elbow is to the body.
- **The fist is closed firmly,** with the knuckles facing upward and slightly outward and the inside of the wrist facing towards you and slightly in. The wrist is straight: the back of the hand continues the line of the forearm.
- **The shoulder is down** and the shoulder blade is drawn down into the back. The upper arm is connected to the trunk through the latissimus muscles.

Some teachers ask for the fists to be turned so that the inside of the forearm faces more directly forward; others turn the fists further, so that the little-finger side faces forward. The difference is small and varies between lineages. Follow your teacher, and keep the wrist straight in either case.

## What the Block Is For

There are several ways of understanding the purpose of the Sanchin block, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**As a defence.** In its most direct reading, the block deflects a straight attack to the trunk or face. The forearm, travelling outwards from the centre, meets the attacking arm and carries it off the line. Because the elbow is down and the arm is connected to the back, the block is very strong for its size; it does not need to travel far.

**As a guard.** Held in place, the double block covers the centre of the body. The forearms protect the head and upper trunk, the elbows protect the ribs. Many teachers point out that the Sanchin block resembles the guard used in close-range fighting in many martial arts and combat sports.

**As a structure.** In training, the block is the arm position in which the connection between the arm and the back is easiest to develop and to test. The upright forearm and the low elbow create a short, strong lever that a partner can press against from almost any direction. This is perhaps its most important role in Sanchin.

**As a hub.** Every punch begins and ends in the block, and so do the transitions to the open-hand techniques. In that sense, the block is the home position of the kata, the place the arms return to between actions.

## Forming the Block

There are two ways the block is formed in Sanchin: the double block from the crossed arms, as at the opening and after each turn, and the single block as the punching arm returns. They follow slightly different paths, but they arrive at the same position.

### The double block from the cross

In Frame 4, the arms cross in front of the chest, fists closed, right arm outside the left. From there, both forearms travel outwards and upwards together in a short arc. The movement begins at the elbows, which drop and draw in slightly as the forearms rise, and it is driven by the back: the shoulder blades draw down and the latissimus muscles engage as the arms open. The fists rotate slightly as they travel, arriving with the knuckles up and slightly out.

The key point is that the elbows do not travel outwards with the fists. Many students open the block by swinging the whole arm out from the shoulder, so that the elbows flare and then have to be drawn back in. Instead, the elbows should stay low and close throughout, acting almost as pivots, while the forearms sweep out around them.

### The single block from the punch

After a punch, the arm returns to the block by bending at the elbow and drawing back. The fist travels back and slightly outwards along a curve, and the elbow drops and draws in towards the body. As the forearm comes upright, the fist rotates back from palm-down to its block orientation.

The risk here is that the arm simply retracts, with the elbow travelling straight back along the line of the punch, and the block is then re-formed with a separate small movement. That costs time and breaks the continuity of the kata. Practise the return as a single, curved movement that arrives directly in the block.

::: drill
**Drill 13.1 – Elbow Pivot**

*You need:* a partner, or a wall.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your arms crossed in front of your chest. Ask a partner to place a hand lightly under each of your elbows, or stand with your elbows just touching the edges of a doorway. Open your arms slowly into the double block without letting your elbows move away from your partner's hands or the doorframe. Ten repetitions.

*What to look for:* If your elbows leave your partner's hands, they are travelling outwards with the fists. Slow down and let the forearms rotate around the elbows.

*Why it matters:* This drill teaches the low, close elbow that makes the block strong and connected.
:::

## Connecting the Block to the Back

A block that is the right shape but not connected to the back is only half a block. It looks right in a photograph, but under a partner's hand it collapses.

The connection comes from the same muscles described in Chapter 6. The shoulder blade draws down. The latissimus muscle, running from the back to the front of the upper arm, engages and presses the upper arm gently down and towards the side of the body. The armpit closes. When this happens, the upper arm is anchored to the trunk, and a force on the forearm is transmitted through the elbow into the back and down into the legs.

You can feel this clearly with a partner. Hold the block and ask them to push down on the top of your fist, slowly. If the connection is missing, your forearm will fold down towards your shoulder, or your shoulder will lift. If it is present, the push will be felt in your back and, if they push hard enough, in your legs. The forearm will stay upright.

Now ask them to push your forearm outwards, away from your centre line, from the inside of your wrist. Without connection, the arm will swing out from the shoulder. With connection, the push is absorbed by the back, and the arm stays where it is.

These are two of the standard tests of the Sanchin block. They, and others, are described in full in Chapter 17.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Three-Push Block Check**

With a partner, set your double block in sanchin dachi. Ask your partner to apply each of the following pushes, slowly, building pressure over three seconds, one arm at a time.

1. **Down** on the top of the fist.
2. **Outwards** on the inside of the wrist.
3. **Inwards** on the outside of the wrist, towards your centre line.

For each push, note whether the forearm stayed upright, whether your shoulder rose and whether you felt the push in your back. Repeat on the other arm. Most students find one direction, and one arm, noticeably weaker. That is the one to work on.
:::

## Common Faults of the Block

The following faults are the ones I see most often. Each is shown with its most likely cause and its correction.

### Elbow flaring out

The elbow drifts away from the body, out to the side, so that the upper arm angles outwards and the forearm leans in. This is the commonest block fault. It disconnects the arm from the back, so that the block feels strong only as long as the shoulder muscles hold it up, and it opens the ribs.

*Cause.* Usually the shoulder blade is not drawn down, so the upper arm hangs from the shoulder rather than being anchored to the back. Sometimes the student is trying to make the block "wider" for protection.

*Correction.* Drill 13.1, and the armpit-closing exercise in Drill 6.2. Think of the elbow as heavy and drawn down towards your hip.

### Fist too high

The fist rises above the shoulder, sometimes to the level of the eyes or higher. The forearm may still be upright, but the whole block has risen.

*Cause.* Often the shoulder is rising with the block; occasionally a student has been taught a higher block in another context and carried it over.

*Correction.* Check the fist height against your shoulder in a mirror. If the shoulder is rising, work on the shoulder draw-down from Chapter 6 first.

### Fist too wide or too narrow

The fist is well outside the line of the shoulder, so that the forearm leans outwards, or well inside it, so that the forearm crosses towards the centre.

*Cause.* A wide fist usually goes with a flared elbow. A narrow fist often comes from over-squeezing the elbows inwards, or from a misunderstanding of "protecting the centre".

*Correction.* Set the fist in line with the outer edge of the shoulder. A useful check is that, looking straight ahead, you should just be able to see the tops of both fists at the edges of your vision without moving your eyes.

### Bent wrist

The fist is bent forward, backward or sideways at the wrist, so that the back of the hand does not continue the line of the forearm.

*Cause.* Usually weak forearm muscles, especially in beginners, or a fist that is clenched hardest at the index finger and thumb rather than at the little finger.

*Correction.* Re-form the fist with the grip strongest at the little and ring fingers. Strengthen the forearms with the gripping exercises in Chapter 23.

### Collapsed angle

The forearm leans back towards the shoulder, closing the angle at the elbow, so that the fist is almost at the shoulder.

*Cause.* Lack of connection: the forearm is held up by the biceps rather than braced by the back. It often appears late in the kata as the arms tire.

*Correction.* Practise holding the block at a slightly more open angle, with the fist a little further forward, and ask a partner to apply the downward push from the three-push check.

### Asymmetrical blocks

The two blocks in the double block are at different heights, widths or angles.

*Cause.* Nearly always a difference in strength or flexibility between the two sides, or a slight rotation of the trunk.

*Correction.* Practise in front of a mirror with tape marks for fist height. If the trunk is rotating, check that your hips are square and your front foot angle is correct.

::: fault
**Problem: "My forearms burn so much I can barely hold the block by the end of the kata."**

*Check.* Ask a partner to press on your block at the start of the kata and again near the end. Is the shape changing as you tire? Is your fist clenched at maximum throughout?

*Cause.* Usually a combination of over-gripping and under-connecting. The fist is held at maximum grip for the whole kata, which exhausts the forearm muscles, and the arm is held up by the arm muscles rather than braced by the back, which adds to the load.

*Correct.* Let the grip ease to about half between techniques, tightening only at the end of each block or punch. Focus on drawing the shoulder blades down and closing the armpits, so that the back takes more of the work. Build forearm endurance with the gripping work in Chapter 23.

*Confirm.* The burning should become manageable within two or three weeks. Some fatigue is normal and is part of the training.
:::

## The Block in Motion

So far we have described the block as a position. In the kata, though, it is rarely completely still. During the steps it has to stay in place while the body moves beneath it. During the turns it opens from the cross. After each punch it re-forms. And between techniques the tension in it rises and falls with the breath.

The common principle in all of these is that the block is carried by the trunk, not held up by the arms. When you step, the block does not move relative to your body because the back is holding it there; you do not need to hold it up with your shoulders. When the tension rises at the end of a technique, it rises in the back and the arm together. When it eases, the arm does not drop, because the baseline tension in the back is still supporting it.

Students who have understood this find that their blocks become remarkably steady. The arms seem to float in position, apparently without effort, and yet they are hard to move. That combination, light to look at and solid to the touch, is the mark of a mature Sanchin block.

::: drill
**Drill 13.2 – The Carried Block**

*You need:* a partner and a light stick or a rolled-up towel.

*What to do:* Set your double block. Ask your partner to rest the stick across the tops of both your fists. Perform five forward steps and five backward steps with the crescent step, slowly, without letting the stick roll or fall.

*Progression:* Have your partner walk alongside you with one hand resting lightly on your elbow. They should feel no movement of the elbow at all during the steps.

*Why it matters:* This drill trains the block to be carried by the trunk rather than held by the arms.
:::

## Strengthening the Block

The block depends on three groups of muscles: the forearm and grip muscles, which hold the fist and wrist; the muscles around the shoulder blade, which anchor the shoulder; and the latissimus and other back muscles, which connect the arm to the trunk. All three can be strengthened directly, and doing so makes the block much easier to hold.

A short sequence that targets all three is given below. Part Eight places it in the context of a full programme, along with the traditional Okinawan tools that were used for the same purpose.

::: drill
**Drill 13.3 – Block Builders**

1. **Isometric block press.** Stand in a doorway in sanchin dachi. Place the outside of your forearm, in the block position, against the doorframe. Press outwards for ten seconds at about half effort, drawing the shoulder blade down. Rest ten seconds. Five times on each arm.
2. **Band pull-apart.** Hold a light resistance band in front of you at shoulder height, arms straight, hands shoulder-width apart. Pull the band apart by drawing your shoulder blades together and down, until your hands are out to your sides. Return slowly. Three sets of fifteen.
3. **Straight-arm pulldown.** Attach a resistance band above head height. Facing it, hold the band with straight arms and pull it down to your thighs, drawing the shoulder blades down. You should feel the latissimus muscles working at the sides of your back. Three sets of twelve.
4. **Wrist curls.** Holding a light weight, rest your forearm on your thigh with the wrist over the knee. Curl the weight up slowly, palm up, then palm down. Three sets of fifteen each way.

*Why it matters:* These four exercises cover the three muscle groups the block depends on. Two short sessions a week for a month will make a noticeable difference.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The block is the most-practised arm position in Sanchin: held during steps and turns, and the start and end point of every punch.
- The finished block has an upright forearm, the fist at shoulder height in line with the outer edge of the shoulder, the elbow a fist's width from the body in front of the lower ribs, a straight wrist and a shoulder drawn down.
- It serves as a defence, a guard, a structure for training connection and a hub for the other techniques.
- The double block opens from the crossed arms around low, close elbows. The single block forms from the punch along a single curved path.
- The block must be connected to the back. Test it with downward, outward and inward pushes.
- Common faults are flared elbows, fists too high, too wide or too narrow, bent wrists, collapsed angles and asymmetry. Most begin with the shoulder blade not being drawn down.
- A mature block is carried by the trunk: light to look at and solid to the touch.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Set your block in front of a mirror and check each feature on the list at the start of the chapter. Mark your fist height on the mirror with tape.
2. Practise Drill 13.1, the elbow pivot, ten repetitions at the start of each session for two weeks.
3. Carry out the Three-Push Block Check with a partner. Record which push and which arm was weakest.
4. Practise Drill 13.2, the carried block, with the stick across your fists, three times a week.
5. Add Drill 13.3, block builders, to your training twice a week for a month.
6. Watch your latest recording of the whole kata. Pause at five different moments when your arms are in the block. Are they the same each time?
:::

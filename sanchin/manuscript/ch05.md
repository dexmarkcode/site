# Knees, Hips and the Pelvic Lock

::: chapter-intro
With the feet set, we move up the leg to the two joints that make or break the stance. This chapter explains how far to bend the knees, where they should point, how the inward pressure of the thighs is produced without strain, and how to set the pelvis so that the weight sinks and the lower back stays long. It ends with tests you can carry out with a partner and on your own, and with the most common faults of the knee and hip.
:::

If the feet are the foundation of Sanchin, the knees and hips are its keystone. Almost every teacher I have trained with has spent more time correcting these two joints than any other part of the body. They are where the stance either comes together or falls apart.

They are also where most of the confusion lies. Students hear "knees in" and pull their knees together until they nearly touch, collapsing the arches of their feet and straining the inside of the knee. They hear "tuck your hips" and roll the pelvis so far under that they lean backwards and lose all the spring from their legs. In each case the instruction was right, but the amount was wrong. This chapter is largely about amounts.

## The Bend of the Knees

In sanchin dachi both knees are bent, but not deeply. When you look down, you should just about be able to see the tips of your toes past your kneecaps, or the kneecaps should sit roughly above the front of the foot. The hips drop by only a few centimetres compared with ordinary standing.

The bend has two purposes. It lowers the centre of mass slightly, which makes the stance more stable. And it puts the large muscles of the thighs and hips to work, so that they can brace the stance and drive the steps. A stance with straight legs relies on the joints themselves to hold it up. It feels stable until someone pushes it, and then it gives way all at once. A stance with bent legs relies on muscle, which can respond to a push.

There are two common errors. The first is too little bend: the knees are almost straight, the hips are high and the stance is brittle. This is especially common in tall students and in anyone who has trained in a style with high, upright stances. The second is too much bend: the student sits low, as if in a deep horse stance, and the thighs quickly tire. A deep stance looks strong, but it makes stepping heavy, it overloads the knees, and it cannot be held for the length of the kata without the posture collapsing.

As a rough guide, if you were to measure the angle at the back of the knee, it would be somewhere in the region of one hundred and forty to one hundred and sixty degrees, not the ninety or so of a deep stance. But do not reach for a protractor. A better check is the one below.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Spring Test**

Stand in your Sanchin stance. Without changing the position of your feet, make three small, quick bounces by flexing and extending your knees a centimetre or two. If you cannot bounce without first straightening your legs, you are too low. If the bounce comes entirely from the ankles and the knees hardly move, you are too high. The right height is one from which you could spring in any direction without first having to adjust.
:::

## Where the Knees Point

The second question is direction. In Sanchin, the knees are drawn inwards, towards the centre line, and slightly forwards. This is what gives the stance its characteristic look, and it is what protects the groin and creates the inward bracing of the legs.

The key rule is that **each knee stays in line with its own foot**. Look down: the centre of each kneecap should sit roughly above the second or third toe of the same foot. Because the front foot is turned inwards by twenty to thirty degrees, the front knee points inwards by the same amount. Because the rear foot points forward or slightly inwards, the rear knee points forward or slightly inwards.

This rule protects the knee. The knee is essentially a hinge. It is happy to bend and straighten in line with the foot, but it does not like being twisted or pushed sideways under load. When the knee points in the same direction as the foot, the joint is aligned and the muscles around it can do their job. When the knee falls inside the foot, or drifts outside it, the joint is twisted, and over time that can cause pain or injury.

### The squeeze without the collapse

If the knees only point the same way as the feet, where does the inward pressure come from? It comes from the thighs. With the knees aligned over the feet, you then press the thighs gently towards each other, as if squeezing a large cushion between them. The knees themselves hardly move inwards at all, perhaps a centimetre or two. What changes is the tension: the muscles on the inside of the thighs, the adductors, engage, and the whole leg firms from the hip to the foot.

It may help to think of this as a gentle tug of war. The feet grip the floor and try to keep the knees in line above them. The thighs press inwards. Neither wins; the leg is braced between them. If the thighs win, the knees collapse inwards. If the feet win, the knees drift out. The stance is strongest when they are balanced.

Many teachers describe a spiral running through the leg: the feet screwing very slightly outwards into the floor, without actually moving, while the thighs press inwards. You do not need to feel a spiral to stand well, but if the image helps you, use it.

::: fault
**Problem: "My knees hurt after Sanchin practice."**

*Stop first.* If you have sharp pain in the knee, or pain that lasts into the next day, stop practising the stance and have the knee checked by a physiotherapist or doctor. Knee pain is not a normal part of training.

*Check.* If the pain is mild and settles quickly, ask a partner to watch your knees from the front during a full kata, or film yourself. Watch especially during the steps and turns.

*Cause.* The most common causes are, in order: knees collapsing inwards past the feet; feet turned in further than the knees can follow; standing too low for too long; and twisting on a planted foot during the turns.

*Correct.* Reset your foot angle to the lower end of the range, about twenty degrees for the front foot. Make sure each kneecap sits over the second or third toe. Raise your stance by a few centimetres. Practise the knee alignment drill below daily for two weeks before returning to full practice. Chapter 12 deals with the turns.

*Confirm.* The pain should not return. If it does, seek professional advice before continuing.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 5.1 – Knee Tracking**

*You need:* a mirror at floor level, or a phone propped up in front of you, and a strip of tape or a pen mark on each kneecap.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi facing the mirror. Slowly bend and straighten your knees by a few centimetres, ten times, watching the marks on your kneecaps. They should move straight up and down over the second or third toe. If they wander inwards or outwards as you bend, adjust the foot angle or the inward squeeze until they travel straight. Then change feet and repeat.

*Progression:* Once the knees track straight, add the crescent step. Step slowly from one stance to the next, keeping the marks over the toes throughout.

*Why it matters:* Knee tracking is the single most important thing you can do to keep your knees healthy in Sanchin. It also makes the stance noticeably stronger.
:::

## The Pelvis

Now we reach the hips, and the instruction that causes more confusion than any other in Sanchin: tuck the hips under.

### What "tucking" means

The pelvis can tilt forwards and backwards. When it tilts forwards, the tailbone moves back and up, the lower back arches, and the belly tends to push forward. This is the posture of someone standing with a hollow back and their bottom sticking out. When it tilts backwards, the tailbone moves down and forward, the lower back flattens, and the hips move slightly forwards under the trunk. This is a tucked pelvis.

In Sanchin, the pelvis is tucked slightly, from a neutral position towards the backward tilt. The aim is not to flatten the lower back completely, which would tend to make you lean back and would remove the natural spring from the spine. It is to remove the excessive arch that many people carry in their lower back, so that the trunk sits directly above the pelvis and the weight can sink straight down through the legs.

![**Figure 5.2** The pelvis seen from the side. Left: tilted forward, with a hollow lower back and the bottom pushed back. The weight hangs in front of the hips. Centre: the Sanchin position, slightly tucked, with a long lower back and the trunk stacked above the pelvis. Right: over-tucked, with the lower back flattened, the hips pushed forward and the upper body leaning back.](figures/fig5_2.png)

### How to tuck without strain

The tuck should come from the muscles that are designed to produce it, not from forcing the pelvis into position. Three muscle groups are involved:

- **The buttocks.** The large gluteal muscles, when gently engaged, draw the back of the pelvis down. You should feel your buttocks firm, as though drawing slightly together and down, not clench.
- **The lower abdomen.** The deep muscles of the lower abdomen draw the front of the pelvis up. You should feel a gentle firmness just above the pubic bone.
- **The pelvic floor.** The muscles that form the floor of the pelvis lift very slightly. Older teachers often describe this as drawing up the groin or the anus. In modern terms, it is the same gentle lift you would use to stop yourself passing wind in polite company.

When these three work together at modest effort, the pelvis tucks smoothly, the lower back lengthens and the hips feel braced rather than locked. None of them should be at maximum effort. If your buttocks are clenched hard, your hip joints will lock and you will lose the spring in your legs. If your abdomen is sucked in hard, your breath will rise into your chest.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Wall Check**

Stand with your back against a wall, heels about a foot's length away from it, knees slightly bent. Slide one hand into the gap between your lower back and the wall.

1. If you can fit your whole hand in easily, with room to spare, your pelvis is tilted forward and your lower back is arched.
2. Gently engage your buttocks and lower abdomen to tuck the pelvis. The gap should shrink until your hand is lightly trapped against the wall.
3. If the gap closes completely and your back presses hard into your hand, you have over-tucked. Ease back slightly.

The position where your hand is just held, without being squashed, is close to the Sanchin tuck. Step away from the wall and try to keep it.
:::

### The pelvic lock

When the knees are braced inwards and the pelvis is tucked, something happens that is greater than either action alone. The thighs, the hips and the lower trunk join into a single structure. Teachers sometimes call this the pelvic lock, or the lock of the hips. It is what allows force to travel from the legs into the trunk without being lost at the waist.

You can feel it quite clearly. Stand in sanchin dachi with your knees aligned and your pelvis tucked. Ask a partner to push gently on your shoulder from the front. You should feel the push travel down through your trunk, through your hips and into your rear foot. Now let your pelvis tilt forward, hollowing your back, and ask for the same push. You will probably feel your trunk rock back at the waist, as though hinged there. That hinge is the leak the pelvic lock closes.

## Hip Height and Level

Two final points about the hips.

First, the hips should be level from side to side. In sanchin dachi, with one foot forward, it is easy to let the hip on the side of the front foot rise or drop, or to let the front hip swing forward or back. Look in a mirror from the front: an imaginary line across the tops of your hip bones should be horizontal. From above, the hips should face almost straight forward, turned very slightly, if at all, towards the side of the rear foot.

Second, the height of the hips should stay constant throughout the kata. We have already seen in Chapter 4 that bobbing during the step is a common fault. It is also common to rise slightly at the end of each punch, as the student drives into the floor and straightens the legs without meaning to. Keep the height. The drive should be felt as pressure into the floor, not as a rise.

::: fault
**Problem: "My teacher keeps telling me to tuck my hips, but I already am."**

*Check.* Film yourself from the side, at hip height, during the kata. Pause on each punch and look at the line of your lower back and the position of your bottom.

*Cause.* You may be tucking at the start of the kata and losing it during movement. The most common moments are the step, when the rear leg pushes and the pelvis tips forward; the punch, when the shoulder reaches and the back arches; and the inhalation, when the belly expands and the lower back hollows.

*Correct.* Practise each of these moments in isolation, using the wall check before and after. For the step, practise ten crescent steps with your hand on your lower back, feeling for any change. For the punch, practise slow punches in a fixed stance with your back an inch from a wall, so that you notice if your bottom touches it. For the inhalation, see Chapter 7 on breathing into the back and sides as well as the front.

*Confirm.* Film again at the same three moments.
:::

## The Groin and the Inner Thighs

The inward bracing of the thighs and the lift of the pelvic floor together protect the groin, and this is often given as one of the original reasons for the shape of sanchin dachi. Some older accounts describe teachers testing the closure of a student's groin by kicking it. That practice is dangerous, unnecessary and should never be carried out. The structure can be checked perfectly well, and much more safely, by testing the thighs and knees as described in Part Six.

What matters for your training is that the inner thighs should stay engaged throughout the kata, including during steps and turns. They are the muscles most likely to switch off when you are concentrating on something else, and when they switch off, the knees drift out and the groin opens. A simple reminder is to imagine a sheet of paper held between your thighs, high up, which you must not drop.

::: drill
**Drill 5.2 – Cushion Squeeze**

*You need:* a firm cushion, a rolled towel or a soft ball about the size of a football.

*What to do:* Stand in a parallel stance, knees slightly bent, with the cushion held between your thighs just above the knees. Tuck your pelvis. Gently squeeze the cushion with your inner thighs and hold for ten seconds, breathing normally. Release for five seconds. Repeat ten times.

*Progression 1:* Do the same in sanchin dachi, with the cushion between your thighs. You will need to bring the knees slightly closer than usual to hold it; this is acceptable for the drill.

*Progression 2:* Hold the cushion in sanchin dachi while performing slow punches with alternate hands. The cushion must not drop or slip.

*Why it matters:* Many students cannot feel their inner thighs working. The cushion gives them something to squeeze against and makes the sensation obvious. After a few weeks, you will be able to find the same tension without it.
:::

## Partner Tests for the Knees and Hips

The following tests are gentle checks, suitable for any student who has learned the basic stance. They are the starting point for the more thorough testing described in Part Six. Use steady pressure, never sudden force, and stop at once if either partner feels pain.

**Knee-out test.** The tester kneels beside the student and places one hand on the inside of the student's front knee. They press slowly outwards, building pressure over two or three seconds. The student resists by pressing the thigh inwards while keeping the foot gripping and the knee over the toes. The knee should move very little. Repeat for the rear knee.

**Knee-in test.** The tester places a hand on the outside of the knee and presses slowly inwards. The student resists without letting the knee collapse. This is usually easier for the student than the knee-out test; if the knee does collapse inwards, the foot is probably rolling.

**Hip push.** The tester stands beside the student and places a hand on the side of the student's hip, at belt height. They push slowly sideways. The student should be able to absorb the push through both legs without the hips shifting or the upper body tilting.

**Back-of-pelvis check.** The tester places the flat of one hand on the student's lower back, just above the belt. The student breathes in. The tester should feel the back widen slightly under their hand, but should not feel the lower back hollow away from it.

## Strength for the Knees and Hips

Holding sanchin dachi correctly for the length of the kata, especially under testing, requires a fair amount of strength in the thighs, the buttocks and the muscles around the hip. Many students, particularly beginners and those returning after a break, simply do not yet have it. Their stance starts well and gradually deteriorates as the muscles tire.

The answer is not to stand in the stance for longer and longer periods, gritting your teeth. That mostly trains your ability to tolerate a poor stance. A better answer is to build the strength separately, with exercises designed for the purpose, and then let the stance benefit. Part Eight sets out a full strength programme. The following three exercises are a good start.

::: drill
**Drill 5.3 – Stance Foundations**

1. **Wall sit, knees aligned.** Lean your back against a wall and slide down until your knees are bent to roughly the Sanchin angle, not a deep squat. Feet hip-width apart, knees over toes, pelvis gently tucked so the lower back is close to the wall. Hold for thirty seconds, rest thirty seconds. Repeat four times. Increase to sixty seconds over a few weeks.
2. **Glute bridge.** Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat. Press through your heels, tuck your pelvis and lift your hips until your body is in a straight line from knees to shoulders. Hold for two seconds at the top, squeezing the buttocks. Lower slowly. Three sets of twelve.
3. **Side-lying leg lift.** Lie on your side with your legs straight. Lift the top leg about thirty centimetres, keeping the toes pointing forward, not up. Lower slowly. Three sets of fifteen on each side. This strengthens the muscles at the side of the hip that keep the knees from collapsing inwards.

*Why it matters:* These three exercises target the muscles that hold the knees in line and the pelvis tucked. Two or three short sessions a week will make a noticeable difference to your stance within a month.
:::

## Bringing the Lower Body Together

You now have all the elements of the lower body of sanchin dachi: the feet set and gripping, the knees bent and aligned, the thighs pressing gently inwards, the pelvis tucked and the hips level. Each has been checked on its own. The final step is to bring them together and hold them together while you move.

That takes time. For the first few weeks, you will probably find that you can manage two or three of the elements at once, and that the others slip whenever you attend to something else. This is entirely normal. Keep returning to the checks. Use the Check, Cause, Correct and Confirm loop from Chapter 1. Gradually, the elements will stop being separate instructions and become a single feeling: the feeling of standing in Sanchin.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The knees are bent only slightly, so that you could spring in any direction. Too straight is brittle; too deep is tiring and overloads the knees.
- Each knee stays in line with its own foot, with the kneecap over the second or third toe. This protects the joint and strengthens the stance.
- The inward pressure comes from the thighs squeezing gently towards each other, not from collapsing the knees inwards.
- The pelvis is tucked slightly from neutral, using the buttocks, lower abdomen and pelvic floor at modest effort. The lower back lengthens but does not flatten completely.
- Braced knees and a tucked pelvis together form the pelvic lock, which lets force travel between the legs and trunk.
- The hips stay level and at a constant height throughout the kata.
- Never test the groin by striking it. Check the structure by testing the knees, thighs and hips instead.
- Many students lack the strength to hold the stance well. Build it with separate exercises rather than longer and longer stance holds.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Carry out the Spring Test and the Wall Check. Record what you found, and what you had to change.
2. Practise Drill 5.1, knee tracking, every day for two weeks. Film the first and last sessions from the front and compare.
3. With a partner, carry out the four partner checks in this chapter. For each, note whether your structure held, and if not, what gave way.
4. Practise Drill 5.2 with the cushion three times a week. When you can perform slow punches without dropping the cushion, try the same punches without it and see if the feeling stays.
5. Add Drill 5.3 to your training two or three times a week for a month. Note any change in how long you can hold your stance before it starts to deteriorate.
6. Watch your side-on baseline recording from Chapter 1. Look at your pelvis on every punch. Does it stay tucked, or does it tilt forward?
:::

# Sanchin and Tensho

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin has a partner. Tensho, composed by Chojun Miyagi in the early twentieth century, uses the same stance and breath but replaces Sanchin's straight lines and closed fists with flowing, circular open-hand movements. This chapter explains how the two kata relate, what Tensho adds, when to begin it and how to practise the two together. It closes by tracing Sanchin's structure through the other kata of Goju-Ryu.
:::

Goju-Ryu is the hard-soft style, and for most of this book we have concentrated on the hard. Sanchin is not entirely hard, as Chapter 3 explained: its tension rises and falls like a wave, its face and mind stay soft, and it ends with open hands and circular blocks. But its character is unmistakably firm. Straight lines, closed fists, braced structure, strong breath.

Tensho is the other half. Its name is usually translated as "rotating palms" or "turning hands", and that is what it is: a slow kata of continuous, circular open-hand movements, performed in sanchin dachi with controlled breathing and tension that flows rather than braces. If Sanchin teaches you to be a wall, Tensho teaches you to be a wheel.

Many teachers describe the two as a single practice in two parts. You cannot fully understand one without the other. This chapter is an introduction to Tensho from the point of view of a Sanchin student. It does not teach Tensho movement by movement, which would need a book of its own; it explains what Tensho is for, how it relates to Sanchin and how each helps the other.

## The Origins of Tensho

Tensho is generally credited to Chojun Miyagi and dated to the early 1920s. It is usually said to have been inspired by a set of hand techniques recorded in the Bubishi, the Chinese text from which Miyagi also took the name of his style. This set is commonly known in Okinawa as *Rokkishu*, "six hand techniques", and is illustrated in the Bubishi with a series of figures demonstrating open-hand blocks and strikes. Miyagi is said to have developed these into a complete kata, adding the stance, breath and structure of Sanchin.

As with much early karate history, the details are not documented as fully as a historian would wish, and some researchers have suggested other influences. But the general picture, that Tensho is Miyagi's own composition, built on Sanchin's foundations and drawing on Chinese open-hand methods, is widely accepted.

Its place in the Goju-Ryu syllabus is fairly consistent. Most associations teach Tensho after Sanchin is reasonably settled, often at intermediate coloured-belt level. Some teach it earlier, some later. In most dojos, once a student has learned both, the two are practised together, often one after the other at the start or end of class.

## What Tensho Is Like

In outline, Tensho is performed as follows. The student opens with the same formal opening as Sanchin, steps into sanchin dachi and then, for most of the kata, stays in place, performing a long sequence of open-hand techniques with one hand and then the other, before stepping back and closing. In the most common versions there is relatively little stepping and no turning.

The techniques are mostly circular blocks and palm strikes: hands that rotate at the wrist, sweep in arcs, hook, press and push. Several distinctive shapes appear, including the bent-wrist block, in which the back of the wrist is used to deflect, and the palm-heel push, in which the heel of the open hand drives forward as in the tiger's mouth. The circular block, *mawashi uke*, which closes Sanchin, also appears in Tensho, as do other movements that will be familiar from the end of Sanchin.

The breath is coordinated with every movement, but the overall character is softer than in Sanchin. Many teachers emphasise the quiet nogare breath in Tensho, or a breath that is firm but less forceful than Sanchin's. The tension, too, is different. It does not peak sharply at the end of each technique and then ease; it flows continuously through each movement, like a hand moving through thick liquid. Some teachers describe it as "soft tension" or "flowing tension".

## Two Halves of One Practice

The relationship between the two kata can be set out in a simple table.

| | Sanchin | Tensho |
|---|---|---|
| Composed | Older; brought from China, adapted by Higaonna and Miyagi | Composed by Miyagi, early 1920s |
| Hands | Mostly closed fists; open at the end | Open throughout |
| Paths | Mostly straight | Mostly circular |
| Character | Hard (go), braced | Soft (ju), flowing |
| Breath | Firmer; ibuki emphasised | Softer; nogare emphasised |
| Tension | Builds to a peak with each technique, then eases | Flows continuously through each movement |
| Stance | Sanchin dachi, with stepping and turns | Sanchin dachi, mostly in place |
| Trains | Structure, rooting, connection, endurance | Sensitivity, circular power, the wrist and hand, softness under control |

: **Table 25.1** Sanchin and Tensho compared. Details vary between lineages.

The table shows how the two complement each other. Sanchin builds the body's structure; Tensho teaches it to move within that structure without losing it. Sanchin builds strength; Tensho teaches the strength to be applied smoothly and redirected. Sanchin trains the body to resist force; Tensho trains it to receive and turn force.

There is also a deeper link. The structure that Sanchin develops is what makes Tensho possible. Without a rooted stance, a connected trunk and a calm breath, Tensho's circular movements become weak, floppy arm-waving. And the softness that Tensho develops is what stops Sanchin becoming rigid. Students who practise only Sanchin often find their kata hardening over the years into something stiff and effortful. Tensho keeps the Ju in the Go.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

I sometimes describe the two kata to students with a simple image. Sanchin is learning to stand in a river without being swept away. Tensho is learning to move your hands in the river without fighting the current. You need the first before you can do the second. But once you can do the second, the first becomes much easier, because you stop fighting the water and start using it.
:::

## When to Begin Tensho

Follow your teacher's syllabus. In general, though, Tensho makes most sense once the following are in place:

- your sanchin dachi is stable and does not need constant attention;
- your breathing is reliably at Stage 3 or above without holding;
- you can perform Sanchin with your face and shoulders soft;
- you have some experience of being tested at Level 2.

Students who begin Tensho before these are in place usually find that their stance collapses as their attention moves to their hands, and that their breathing becomes muddled by the more continuous movements. That is not a reason never to try it, but it is a reason to keep Sanchin as the priority until the foundation is secure.

## What Tensho Teaches a Sanchin Student

Even if you have not yet learned Tensho, you can bring some of its lessons into your Sanchin.

### Continuity

Tensho has no stop-start. Every movement flows into the next. Sanchin students who learn Tensho often find their Sanchin becomes noticeably smoother, particularly in the transitions discussed in Chapter 9.

### The wrist and hand

Tensho trains the wrist and fingers through a wide range of positions under tension. This strengthens the forearm and improves control of the open hands, which helps the spear-hands and circular blocks at the end of Sanchin.

### Soft tension

Tensho teaches a kind of tension that is firm but not rigid, like the tension of a well-strung bow rather than a locked door. Once you have felt it in Tensho, you can begin to bring it into Sanchin, particularly in the baseline zone between techniques.

### Sensitivity

Tensho's circular blocks are designed to meet and redirect force rather than stop it. Practising them, especially with a partner, develops sensitivity to pressure and direction. This sensitivity helps both in testing, where it makes you aware of exactly where a push is coming from, and in application, as Chapter 26 explains.

::: drill
**Drill 25.1 – Soft Circles in Sanchin Dachi**

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your arms in the double block, fists open. Keeping the stance, pelvis and shoulders exactly as in Sanchin, let one hand begin to turn slowly at the wrist, the palm rotating from facing you, to facing out, to facing down and back, in a smooth, continuous circle. Breathe slowly and quietly. Let the tension in the forearm flow evenly through the whole circle, never peaking, never dropping. Ten circles with each hand, then ten with both together.

*What to look for:* The stance and shoulders must not change. All the movement is in the forearm and hand; all the stability is in the body.

*Why it matters:* This simple drill gives a Sanchin student a first taste of Tensho's quality: soft, continuous, controlled movement on top of a firm structure. Practised before Sanchin, it often helps the kata feel less rigid.
:::

## Practising the Two Together

Once you know both kata, there are several useful ways of combining them in a session.

**Sanchin then Tensho.** The traditional order in many dojos. Sanchin sets the structure and warms the body; Tensho then softens and refines it. A good pattern for most sessions.

**Tensho then Sanchin.** Useful on days when your Sanchin is feeling stiff or effortful. Tensho first loosens the body and quietens the breath, so that Sanchin can be performed with less excess tension.

**Alternating.** Sanchin, Tensho, Sanchin. The second Sanchin often feels quite different from the first, carrying some of Tensho's softness.

**Paired techniques.** Choose a Sanchin technique and a related Tensho technique and practise them alternately: for example, the Sanchin punch and Tensho's palm-heel push, or the Sanchin block and Tensho's bent-wrist block. Notice what is shared and what is different.

## Common Problems When Learning Tensho

Students who come to Tensho from Sanchin tend to meet the same three or four problems. They are worth knowing about in advance, because each one also says something about the student's Sanchin.

::: fault
**Problem: "My Tensho looks like Sanchin with open hands."**

*Cause.* The student is carrying Sanchin's peak-and-ease tension into Tensho, so that each circular movement stops at a hard end-point instead of flowing into the next. The hands open, but the quality has not changed.

*Correct.* Practise the hand movements of Tensho, or Drill 25.1, at very low tension, around two on the scale, with natural breathing, until they become genuinely continuous. Only then raise the tension, keeping it even throughout each movement rather than letting it build to a peak.

*What it says about your Sanchin.* You may be relying on peak tension rather than baseline structure. Check that your baseline zone stays engaged between techniques.
:::

::: fault
**Problem: "When I concentrate on my hands in Tensho, my stance falls apart."**

*Cause.* The stance is not yet automatic. In Sanchin, the movements are simple enough that some attention is left over for the stance. In Tensho, the hands demand more attention, and the stance loses out.

*Correct.* Return to Sanchin and the stance drills of Part Two for a few more weeks. In Tensho, use the single-check practice: attend only to the stance for a whole performance, letting the hands be imperfect.

*What it says about your Sanchin.* Your stance is still being held by attention rather than by habit. That is normal, but it is worth working on.
:::

::: fault
**Problem: "Tensho makes my wrists ache."**

*Cause.* Tensho asks the wrists to bend and rotate under tension through positions most people rarely use. If the forearm muscles are weak, or the tension is too high, the wrist joints and tendons take the strain.

*Correct.* Reduce tension in the wrists. Strengthen the forearms with the makiage kigu and rice bucket from Chapter 23. Warm the wrists with gentle circles before practice. If pain persists, see a physiotherapist.

*What it says about your Sanchin.* Probably nothing directly, but stronger forearms will also help your Sanchin blocks hold late in the kata.
:::

## Sanchin in the Other Kata

Finally, it is worth looking beyond Tensho to the rest of the Goju-Ryu syllabus, because Sanchin's influence is everywhere.

**Sanchin dachi itself** appears in several of the older kata, including Seisan, Sanseiru and Suparinpei, as well as in the openings and some sections of others. When it appears, everything you have learned in Part Two applies directly.

**Sanchin's opening** appears, in whole or in part, at the start of several kata, particularly the step into sanchin dachi with the double block.

**The circular block and tiger's mouth** appear in many kata, sometimes in exactly the form described in Chapter 15, sometimes with variations.

**Sanchin's structure**, the rooted stance, connected trunk and closed body, applies to every kata, whatever the stance. When you perform Seiyunchin in shiko dachi, or Kururunfa with its fast changes of direction, your teacher may tell you to "put more Sanchin in it". They mean: keep the feet gripping, the pelvis tucked, the back wide, the shoulders down and the breath low.

A useful exercise, once you know several kata, is to go through each one looking for Sanchin. Where does the stance appear? Where does the double block appear? Where would the kata collapse without Sanchin's structure? You will find more than you expect.

::: test
**Test Yourself: Finding Sanchin**

Choose another kata you know well. Perform it once slowly, and at each technique ask: is my structure the same as in Sanchin? Are my feet gripping, my pelvis tucked, my shoulders down, my back wide, my breath low? Note the techniques where the answer is no. Those are the places where your Sanchin training has not yet reached the rest of your karate.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Tensho, "rotating palms", was composed by Chojun Miyagi in the early 1920s, probably inspired by the Rokkishu hand techniques of the Bubishi, and built on Sanchin's foundations.
- Tensho uses open hands, circular paths, flowing tension and a softer breath, in sanchin dachi, mostly in place.
- The two kata are complementary halves of one practice: Sanchin builds structure; Tensho teaches the structure to move, receive and redirect.
- Begin Tensho when your Sanchin stance, breath and soft zone are reliable.
- Tensho teaches a Sanchin student continuity, wrist and hand control, soft tension and sensitivity.
- Practise the two together in different orders for different purposes.
- Sanchin's stance, opening, closing techniques and above all its structure appear throughout the Goju-Ryu kata.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Practise Drill 25.1, soft circles in sanchin dachi, before your Sanchin practice three times a week for two weeks. Note any change in how stiff your Sanchin feels.
2. If you know Tensho, try each of the four ways of practising the two kata together, and note which suits you best on different days.
3. Carry out the Finding Sanchin test on two other kata you know.
4. Ask your teacher when Tensho is taught in your syllabus, and what they look for before introducing it.

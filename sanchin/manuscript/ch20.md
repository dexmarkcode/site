# Faults of the Hips, Trunk and Shoulders

::: chapter-intro
The middle of the body is where the stance meets the arms, and where most leaks of force occur. This chapter catalogues the common faults of the pelvis, the spine, the ribcage, the shoulders and the arms, using the same format as Chapter 19. It pays particular attention to faults that start lower down and show higher up, and to the habit of uneven, one-sided Sanchin that most of us develop without noticing.
:::

If the lower body is where Sanchin stands, the trunk is where it connects. Every force that travels from the floor to the fist, or from a tester's hand into the floor, has to pass through the pelvis, the spine and the shoulder girdle. Each of those is a junction, and each junction is a potential leak.

The faults in this chapter are often harder to see than the faults of the feet and legs. A stance that is too long is visible from across the room. A pelvis that tips forward by a few degrees during the punch, or a shoulder blade that slides an inch up the back, may be invisible to everyone except a teacher with a hand in the right place. That is why testing matters so much for this region, and why the Check step for many of these faults uses one of the tests from Chapter 17.

## Faults of the Pelvis

::: fault
**Fault 20.1 – Pelvis Tilted Forward (Hollow Back)**

*Looks like.* The lower back arches, the bottom pushes back and the belly pushes forward. From the side, the waistband slopes down at the front.

*Check.* The Wall Check (Chapter 5); Test 12, lower back; side-on video at the punch and at the in-breath.

*Causes.* Long-standing postural habit; tight hip flexors from sitting; breathing only into the front of the belly; leaning forward to punch. It often appears only at particular moments, such as the step or the in-breath.

*Correct.* Practise the tuck with the three muscle groups at modest effort (Chapter 5). Practise belt breathing into the back and sides (Drill 7.2). If the hip flexors are tight, stretch them daily: kneel on one knee, tuck the pelvis and shift gently forward until you feel a stretch at the front of the rear hip, thirty seconds each side.

*Confirm.* Repeat the Wall Check and Test 12 during movement, not just standing still.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.2 – Pelvis Over-Tucked**

*Looks like.* The lower back flattens completely, the hips push forward and the upper body leans back. The stance looks rigid and the student often has trouble stepping smoothly.

*Check.* The Wall Check: the hand is squashed hard against the wall. From the side, the shoulders are behind the hips.

*Causes.* Over-correction of a hollow back; clenching the buttocks at maximum; a misunderstanding of "tuck".

*Correct.* Reduce the effort in the buttocks to about a third. Find the middle position with the Wall Check, then the stick test (Drill 6.1): the stick should touch the head, upper back and sacrum with a hand's-width gap at the lower back.

*Confirm.* Stick test; side-on video; stepping should feel freer.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.3 – Hips Twisting**

*Looks like.* The hips rotate with the punch, so that the hip on the punching side moves forward. The front knee may be pulled out of line.

*Check.* Film from above if possible, or from the front, watching the belt knot. It should stay pointing forward during the punch.

*Causes.* Habit from other punches in karate, where hip rotation is used for power; trying to add power to a weak punch.

*Correct.* Practise punches with the hands of a partner resting on both hips, or with a stick held across the front of your hips by a partner, which will tilt if the hips turn. Focus on pressing the rear foot into the floor rather than turning.

*Confirm.* Belt knot stays forward on video; Test 8, punch push-back, should hold as well or better.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.4 – Hips Rising and Falling**

*Looks like.* The height of the hips changes during the kata: rising during steps, at the end of punches or at the turns, and sinking again.

*Check.* Side-on video with a horizontal reference line, such as a strip of tape on the wall behind you at belt height.

*Causes.* Straightening the legs during steps (see Fault 19.15); "driving up" into the punch rather than into the floor; fatigue.

*Correct.* Practise with the tape reference and a mirror. Practise the punch with attention on pressing the rear foot down, not forward or up.

*Confirm.* Video against the reference line.
:::

## Faults of the Spine and Ribcage

::: fault
**Fault 20.5 – Leaning Forward**

*Looks like.* The upper body leans ahead of the hips, especially during punches, spear-hands and the circular block push. The shoulders are in front of the hips.

*Check.* The stick test during a punch; side-on video.

*Causes.* Reaching with the shoulders; a stance that is too long; weight on the front foot; trying to make the technique travel further.

*Correct.* Practise punches with the stick along the spine. Accept that the punch will feel shorter. Check stance length and weight distribution (Faults 19.1 and 19.13).

*Confirm.* Stick test; front and back push tests.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.6 – Leaning Back**

*Looks like.* The upper body is behind the hips. Usually goes with an over-tucked pelvis.

*Check.* Side-on video; the stick test shows the head leaving the stick or the sacrum pressing hard.

*Causes.* Over-tucking (Fault 20.2); trying to avoid leaning forward; bracing against an expected test from the front.

*Correct.* As Fault 20.2. Practise the stack: ear, shoulder and hip on one line (Chapter 6).

*Confirm.* As Fault 20.2.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.7 – Ribs Flaring**

*Looks like.* The lower ribs push forward and up, the upper back arches, and the chest looks puffed out.

*Check.* Place your hands on your lowest ribs. Breathe in. If the ribs move forward and up under your hands, rather than widening sideways, they are flaring.

*Causes.* "Chest out" posture; breathing into the upper chest; a mistaken idea that a big chest looks strong.

*Correct.* The rib settle from Drill 6.2. Practise breathing with your hands on your lower ribs, widening them sideways and back. Lying on your back with knees bent, practise keeping the lower ribs heavy on the floor as you breathe.

*Confirm.* Hands on ribs during the kata's in-breaths.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.8 – Rounded Upper Back**

*Looks like.* The upper back curves forward, the shoulders roll forward and the head is carried in front of the body. The chest looks hollow.

*Check.* The stick test: the stick cannot touch the back of the head and the upper back at the same time without the student straining.

*Causes.* Long-standing posture from desk work and screens; tight chest muscles; weak muscles between the shoulder blades.

*Correct.* The band pull-apart from Drill 13.3; a doorway chest stretch (forearms on the doorframe, step gently through, thirty seconds); the chin glide from Drill 6.2. These take time. Expect improvement over months, not days.

*Confirm.* Stick test every two weeks.
:::

## Faults of the Shoulders

::: fault
**Fault 20.9 – Shoulders Raised**

*Looks like.* The tops of the shoulders rise towards the ears, especially at the end of techniques. The neck looks short.

*Check.* Test 2, shoulder press: the shoulders are pushed down easily. Front-on video: measure the distance from shoulder to earlobe at the start and at the end of the kata.

*Causes.* Tension spilling into the soft zone (Chapter 8); upper-chest breathing; shoulder blades not drawn down; anxiety; a stance that is not providing a stable base.

*Correct.* First check the base (Part Two). Then the shoulder slide from Drill 6.2; the tension dial (Drill 8.1), staying below your spill point; low breathing (Chapter 7).

*Confirm.* Test 2; video comparison.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.10 – Shoulders Rolled Forward**

*Looks like.* The fronts of the shoulders move forward, particularly on the punching side, and the chest narrows.

*Check.* Side-on video at the end of each punch; a partner's hand on the front of the punching shoulder feels it move forward.

*Causes.* Reaching with the punch; weak muscles between the shoulder blades; rounded upper back (Fault 20.8).

*Correct.* Practise punches with a partner's hand resting on the front of the shoulder. Initiate the punch from the rear foot and the back, not the shoulder (Chapter 14).

*Confirm.* Test 8, punch push-back: the shoulder should no longer be pushed back.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.11 – Shoulder Blades Winging**

*Looks like.* The inner edges of the shoulder blades lift away from the ribcage, so that a tester can get their fingers under them.

*Check.* Test 3, shoulder blades. From behind, the edges of the blades stand out.

*Causes.* Weak serratus anterior and lower trapezius muscles, which hold the blades flat; reaching forward with the arms.

*Correct.* Wall slides: stand with your back to a wall, arms in a "W" against the wall, and slide them slowly up and down without the blades lifting. Three sets of ten. Press-up plus: at the top of a press-up, or a press-up against a wall, push the floor or wall slightly further away, spreading the shoulder blades, then return. Three sets of ten.

*Confirm.* Test 3.
:::

## Faults of the Arms

::: fault
**Fault 20.12 – Elbows Flaring**

*Looks like.* The elbows drift away from the body in the block, or swing out during the punch.

*Check.* Tests 5 and 7; front-on video.

*Causes.* Shoulder blades not drawn down; latissimus not engaged; early rotation of the fist in the punch.

*Correct.* Drill 13.1, elbow pivot; Drill 14.1, wall slide; the armpit close from Drill 6.2.

*Confirm.* Tests 5 and 7; video.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 20.13 – Arms Drifting During Movement**

*Looks like.* The block moves, rises, drops or widens during steps and turns.

*Check.* Drill 13.2, the carried block, with a stick across the fists.

*Causes.* The block is held up by the arm muscles rather than carried by the back; the trunk is rotating during the step.

*Correct.* Drill 13.2; the stick-across-forearms exercise from Chapter 11.

*Confirm.* Repeat Drill 13.2; the stick should stay put.
:::

## One-Sidedness

::: fault
**Fault 20.14 – Asymmetry**

*Looks like.* One side of the body consistently performs differently from the other: one shoulder higher, one block wider, one punch weaker, one knee less braced, one turn less stable.

*Check.* Compare left and right versions of every test in the standard round. Compare left and right punch-and-block cycles on video, side by side.

*Causes.* Everyone has a dominant side. Years of using one hand and leg more than the other create differences in strength, flexibility and coordination that show up clearly in a symmetrical kata like Sanchin. Old injuries can add to them.

*Correct.* Identify your weaker side for each element. Give it extra repetitions: for every ten repetitions on the strong side, do fifteen on the weak side. Use the solo tests from Chapter 18 to train the weaker side specifically. Do not try to make the strong side weaker.

*Confirm.* Repeat the left-right comparison monthly.
:::

## Corrections by Hand

Many of the faults in this chapter are traditionally corrected by touch rather than by words, and if you teach, it is worth knowing the common hand corrections. They are all Level 1: no force, only contact and a little guidance.

**For a hollow back,** the teacher places one hand flat on the student's lower back and the other on the lower abdomen, and asks the student to fill both hands with the in-breath while drawing the lower back gently towards the rear hand. The student feels the tuck and the breath together.

**For raised shoulders,** the teacher rests both hands lightly on the tops of the shoulders, without pressing, and asks the student to "move away from my hands". Most students drop their shoulders immediately, and the absence of pressure stops them pushing back.

**For winging or forward shoulders,** the teacher places the fingertips on the lower inside edges of the shoulder blades and asks the student to draw the blades down "into my fingers". The fingertips give the student something specific to move towards.

**For leaning,** the teacher places one hand on the student's sternum and the other between the shoulder blades, and asks the student to feel equal light pressure on both hands. A leaning student immediately feels one hand more than the other.

**For flared elbows,** the teacher rests the backs of their fingers against the outside of the student's elbow and asks the student to bring the elbow "just to touch my fingers, no further".

What these corrections have in common is that they give the student a target to move towards rather than an instruction to obey. Students respond much better to "move into my hand" than to "do not lift your shoulders". The first gives the body something to do; the second gives it something to avoid, and bodies are bad at avoiding things.

You can use the same principle on yourself. Instead of telling yourself not to lean, place a hand on your sternum and one on your back and balance them. Instead of telling yourself not to raise your shoulders, imagine a hand resting on them and move away from it. Positive instructions work better than negative ones, in Sanchin as in most things.

## The Chain Reaction

Faults in the trunk rarely occur alone. More often, one fault sets off a chain reaction. A typical chain looks like this:

The pelvis tips forward on the in-breath (Fault 20.1). The lower back hollows, so the trunk is no longer stacked over the hips. To keep balance, the upper body leans slightly forward (Fault 20.5). To stay upright, the shoulders tense and rise (Fault 20.9). The raised shoulders lift the elbows (Fault 20.12). The block widens and the punch reaches (Fault 20.10). When tested, every one of these gives way, and the student receives five corrections, one for each.

Working on any of the upper links in this chain will produce only temporary improvement, because the cause is still there at the bottom. The student who is told to drop their shoulders will drop them, and they will rise again on the next in-breath because the pelvis is still tipping.

This is why the Cause step of the loop is so important, and why, in this chapter as in the last, the advice is to start at the bottom. When you find several faults in the trunk and shoulders, look first at the pelvis and the breath. Fix those, then re-check the rest.

::: drill
**Drill 20.1 – Trunk Audit**

*What to do:* As for Drill 19.1, work through Faults 20.1 to 20.14, carrying out each Check and recording "yes", "no" or "sometimes". Then look for chains: which faults are likely to be causing the others? Circle the lowest fault in each chain. Those are your starting points.

*Why it matters:* The audit turns a list of corrections into a map of causes.
:::

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Chain Test**

Perform five slow punch-and-block cycles on each side while a partner watches from the side, with a hand resting lightly on your lower back. After each punch, your partner tells you whether your lower back moved away from or into their hand. Then repeat with their hand on the top of your punching shoulder.

If your lower back moved and your shoulder rose on the same punches, you have found a chain. Work on the pelvis first.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The trunk is where force passes between the legs and the arms. Its junctions, the pelvis, the spine and the shoulder girdle, are where leaks occur.
- Pelvis faults include tilting forward, over-tucking, twisting and changing height.
- Spine and ribcage faults include leaning forward, leaning back, flaring the ribs and rounding the upper back.
- Shoulder faults include raising, rolling forward and winging the shoulder blades.
- Arm faults include flaring elbows and drifting blocks.
- Asymmetry affects everyone. Give the weaker side extra work.
- Trunk faults often occur in chains that begin at the pelvis or the breath. Fix the lowest link first.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Carry out Drill 20.1, the trunk audit, and identify your chains.
2. Carry out the Chain Test with a partner.
3. Work on the lowest link of your main chain for two to four weeks, then re-audit.
4. Compare left and right versions of the standard testing round. List the elements in which your weaker side is noticeably weaker.
5. For one month, give your weaker side fifteen repetitions for every ten on your stronger side in all your Sanchin drills.
6. If your upper back is rounded, add the doorway stretch and band pull-apart to your daily routine and check the stick test every two weeks.
:::

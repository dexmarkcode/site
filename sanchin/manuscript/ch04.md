# Sanchin Dachi from the Ground Up

::: chapter-intro
Everything in Sanchin rests on the stance, and the stance rests on the feet. This chapter sets the feet: how far apart, how far forward, at what angle and with what kind of contact. It gives you simple measurements you can mark on the floor, a method for building the stance in four steps, the basic crescent step and a set of checks and corrections for the faults that begin at ground level.
:::

There is an old piece of dojo wisdom that says you can tell a good Sanchin from the doorway, before you can see the student's hands or hear their breath. You look at the feet. If the feet are right, the rest has a chance. If the feet are wrong, nothing above them will be stable, however hard the student works.

That is why we start here. It can feel slow, spending a whole chapter on the position of two feet, when the kata seems to be about so much more. But time spent here is never wasted. In my experience, a large share of the faults that teachers spend years correcting higher up the body, in the knees, the hips and even the shoulders, can be traced back to feet that were set a few centimetres wrong on the first day and never put right.

## What the Stance Has to Do

Before we put the feet anywhere, it helps to be clear about what sanchin dachi is designed for. It has four jobs.

**It must resist pushes from every direction.** In testing, and in application, force comes from the front, from behind and from the sides. A long, deep front stance is strong from the front and back but weak from the side. A wide horse stance is strong from the side but weak from front and back. Sanchin dachi is a compromise: short enough to be strong sideways, offset enough to be strong forwards and backwards.

**It must protect the groin.** The inward rotation of the knees and thighs closes the centre line of the lower body. This is one reason the stance looks the way it does.

**It must allow movement.** Sanchin steps forward, back and around. A stance that is stable only when standing still is no use. The foot positions must allow the body to move without first having to rearrange itself.

**It must let force travel.** The stance is the first link in the chain that carries force from the floor to the fist. It has to be braced in such a way that the push of the rear leg and the grip of the front leg combine rather than cancel out.

Every measurement in this chapter serves one or more of these jobs. When you understand why a foot goes where it does, you will be much less likely to let it drift.

## Measuring the Stance

The measurements below are those most commonly taught for Okinawan Goju-Ryu Sanchin. Your teacher may use slightly different ones, particularly for the angle of the feet, and if so you should follow them. The principles will not change.

### Width

Measured side to side, the feet are roughly shoulder-width apart. More precisely, if you draw a line forward from the centre of your rear heel and another from the centre of your front heel, the two lines should be about as far apart as the outsides of your shoulders. For most adults, that is somewhere between thirty and forty-five centimetres.

A stance that is too narrow, with the feet almost on a single line, is unstable from the side. A stance that is too wide turns into something closer to a horse stance and loses the inward grip of the knees.

### Length

Measured front to back, the heel of the front foot is roughly level with the toes of the rear foot. Put another way, the front foot is about one foot's length ahead of the rear foot. Some teachers place the front heel slightly behind the rear toes, giving a shorter stance; others slightly ahead. The general rule is that the stance should be short enough that you could, if you wished, step either foot forward without first shifting your weight.

### Angle of the front foot

The front foot turns inwards, towards the centre line. The angle varies between lineages and teachers, but most commonly lies between about twenty and thirty degrees. A useful check is that the big toe of the front foot should point roughly towards the space in front of the rear foot, not across in front of it.

### Angle of the rear foot

The rear foot points straight ahead or turns very slightly inwards, up to about ten or fifteen degrees. It should never turn outwards. An outward-turned rear foot lets the rear knee fall out and breaks the inward spiral of the leg.

**Teacher's Note**

When students first see the turned-in front foot, many of them worry that it will hurt their knees. It should not, as long as the knee follows the foot. The problem arises when the foot turns in but the knee stays pointing forward or, worse, drifts outward, so that the knee is twisted between a foot going one way and a thigh going another. Chapter 5 deals with this in detail. For now, a simple rule: wherever the toes point, the knee goes roughly over them, never outside them.

## The Contact of the Foot

Where the feet are placed is only half the story. The other half is how they meet the floor.

### Three points of contact

A healthy foot bears weight on a tripod: the centre of the heel, the ball of the big toe and the ball of the little toe. In Sanchin, you want weight spread evenly across all three points on both feet. When this happens, the arch of the foot lifts very slightly, the ankle sits straight above the heel, and the foot feels alive and springy rather than flat and heavy.

The commonest fault is weight that has drifted to one of the three points at the expense of the others. If it has drifted to the inside of the foot, the arch collapses and the knee tends to fall inwards too far. If it has drifted to the outside edge, the ankle rolls and the knee drifts out. If it is all on the heels, the body sits back; all on the balls of the feet, and it tips forward. Each of these weakens the stance in a particular direction, and a teacher who pushes you will find the weakness very quickly.

### Gripping

In Sanchin, the feet do not simply rest on the floor. They grip it. But the grip is easy to get wrong.

Many students grip by curling their toes, as if trying to pick up a pencil. This lifts the balls of the feet off the floor and reduces the contact area, and it tends to make the calves cramp. Instead, keep the toes long and press their pads gently into the floor, while also pressing down through the heel and the balls of the feet. You should feel the whole sole engage, as though you were trying to draw the floor slightly together beneath you, without the toes leaving the ground or bunching up.

Older teachers sometimes describe this as "grasping the floor" or "rooting like a tree". Another useful image is of standing on a towel and trying to pull it towards the centre from both feet without letting the feet slide.

![**Figure 4.2** The foot tripod. Weight is shared between the heel, the ball of the big toe and the ball of the little toe (black circles). The toes press down without curling, and the arch lifts slightly (arrow). The shaded areas show where weight should not be concentrated.](figures/fig4_2.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Coin Test**

Stand barefoot in your Sanchin stance. Ask a partner to try to slide a thin coin under each of the three contact points of each foot: the heel, the ball of the big toe and the ball of the little toe. If the coin slides under any of them easily, your weight is not reaching that point. Adjust and test again. Then ask your partner to try sliding the coin under the pads of your toes. If it slides in easily, your toes are curling.
:::

## Building the Stance in Four Steps

Most students learn sanchin dachi by stepping into it from a natural stance, copying what they see. That works, but it tends to produce a stance that varies from one day to the next. A more reliable method is to build it in four steps, checking each before moving on. Practise it this way for a few weeks and the position will settle into your body.

**Step 1: Parallel stance.** Stand with your feet parallel and shoulder-width apart, measured from the outsides of the feet to roughly the outsides of the shoulders. Knees soft, weight even across both feet.

**Step 2: Slide forward.** Slide your right foot straight forward until its heel is level with the toes of your left foot. Keep the width the same. The right foot should still point straight ahead at this stage.

**Step 3: Turn the front foot in.** Keeping your right heel in place, pivot on it to turn the right toes inwards by twenty to thirty degrees. Turn the left toes in very slightly, or leave them straight, as your teacher prefers.

**Step 4: Grip and set.** Bend both knees slightly and draw them gently inwards and forwards, so that each kneecap sits roughly over the second or third toe of its own foot. Spread your weight across the three points of each foot. Press the toes down without curling. Tuck your hips under slightly. You are now standing in a basic sanchin dachi.

The dashed outline shows where each foot has come from.](figures/fig4_3.png)

::: drill
**Drill 4.1 – The Floor Template**

*You need:* masking tape or chalk, a tape measure and a protractor or a phone with an angle app.

*What to do:* Build your stance using the four steps above, with the help of a partner or a mirror. When you are satisfied, ask your partner to mark the outline of both feet on the floor with tape, along with a line through the centre of each heel and the line of the front heel across to the rear toes. Measure the width, the length and the two foot angles and write them in your training notebook.

*Practice:* For two weeks, every time you practise, step into your stance on the template first, then step off it, close your eyes and try to step back into exactly the same position. Open your eyes and check. Repeat ten times.

*What to look for:* Most people find they consistently miss in one direction: always too long, or always with the front foot turned too little. That consistent miss is your personal fault. Write it down.

*Why it matters:* A template turns an approximate idea into a precise one. Once your body knows the exact position, you will be able to find it without the tape.
:::

## Weight Distribution

In sanchin dachi, the weight is shared roughly equally between the two feet, perhaps with a very slight bias towards the rear foot in some lineages. It is not a front-loaded stance like the forward stance of many other karate styles, and it is not a back stance either.

Even weight matters because the stance has to resist pushes from all directions and has to be ready to step either way. If the weight is mostly on the front foot, a push from the front will rock you back onto the rear foot before the stance can resist it, and stepping forward again requires an extra shift. If it is mostly on the rear foot, the reverse is true.

A simple way to check is to stand with each foot on a separate bathroom scale. The two readings should be within a few kilograms of each other. Without scales, you can rock very slightly forward and back until you feel the point where neither foot is doing more work than the other, and settle there.

::: fault
**Problem: "My front foot keeps sliding outwards during the kata."**

*Check.* Film yourself from above if you can, or ask a partner to watch only your front foot through three or four steps. Note whether the foot is placed in the wrong position when you step, or placed correctly and then drifts.

*Cause.* If it is placed wrongly, the problem is in the step: you are probably bringing the moving foot forward in a straight line rather than in the crescent path described below. If it drifts afterwards, you are probably not gripping with the front foot, or your front knee is pushing outwards and dragging the foot with it.

*Correct.* For a placement problem, practise the crescent step slowly on the floor template from Drill 4.1, twenty steps a day. For a drift problem, practise standing in the stance and pressing the front knee gently inwards against a partner's hand while keeping the foot still, for three sets of thirty seconds.

*Confirm.* Film the same three or four steps again and compare.
:::

## Stepping in Sanchin Dachi

A full account of stepping in the kata comes in Part Four, where each step has its own frames. But because the stance must allow movement, and because many foot faults come from the step rather than the standing position, it makes sense to introduce the basic step here.

Sanchin uses a crescent step, sometimes called a half-moon step. The moving foot does not travel in a straight line from its old position to its new one. Instead, it travels in an inward curve: first drawing in towards the standing foot, passing close beside it, then curving out again to its new position. Seen from above, its path looks like a narrow crescent.

The step works like this, moving from right sanchin dachi, with the right foot forward, into left sanchin dachi:

1. Keep the right foot gripping and the knees drawn in. Keep your hips at the same height.
2. Slide the left foot forward and inward along the floor, the sole just brushing the surface, so that it passes close beside the right foot. At this point the two feet are side by side, a few centimetres apart.
3. Continue the movement, curving the left foot out to its new front position, one foot's length ahead of the right toes and shoulder-width to the side.
4. As the left foot arrives, turn its toes in to the correct angle, set its three points of contact and draw the left knee inwards. The right foot, now the rear foot, adjusts its angle if necessary.

![**Figure 4.4** The crescent step seen from above, moving from right-foot-forward to left-foot-forward. The moving foot draws in towards the standing foot, passes close beside it, then curves out to its new position. The dashed outlines show the starting and passing positions.](figures/fig4_4.png)

The crescent step has two purposes. In application, it protects the groin as the legs pass each other, because the knees stay close together. And in training, it forces you to keep the inward tension of the legs throughout the step, rather than letting the knees fall apart and then trying to squeeze them back together when you arrive.

Throughout the step, the hips should stay level, neither bobbing up and down nor tilting sideways. The upper body should not lean. Imagine a book balanced on your head: it should neither rise nor fall nor slide off as you step.

::: fault
**Problem: "When I step, my head bobs up and down."**

*Cause.* You are straightening the standing leg as the moving foot passes it, then bending again as it lands. This usually happens because the standing leg is not strong enough to hold the bent position on its own for the moment of the step, or because you are lifting the moving foot instead of sliding it.

*Correct.* Practise the step beside a wall with a strip of tape at the height of the top of your head. Watch in a mirror, or film, and keep the top of your head level with the tape throughout. Slide the foot; do not lift it. If the standing leg is tiring, see the leg strength work in Chapter 22.
:::

## The Feet in Profile

So far we have looked at the feet from above. It is also worth looking at them from the side, because a few common faults only show from that angle.

**The heel should stay down.** In particular, the rear heel must stay on the floor throughout the kata. A lifted rear heel shortens the leg, shifts the weight forward and makes the stance easy to push from the front. It often lifts when students lean forward to punch or when they bend the rear knee too much.

**The ankle should be stacked.** Viewed from the side, the shin of each leg leans slightly forward, because the knee is bent. Viewed from the front or back, the ankle should sit straight above the heel, neither rolled in nor out.

**The toes should be flat.** A side view shows quickly whether the toes are curling. If you can see the tops of the toe joints lifting into little hills, the grip has turned into a clench.

## Common Faults of the Feet

We will return to these in detail in Chapter 19, but here, briefly, are the faults that most often begin at ground level, with the quickest corrections.

| Fault | What it does | Quick correction |
|---|---|---|
| Stance too long | Weakens the stance from the side; makes stepping slow | Shorten until front heel is level with rear toes |
| Stance too wide | Turns into a horse stance; knees lose inward grip | Narrow to shoulder width |
| Stance too narrow | Unstable from the side | Widen to shoulder width |
| Front foot not turned in | Front knee falls out; groin open | Pivot on front heel to 20–30° |
| Rear foot turned out | Rear knee falls out; rear leg cannot drive | Turn rear toes forward or slightly in |
| Toes curling | Balls of feet lift; calves cramp | Press toe pads down, keep toes long |
| Weight on outer edges | Ankles roll; knees drift out | Press through ball of big toe |
| Rear heel lifting | Weight tips forward; easy to push over | Press rear heel down; check rear knee bend |

## Strengthening the Feet

Many people today have weak feet. We wear cushioned shoes for most of our waking hours and walk on flat, even surfaces, and the small muscles that lift the arch and control the toes are rarely asked to do much. When those people step into sanchin dachi and are told to grip the floor, their feet simply do not know how.

The good news is that foot strength responds quickly to training. A few minutes a day, for a few weeks, makes a noticeable difference. The following simple exercises are a good starting point. Part Eight places them in a full programme.

::: drill
**Drill 4.2 – Foot Wake-Up**

*You need:* a towel and a smooth floor. Do this barefoot.

1. **Towel gather.** Sit on a chair with a towel spread flat on the floor in front of you. Place one foot on the near edge. Using only the toes, without lifting the heel, draw the towel towards you in small bunches. Do this until the whole towel is gathered under your foot, then switch feet. Two rounds each foot.
2. **Short foot.** Standing, try to shorten your foot by drawing the ball of the big toe towards the heel without curling the toes. The arch should lift. Hold for five seconds, release, repeat ten times on each foot.
3. **Toe splay.** Standing, lift all ten toes, spread them as wide as you can, then place them back down one at a time, little toe first. Repeat ten times.
4. **Three-point rock.** Standing on one foot, slowly shift your weight in a circle around the tripod: heel, little toe ball, big toe ball, back to heel. Five circles each way on each foot.

*Why it matters:* These exercises wake up the muscles you need to grip the floor in sanchin dachi without curling the toes. After two or three weeks, most students notice their stance feels noticeably more solid.
:::

## Footwear and Floors

Sanchin is traditionally practised barefoot on a wooden floor, and that is the ideal. Bare feet give you the best contact and the best feedback, and a wooden floor has just enough give to be comfortable while still being firm.

If you must practise in shoes, choose ones with thin, flexible soles and a wide toe box, so that your toes can spread. Avoid thick cushioned trainers, which make it very hard to feel the tripod. If you practise on a carpet, be aware that it hides foot faults: the pile fills in the gaps under a curled toe or a lifted arch, so the stance can feel better than it is. Check your stance on a hard floor from time to time.

Mats used for grappling are usually too soft for good Sanchin practice. If your dojo trains on mats, practise the stance at home on a firm surface as well.

### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin dachi must resist pushes from all directions, protect the groin, allow movement in any direction and let force travel from the floor to the fist.
- The feet are about shoulder-width apart; the front heel is roughly level with the rear toes; the front foot turns in by about 20–30°; the rear foot points forward or turns in slightly. Follow your teacher's exact measurements if they differ.
- Weight is spread evenly across three points of each foot: heel, ball of the big toe, ball of the little toe. The toes press down without curling.
- Build the stance in four steps: parallel, slide forward, turn the front foot in, grip and set.
- Weight is shared roughly equally between the feet.
- The crescent step draws the moving foot in past the standing foot and out to its new position, keeping the knees together and the head level.
- The rear heel stays down; the ankles stack above the heels; the toes stay flat.
- Weak feet are common and respond quickly to simple daily exercises.

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Complete Drill 4.1 and record your stance measurements. Note your consistent miss.
2. Carry out the Coin Test with a partner. Record which contact points, if any, the coin slid under.
3. Practise Drill 4.2 every day for two weeks. At the end, repeat the Coin Test and compare.
4. Practise twenty slow crescent steps a day for a week, alternating feet, using the head-height tape check from the problem box. Film the last session from the side.
5. Look at your baseline recording from Chapter 1. Using Table 4.1, identify any foot faults you can see. Write them down in order of how obvious they are.
6. If you can, stand on two bathroom scales in your stance and note the reading on each. Adjust until they are within a few kilograms of each other and notice how that feels.

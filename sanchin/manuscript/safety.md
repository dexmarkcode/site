# Before You Begin

Please read this short section before you try anything in the book. It will take five minutes, and it may save you a good deal more than that.

## Who should take extra care

Sanchin combines three things that each place a demand on the body: a held, slightly crouched stance; strong, sustained muscular tension; and forceful breathing, often with the throat partly closed. Together these raise blood pressure and the pressure inside the chest and abdomen, sometimes sharply.

For most healthy adults this is no more risky than lifting a heavy shopping bag or pushing a stalled car. For some people, though, it calls for medical advice first. Speak to your doctor before practising the breathing and tension methods in this book if any of the following apply to you:

- high blood pressure, whether treated or not;
- any heart condition, including irregular heartbeat, or a history of heart attack;
- a history of stroke, aneurysm or mini-stroke;
- glaucoma or a detached retina;
- a hernia of any kind, or recent abdominal, chest or eye surgery;
- pregnancy, or having given birth in the past few months;
- pelvic floor problems;
- dizziness or fainting during exercise.

If any of these apply, the practice is not necessarily closed to you. Many people in these groups practise a soft version of Sanchin, with natural breathing and moderate tension, for years. But the decision about how hard to work should be made with someone who knows your health, and you should tell your instructor.

## The three stop signs

Whoever you are, stop at once and rest if you feel any of the following during practice:

1. **Dizziness, light-headedness or greying of your vision.** This is usually a sign that you have held your breath or bore down too hard. Sit or kneel, breathe normally, and do not stand up quickly.
2. **A pounding headache that comes on during effort.** This can be a sign of a sharp rise in blood pressure. Stop the session and seek advice if it happens more than once.
3. **Sharp pain in a joint**, especially the knee, lower back or shoulder. Muscular burning is expected. Joint pain is not.

## Testing with a partner

Part Six of this book describes *shime*, the practice of checking a student's Sanchin by pressing, pushing and sometimes striking. Partner testing should only be done under the supervision of a qualified instructor, and both partners should understand the rules set out in Chapter 16. Never strike the head, throat, spine, kidneys or groin. Never test a person who has not agreed to be tested. Start lighter than you think you need to.

## Children and young people

Children can learn the shape of Sanchin and benefit from its posture work, but they should breathe naturally and use only light tension. Forceful breathing, heavy tension and striking-based testing are not suitable for children. Chapter 27 has more on teaching the form to different age groups.

## A note on this book's version of the kata

Sanchin is practised differently in different lineages. The version described here is presented as a teaching model. Where your teacher's instruction differs from this book, follow your teacher.

# Rhythm, Timing and Slowness

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is slow, and its slowness is deliberate. This chapter explains what slowness trains, how to set a tempo for the kata and keep to it, how to align the three timelines of breath, movement and tension, and how to move through the transitions between techniques without the stop-start jerkiness that spoils so many performances. It finishes with practical tools for training rhythm: counting, metronomes and video timing.
:::

Watch a room full of students performing Sanchin together and you will see a dozen different rhythms. One student rushes every step and then stands frozen waiting for the others. Another moves smoothly but breathes in bursts. A third punches in two jerky stages, as though the arm were on a ratchet. A fourth is so slow that by the end of the kata they are three movements behind everyone else and visibly exhausted.

Each of them has learned the movements. What none of them has yet learned is the rhythm, and in Sanchin rhythm is not decoration. It is the means by which breath, movement and tension are bound together. A student with the right rhythm finds that the other elements tend to fall into place. A student with the wrong rhythm finds that every correction is undone the moment they start to move.

## Why Sanchin Is Slow

Students sometimes ask why Sanchin is performed so slowly when karate, in the end, is about speed. There are several good answers.

**Slowness reveals.** At speed, the body can get away with a great deal. Momentum carries a punch past a weak point in the trunk; a quick step hides a wobble in the knee. When the same movements are performed slowly, there is nowhere to hide. Every weak link shows. This is why Sanchin is such a good diagnostic tool, and why it can be tested by hand.

**Slowness builds strength.** A slow movement under tension keeps the muscles working for longer than a fast one. Sports scientists call this time under tension, and it is one of the main drivers of strength and muscular endurance. The deliberate pace of Sanchin is part of what makes it such effective conditioning.

**Slowness teaches the body.** When we learn a new movement pattern, we learn it best when we can attend to it closely, and we can attend most closely when it is slow. A slow, correct repetition teaches the body more than a fast, sloppy one. Many skills, from playing a musical instrument to learning a golf swing, are first practised slowly for exactly this reason.

**Slowness trains the mind.** Moving slowly under tension, breathing deliberately, is uncomfortable. It requires patience and concentration. The urge to hurry is a form of the inner battle described in Chapter 3, and learning to resist it is part of the training.

None of this means that Sanchin has nothing to do with speed. The structure it builds is exactly what makes fast techniques powerful. But the structure has to be built slowly before it can be used quickly.

## Setting the Tempo

How slow is slow? There is no single correct speed for Sanchin, and different lineages and teachers vary considerably. As a rough guide, a full performance of the version described in this book typically takes somewhere between two and a half and four minutes. Individual movements are paced by the breath: an in-breath of two to four seconds and an out-breath of four to seven seconds is a common range for each punch.

What matters more than the absolute speed is consistency. Each punch should take roughly the same time as every other punch. Each step should take roughly the same time as every other step. The kata should have a steady, even pulse, like a slow heartbeat, with no sudden accelerations and no long pauses.

A good starting tempo for most students is the one in Table 9.1. Use it as a reference while you learn the kata, then adjust it to suit your teacher's instruction and your own breathing capacity.

| Element | In-breath | Out-breath | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening step and double block | 3 s | 5 s | Step during in-breath, block on out-breath |
| Pulling hand to hip | 3 s | — | In-breath only |
| Punch | — | 5 s | Out-breath ends as punch tightens |
| Return to block | 2 s | 2 s | Shorter exchange |
| Step forward | 3 s | — | Step during in-breath |
| Turn | 3 s | 3 s | Slower than a step; see Chapter 12 |
| Double spear-hand | 3 s | 5 s | Hands draw back on in-breath |
| Circular block | 4 s | 5 s | Longer, more continuous |

## Three Timelines

At any moment in Sanchin, three things are happening: you are breathing, you are moving and you are changing your level of tension. Each of these has its own timeline, and the rhythm of the kata depends on how well the three line up.

**The breath** alternates between in and out, with brief transitions between.

**The movement** alternates between preparation (drawing back, stepping) and execution (punching, blocking), with moments of stillness at the end of each technique.

**The tension** rises from the baseline to a peak and eases back again, in a wave.

In a well-timed Sanchin, these three timelines are locked together. The in-breath coincides with the preparation and with the easing of tension. The out-breath coincides with the execution and with the build-up of tension. All three reach their turning points together: the end of the out-breath, the end of the punch and the peak of tension arrive at the same instant.

When they drift apart, problems follow. If the tension peaks before the punch is finished, the end of the punch is stiff and slow. If the breath ends before the punch, the final stage of the technique has no breath to support it. If the movement finishes before the tension has built, the technique arrives soft and then stiffens afterwards, a common fault that teachers sometimes describe as "tensing after the event".

::: drill
**Drill 9.1 – Three-Line Check**

*You need:* a phone that can record video with sound, and a quiet room.

*What to do:* Record yourself from the side performing four punch-and-block cycles in a fixed stance. Play the recording back three times. The first time, watch only the arm, and note the moment each punch arrives. The second time, listen only to the breath, and note the moment each out-breath ends. The third time, watch your back and trunk, and note the moment the tension visibly peaks: the back spreads, the stance sets.

*What to look for:* All three moments should coincide. If they do not, note which one is early and which is late.

*Correct:* If the breath ends early, slow the out-breath and start it less forcefully. If the tension peaks late, practise the slow-motion sequence from Drill 8.2. If the punch arrives early, slow the arm and let the breath lead.

*Why it matters:* Most students cannot feel timing errors while they perform. Seeing and hearing them on video makes them obvious.
:::

## Continuous, Not Stop-Start

The most common rhythm fault in Sanchin is what I call stop-start movement. The student punches, stops dead, waits, draws the hand back, stops dead, waits, steps, stops dead. The kata becomes a series of separate poses joined by bursts of movement, and it looks mechanical, like a robot or a figure in a flick-book.

Sanchin is slow, but it should be continuous. Each movement flows into the next. At the end of a technique there is a moment where the movement settles and the tension peaks, but it is a moment, not a freeze, and the next movement begins out of it rather than after it.

A good way to think about this is in terms of speed. In stop-start movement, the speed of the hand drops to zero between every technique and then jumps up again. In continuous movement, the speed slows smoothly to almost nothing at the end of each technique and then rises smoothly again into the next. The difference is like the difference between driving with sudden braking and acceleration and driving with smooth, anticipated changes of pace.

::: fault
**Problem: "My teacher says my Sanchin looks robotic."**

*Check.* Film your kata and watch it at double speed. Robotic movement becomes very obvious when sped up: the body jerks from pose to pose.

*Cause.* Usually one or more of these: thinking of the kata as a sequence of positions rather than movements; holding the breath at the end of each technique, which freezes the body; or waiting at the end of each technique for the next instruction from your own memory.

*Correct.* Practise Drill 9.2 below to develop flow. Make sure the in-breath starts as soon as the out-breath ends, without a held pause. Make sure you know the kata well enough that you do not need to stop and think; if you do, practise the sequence at a walking pace with no tension until it is automatic, then return to the slow version.

*Confirm.* Film again, watch at double speed and compare.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 9.2 – Water in the Glass**

*What to do:* Imagine you are holding a glass filled to the brim with water in each hand, and that you must perform the kata without spilling a drop. Water spills when you start or stop suddenly. Perform one complete kata at Stage 2 breathing and light tension, focusing entirely on starting and stopping each movement smoothly.

*Progression:* When you can perform the whole kata "without spilling", gradually return to your normal breathing and tension, keeping the same smoothness.

*Why it matters:* This simple image does more than any technical instruction to cure stop-start movement. It shifts your attention from where the hands are to how they get there.
:::

## The Transitions

Most rhythm problems occur not during techniques but between them, in the transitions. These are the moments where one movement ends and the next begins, and where the student must manage a change of breath, a change of direction and a change of tension all at once.

The main transitions in Sanchin are these:

**From punch to block.** After the punch reaches its peak, the arm returns to the block position. The tension eases slightly, the breath changes, and the arm travels back along a path that keeps the elbow down and the shoulder connected. The common fault is to let the arm drop or swing back loosely, losing the connection, and then to re-establish it at the block. The arm should return under control, with the back still engaged.

**From block to step.** After the block is settled, the student steps forward. The breath comes in, the rear foot begins its crescent, and the arms hold their position. The common fault is to let the arms or the upper body move with the step, so that the block wobbles or the shoulders rise. The upper body should travel forward as a unit on top of the legs, as though carried on a trolley.

**From step to next technique.** As the stepping foot settles and the new stance sets, the next technique begins. The common fault is to start the technique before the stance has set, so that the punch arrives on an unstable base. Wait until the foot has gripped and the knees have braced. It takes a fraction of a second, but it matters.

**Turns.** The turns are the most demanding transitions in the kata and have their own section in Chapter 12. Students almost always rush them.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

A simple way to improve your transitions is to practise them on their own, out of context. Take the last half-second of one movement and the first half-second of the next, and repeat that join ten times. Then take the next join. It is a little like a musician practising the change between two chords rather than playing the whole piece. The joins are where most of the mistakes live.
:::

## Counting and Metronomes

Many students find rhythm easier to learn if they count. Counting gives the mind something to hold on to and makes timing errors obvious. There are two ways to count in Sanchin, and both are useful.

**Counting the breath.** Count silently during each in-breath and out-breath: "in, two, three; out, two, three, four, five". This sets the tempo of the breath, and because the movement is tied to the breath, it sets the tempo of the movement too.

**Counting with a metronome.** A metronome, or a metronome app on your phone, set to sixty beats a minute, gives you a steady one-second pulse. Breathe and move to it: three beats in, five beats out, and so on. This removes any tendency to speed up or slow down without noticing. It is particularly useful for students who rush, because the metronome refuses to hurry.

Counting is a learning tool, not a permanent fixture. Once the rhythm is established, which usually takes a few weeks of regular practice, stop counting and let the breath set the pace on its own. Check with the metronome from time to time to make sure the tempo has not drifted.

::: drill
**Drill 9.3 – Metronome Kata**

*You need:* a metronome or metronome app set to 60 beats per minute, played quietly.

*What to do:* Perform the whole kata to the metronome, using the timings in Table 9.1. Count the beats silently. Every in-breath and out-breath should start on a beat.

*What to look for:* Where do you lose the beat? Most students find they speed up during the steps and turns and slow down during the punches. Note the places where you lose the beat in your training notebook.

*Progression:* Perform the metronome kata three times a week for two weeks. Then perform the kata without the metronome, timing the whole performance with a stopwatch. Compare the total time with the metronome version. If it is much shorter, you are rushing without the metronome; if much longer, you are dragging.

*Why it matters:* A metronome gives you an objective, unarguable reference for tempo, which is much more reliable than your own sense of time under effort.
:::

## Rushing and Dragging

The two great enemies of rhythm are rushing and dragging.

**Rushing** is far more common. It happens because Sanchin is uncomfortable, and the mind wants to get through the discomfort as quickly as possible. It also happens when a student is nervous, for example in a grading or when being tested. The usual signs are steps that are faster than the punches, turns that are hurried, out-breaths that are short and forceful, and a total kata time that is well under two minutes.

The cure for rushing is to slow down deliberately, with external help: a metronome, a partner counting aloud or the breathing count. It also helps to recognise that rushing is a form of the inner battle. When you notice the urge to hurry, treat it as a signal to slow down and breathe.

**Dragging** is less common but does happen, particularly in students who have been told repeatedly to slow down, or who are trying to look impressive. The signs are long pauses between techniques, out-breaths that go on until the student is visibly short of air, and a kata that leaves them exhausted. Dragging also tends to make the movements lose their intent: the punch becomes a slow extension of the arm rather than a strike performed slowly.

The cure for dragging is to keep the breath comfortable and the movement purposeful. Each punch should feel like a real punch, performed slowly, not like a stretching exercise. If you are running out of breath, your tempo is too slow for your current capacity.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Stopwatch Test**

Time three consecutive performances of your Sanchin with a stopwatch, with a minute's rest between each. Write down the three times.

- If the times differ by more than about fifteen seconds, your tempo is inconsistent. Use the metronome drill.
- If the times get shorter each time, you are rushing as you tire. Work on endurance and on deliberate slowness late in the kata.
- If the times get longer, you are dragging as you tire. Check that your breath is comfortable.
- If all three are well under two minutes, you are probably rushing throughout.
:::

## Rhythm Within the Kata

So far we have spoken as though Sanchin has a single, even rhythm throughout. In broad terms it does, but within that steady pulse there is some variation, and it is worth being aware of it.

The opening of the kata, from the bow to the first double block, often has a slightly more measured, ceremonial quality. The advance, with its repeated step, punch and block, has the most regular rhythm of all. The turns slow down noticeably, because the body has to change direction without losing the stance. The closing section, with the double spear-hand and the circular block, has a different character: longer, more flowing movements with fuller breaths. And the final return to the starting position slows again, as the kata settles.

Some lineages also include moments of greater speed or sharper breath within the kata, particularly in the closing techniques. If your teacher includes these, they are deliberate contrasts and should be practised as such.

## Rhythm as Expression

Finally, a word about rhythm in a more personal sense. As your Sanchin matures, you will find that its rhythm becomes your own. Two experienced practitioners performing the same version of the kata, both technically excellent, will rarely look quite the same. One may have a slightly longer breath, another a slightly sharper finish to each technique. These differences reflect body, temperament and years of practice.

That is as it should be. The aim of rhythm training is not to make everyone identical but to give you control. A student who can perform the kata to a metronome, consistently, with breath, movement and tension locked together, has the control to choose their rhythm. A student who cannot is simply at the mercy of their nerves, their fatigue and their habits.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin is slow because slowness reveals faults, builds strength through time under tension, teaches the body correct patterns and trains patience.
- A full performance typically takes two and a half to four minutes. Consistency of tempo matters more than absolute speed.
- Breath, movement and tension are three timelines that should turn together: the end of the out-breath, the arrival of the technique and the peak of tension coincide.
- Sanchin is slow but continuous. Avoid stop-start movement; let each movement flow from the last.
- Most rhythm problems occur in the transitions: punch to block, block to step, step to technique, and the turns. Practise them in isolation.
- Counting and metronomes are useful learning tools. Remove them once the rhythm is established, and check back with them occasionally.
- Rushing is the commonest rhythm fault and is a form of the inner battle. Dragging is less common but drains the kata of intent.
:::

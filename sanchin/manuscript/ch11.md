# The Advance and the First Turn: Frames 11 to 24

::: chapter-intro
This chapter takes you forward along the floor pattern with two stepping punch-and-block cycles, then through the first turn to face the rear, a cycle in the new direction and a step towards the starting point. The stepping cycle is the heart of Sanchin, and the turn is its hardest transition. Both are broken down frame by frame, with the checkpoints and errors that matter most.
:::

In Chapter 10 you performed the first punch-and-block cycle standing still. Now you move. The cycle stays the same, but each time it is preceded by a step, and the step brings new problems: keeping the head level, keeping the arms quiet, arriving in a correct stance and starting the next technique only when that stance has set.

Then comes the turn. Almost every student finds the turns the hardest part of Sanchin. The body has to change direction by half a circle without losing the stance, the tension or the breath, and it has to do so slowly enough that every weakness shows. Teachers watch the turns closely. If your Sanchin holds together through the turns, it will hold together almost anywhere.

## The Stepping Cycle

Before the individual frames, a word on the pattern. Each stepping cycle has five parts:

1. **Step.** The rear foot travels forward in a crescent to become the new front foot. The arms hold the double block. Breathe in.
2. **Set.** The new stance settles: foot grips, knees brace, pelvis tucks. A short out-breath or simply the end of the in-breath, depending on your teacher.
3. **Pull.** The hand on the side of the new rear foot draws back to the hip. Breathe in.
4. **Punch.** That hand punches to the centre line. Breathe out.
5. **Return.** The punching arm returns to the block. A short exchange of breath.

The step is always taken with the rear foot, so the lead foot alternates: right, left, right. The punching hand is always on the same side as the rear foot, so it alternates too: left, right, left.

## Frames 11 to 15: First Stepping Cycle

You begin these frames in right sanchin dachi, right foot forward, with both arms in the double block, as at the end of Frame 10.

![**Figure 11.1** Frames 11 to 15. The left foot steps forward in a crescent into left sanchin dachi while the arms hold the block; the stance sets; the right fist draws to the hip; the right fist punches; the right arm returns to the block.](figures/fig11_1.png)

### Frame 11 – Step forward (left foot)

**Action.** Keeping the right foot gripping and both arms in the block, slide the left foot forward in a crescent: first in towards the right foot, passing close beside it, then out and forward to its new position, one foot's length ahead of the right toes and shoulder-width to the left. The toes turn in as the foot arrives. The right foot, now the rear foot, adjusts its angle slightly if needed.

**Breath.** In, through the nose, for about three seconds.

**Checkpoints.** The head stays level: no rise as the feet pass. The upper body travels forward as one unit, carried by the legs. The arms do not swing, drop or drift; the blocks stay exactly where they were. The knees stay close as the feet pass each other.

**Common errors.** Rising in the middle of the step; leaning forward to "reach" with the step; letting the arms float up or the elbows flare; stepping in a straight line. See Chapter 4 for the crescent step and the head-bobbing problem.

### Frame 12 – Set

**Action.** As the left foot lands, set the new stance: grip with both feet, brace the knees inwards, tuck the pelvis, settle the shoulders. This takes a fraction of a second, but it must happen before the next technique begins.

**Breath.** End of the in-breath. Some teachers ask for a short out-breath here to settle the body; others carry the in-breath straight into the pulling hand. Follow your teacher.

**Checkpoints.** The new stance is complete: correct width and length, front toes in, three points of contact, knees over toes. The arms are still symmetrical in the block.

**Common errors.** Starting the pulling hand before the stance has set, so that the technique arrives on an unstable base. This is one of the commonest faults in the advance. If you are unsure, pause for a heartbeat.

### Frame 13 – Pulling hand (right)

**Action.** Draw the right fist back to the right hip, palm up, elbow pointing straight back, exactly as the left hand did in Frame 7. The left arm holds the block.

**Breath.** In, through the nose.

**Checkpoints.** As Frame 7, mirrored: the right elbow draws straight back close to the body, the right shoulder stays down, the left block does not move, the trunk does not twist.

**Common errors.** As Frame 7. Note that most people have a dominant side, and faults that are absent on one side often appear on the other. Watch especially for the shoulder rolling forward on your weaker side.

### Frame 14 – Punch (right)

**Action.** Drive the right fist forward to the centre line at solar-plexus height, rotating it during the final third of the movement so that it arrives palm down. The left arm holds the block.

**Breath.** Out, steady and audible, ending as the punch arrives.

**Checkpoints.** As Frame 8, mirrored. In addition, check that the rear leg, now the right leg, is driving the punch: you should feel pressure into the floor through the right foot as the punch arrives, without the heel lifting.

**Common errors.** Rear heel lifting; trunk twisting with the punch; punch crossing the centre line.

### Frame 15 – Return to block

**Action.** Bring the right arm back into the block position, matching the left. Settle.

**Breath.** A short exchange, then the end of the out-breath and a brief pause.

**Checkpoints.** As Frame 9: arm returns under control, elbow down and in, blocks symmetrical.

**Common errors.** As Frame 9.

::: fault
**Problem: "My arms drift during the step."**

*Check.* Film Frames 10 to 12 from the front. Pause at the start, middle and end of the step and compare the positions of your fists and elbows.

*Cause.* Usually one of three things: the arms are being held by the shoulders rather than by the back, so they move whenever the trunk moves; the student is unconsciously using the arms for balance; or the upper body is rotating slightly with the step.

*Correct.* Practise the step with a light stick, such as a length of dowel, resting across both forearms in the block position. The stick will roll or slide if the arms move unevenly. Ten steps forward and back, slowly. If the trunk is rotating, practise the step with your hands on your hips, watching in a mirror that your belt knot stays pointing forward.

*Confirm.* Film the same frames again.
:::

## Frames 16 to 19: Second Stepping Cycle

This cycle is the mirror image of the first. You begin in left sanchin dachi with both arms in the block, and you step forward with the right foot.

![**Figure 11.2** Frames 16 to 19. The right foot steps forward into right sanchin dachi and the stance sets; the left fist draws to the hip; the left fist punches; the left arm returns to the block. This position, at the far end of the floor pattern, is where the first turn begins.](figures/fig11_2.png)

### Frame 16 – Step forward (right foot) and set

**Action.** Slide the right foot forward in a crescent into right sanchin dachi, arms holding the block, and set the stance as the foot lands. Because this is the second time you have performed this pattern, Frames 11 and 12 are combined into one frame here.

**Breath.** In during the step; settle at the end of the in-breath.

**Checkpoints.** As Frames 11 and 12. This is the last forward step before the turn; make sure it is the same length as the others. Students often take a short last step, without meaning to, because they are already thinking about the turn.

### Frame 17 – Pulling hand (left)

**Action.** Draw the left fist to the left hip. **Breath.** In. **Checkpoints.** As Frame 7.

### Frame 18 – Punch (left)

**Action.** Punch with the left fist to the centre line. **Breath.** Out. **Checkpoints.** As Frame 8. Rear foot, now the left, presses into the floor.

### Frame 19 – Return to block

**Action.** Return the left arm to the block and settle. **Breath.** Short exchange, end of out-breath. **Checkpoints.** As Frame 9. Take a moment here. The turn that follows needs a fully settled starting position.

::: drill
**Drill 11.1 – Walking Cycles**

*What to do:* Starting from right sanchin dachi with the double block, perform Frames 11 to 19 three times in succession, moving forward along a line. If your space is short, step backwards slowly to return to the start between sets, without arm movements.

*Focus:* On the first set, attend only to the head height. On the second, attend only to the arms during the step. On the third, attend only to the moment of setting the stance before the pulling hand.

*Why it matters:* These are the three most common faults of the advance. Taking them one at a time is much more effective than trying to watch all three at once.
:::

## The Turns: Principles

Before we perform the first turn, it is worth understanding what a turn in Sanchin has to achieve and why it is difficult.

A turn must change the direction you are facing by 180 degrees. It must leave you in a correct sanchin dachi facing the new direction. It must keep the head at the same height throughout. It must keep the arms in the block, or move them in a controlled way, without loose movement. It must keep the baseline tension in the legs, hips and back. And it must be performed slowly, on a single breath cycle.

What makes it difficult is that, for a moment in the middle of the turn, the feet are neither in the old stance nor in the new one. The base is at its narrowest and the body is at its most vulnerable. Many students rush through this moment, and the rush shows as a wobble, a rise, a sudden shift of weight or a foot placed in the wrong spot that has to be adjusted afterwards.

The turn described here is performed by sliding the rear foot across behind the front foot, then pivoting on both feet to face the rear. It ends in sanchin dachi with the foot that moved now in front. Turns are one of the points at which lineages differ most, as Table 10.1 noted. Some step the rear foot across in front of the front foot instead; some pivot first and step afterwards. If your dojo turns differently, use the principles and the checkpoints from this section, which apply to every version.

## Frames 20 to 24: The First Turn and the Cycle Facing the Rear

You begin in right sanchin dachi, right foot forward, arms in the double block, facing the front.

![**Figure 11.4** Frames 20 to 24. The left foot slides across behind; the body pivots to face the rear in left sanchin dachi as the arms re-form the block; the right fist draws to the hip; the right fist punches and returns; the right foot steps forward (towards the starting point) into right sanchin dachi.](figures/fig11_4.png)

### Frame 20 – Slide across

**Action.** Keeping the right foot planted and gripping, slide the left foot across the floor, behind the line of the right heel, to a point about shoulder-width to the far side of the right foot. As the left foot moves, the arms may draw in slightly, crossing in front of the chest as in Frame 4, or remain in the block; follow your teacher. The version shown crosses the arms.

**Breath.** In, through the nose, slowly. The in-breath should cover both this frame and the start of the pivot.

**Checkpoints.** The head stays level. The left foot slides close to the floor without lifting. The weight stays centred between the feet and does not lurch onto the right foot. The knees stay slightly bent.

**Common errors.** Lifting the left foot and stamping it down; placing it too far or too close; rising onto the toes; looking round over the shoulder before the feet have moved.

### Frame 21 – Pivot and block

**Action.** Pivoting on both feet, turn clockwise through 180 degrees to face the rear. The left foot, which is now in front, turns its toes in; the right foot, now behind, turns its toes forward or slightly in. The stance sets in left sanchin dachi facing the rear. As the stance sets, the arms open into the double block.

**Breath.** Out, steady and audible, ending as the block arrives and the stance sets.

**Checkpoints.** The head turns with the body, not before it; the eyes arrive at the rear gaze point as the body does. The stance is correct on arrival, without adjustment: width, length, toe angles, knees, pelvis. The double block is symmetrical. The baseline tension has not dropped during the turn.

**Common errors.** Rising during the pivot; turning the head first and "dragging" the body after it; arriving with a stance that is too narrow or crossed; letting the knees open during the pivot and then squeezing them back afterwards. See the problem box below.

### Frame 22 – Pulling hand (right)

**Action.** Draw the right fist to the right hip. **Breath.** In. **Checkpoints.** As Frame 13. Make sure the stance has fully set after the turn before you begin.

### Frame 23 – Punch and return (right)

**Action.** Punch with the right fist to the centre line, then return the right arm to the block and settle. Two movements are combined in this frame because they are identical to Frames 14 and 15.

**Breath.** Out on the punch; short exchange on the return.

**Checkpoints.** As Frames 14 and 15. Watch especially that the rear foot, now the right, presses into the floor during the punch. After a turn, many students find their weight has drifted forward, and the rear heel lifts.

### Frame 24 – Step forward (right foot), facing the rear

**Action.** Facing the rear, step the right foot forward in a crescent into right sanchin dachi. Note that "forward" now means towards the end of the floor pattern where you started. The arms hold the block. Set the stance as the foot lands.

**Breath.** In during the step; settle at the end of the in-breath.

**Checkpoints.** As Frame 11. The step should be exactly as long as the forward steps were. If it is shorter, the floor pattern will drift and you will finish the kata in the wrong place.

::: fault
**Problem: "I wobble in the middle of the turn."**

*Check.* Film the turn from the side and from above if possible. Pause at the halfway point of the pivot. Look at the distance between your feet, the height of your head and the position of your weight.

*Cause.* The wobble almost always comes from one of three things. The feet are too close together at the halfway point because the sliding foot did not travel far enough across. The knees have straightened and the hips risen, raising the centre of mass. Or the weight has shifted onto one foot, usually the one that did not move.

*Correct.* First, mark the floor: put a strip of tape where the sliding foot should land at the end of Frame 20, shoulder-width to the far side of the front foot. Practise Frame 20 alone, twenty times, until the foot lands on the tape without looking. Second, practise the pivot of Frame 21 with your hands on your hips and your eyes on a mirror, keeping the top of your head level with a tape mark on the mirror. Third, practise the whole turn slowly with a partner's hand resting lightly on top of your head; you should feel no rise against it.

*Confirm.* Film the turn again and pause at the same point.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 11.2 – Turn Ladder**

Build the turn in four rungs, practising each until it is smooth before climbing to the next.

1. **Feet only.** With your hands on your hips and natural breathing, perform the slide and pivot ten times, turning back and forth. Keep your head level.
2. **Feet and arms.** Add the arm action: cross on the slide, block on the pivot. Ten times.
3. **Feet, arms and breath.** Add the in-breath on the slide and the out-breath on the pivot and block. Ten times.
4. **Full turn.** Add tension, building through the out-breath to a peak as the block and stance set. Five times.

*Why it matters:* The turn has more simultaneous demands than any other part of the kata. Building it in rungs stops you being overwhelmed and makes each element secure before the next is added.
:::

## Watching the Turn From Outside

Because the turn is so revealing, it is worth knowing what a teacher looks for when they watch it. Here, roughly in order of importance, are the things I look for when a student turns in Sanchin.

**Head height.** Does the head stay level, or does it rise and fall? A rise is the single most common sign of a weak turn.

**Arrival.** Does the student arrive in a correct stance, or do they have to adjust their feet after the turn? Any adjustment means the turn was not accurate.

**Eyes.** Do the eyes arrive with the body, or does the head turn first? A head that leads the turn usually means a body that is being dragged round rather than turned from the centre.

**Tension.** Does the baseline tension in the legs and back hold throughout, or does the body go soft in the middle of the turn and then re-tense? You can often see this as a slight sag in the knees or a loss of the tucked pelvis.

**Breath.** Is the breath continuous and steady, or does it catch or stop during the turn?

**Speed.** Is the turn performed at the same deliberate pace as the rest of the kata, or is it hurried?

You can use this list to watch yourself on video, or to watch a training partner. The practice of watching others carefully is valuable in its own right; it trains your eye, which in turn makes you more aware of your own movement.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Turn Score**

Film one complete first turn, Frames 19 to 21. Score each of the six points above from 0 (clearly wrong) to 2 (clearly right). Add up the scores out of 12.

- 10–12: your turn is secure. Keep practising it as part of the whole kata.
- 6–9: work on the lowest-scoring points with Drill 11.2.
- 0–5: return to rung 1 of the turn ladder and rebuild the turn slowly over two or three weeks.

Repeat the test every two weeks and record your scores.
:::

## The Far End of the Floor Pattern

A final practical point. If your training space is short, you may find that by the time you reach the turn you are close to a wall. This affects the kata more than you might expect: students who are near a wall unconsciously shorten their steps, hurry their turns and lose their gaze points.

If you have the choice, practise Sanchin in a space that gives you at least half a metre of clear room beyond the farthest point of the floor pattern. If you do not, practise the turns separately in a more open part of the room from time to time, so that the cramped version does not become your only version.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Each stepping cycle has five parts: step, set, pull, punch, return. The step is always taken with the rear foot, and the punch with the hand on the side of the new rear foot.
- During the step, the head stays level, the upper body travels as one unit and the arms hold the block without drifting.
- The stance must set fully before the pulling hand begins. Starting the technique early is a common fault.
- The turn described here slides the rear foot across behind the front foot and pivots through 180 degrees, ending in sanchin dachi facing the rear with the moving foot in front. Lineages differ; the principles apply to all.
- In the turn, the head stays level, the eyes arrive with the body, the stance is correct on arrival without adjustment, and the baseline tension and breath are continuous.
- After the turn, check that the rear heel stays down during the punch.
- Build the turn in rungs: feet, then arms, then breath, then tension.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Practise Drill 11.1, walking cycles, three times a week for two weeks.
2. Practise the stick-across-the-forearms exercise from the problem box, ten steps forward and back, before each session for a week.
3. Mark the landing point for the sliding foot of the turn with tape, and practise Frame 20 alone until you land on the tape without looking.
4. Build the turn using Drill 11.2, the turn ladder, over two to three weeks.
5. Complete the Turn Score test and record the result. Repeat every two weeks.
6. Perform Frames 1 to 24 in one continuous sequence, at Stage 2 breathing and light tension, three times. Note the frame at which your concentration first slips.

# Sanchin in Application

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is a training form, but it is also a fighting form. Its structure, its techniques and its testing all have direct uses at close range. This chapter looks at the principles behind Sanchin's applications, offers practical interpretations of its main techniques, introduces kakie, the Goju-Ryu pushing-hands practice that connects Sanchin to fighting, and sets out partner drills to test what the kata has built.
:::

Students sometimes ask what Sanchin is "for" in a fight. Nobody, after all, is going to advance on an attacker in slow crescent steps, breathing loudly and punching at a tenth of normal speed. If the kata is so important, how does it help when things are fast, chaotic and frightening?

The answer has two parts. The first is that Sanchin is mainly a training method rather than a fighting sequence. Its slowness, tension and testing build a body that is rooted, connected and hard to move, a mind that stays calm under pressure, and a breath that does not collapse when struck. Those qualities serve you in any kind of fighting, at any speed, in any stance. When teachers say Sanchin is the foundation of Goju-Ryu fighting, this is mostly what they mean.

The second part is that Sanchin's individual techniques do have direct applications, mostly at very close range, where Goju-Ryu has always been strongest. This chapter looks at both parts.

The study of kata applications is called *bunkai*, meaning analysis or breaking down. Applications vary widely between teachers and lineages, and nobody can say with certainty what the creators of a kata had in mind. The interpretations in this chapter are those most commonly taught for Sanchin, presented as useful ways to practise rather than as the only correct answers. Your teacher may show you others.

## Principles

Before looking at specific techniques, it helps to understand the principles that run through all of Sanchin's applications.

### Close range

Goju-Ryu was developed for fighting at close range, where opponents are near enough to grab, push and strike with short movements. Sanchin's stance, compact and strong in every direction, its short techniques and its emphasis on structure all suit this range. The applications of Sanchin are, almost without exception, close-range applications.

### Structure beats strength

At close range, the person whose structure holds usually wins. If your opponent pushes, pulls or strikes you and your stance stays rooted and your trunk stays connected, their force has nowhere to go but into the floor through your legs, and they are left off balance by their own effort. If your structure collapses, even a weaker opponent can move you. Sanchin's testing trains exactly this.

### Absorb, then respond

Sanchin's conditioning and breathing allow you to absorb some force without being damaged or winded. This buys time, at close range, to respond. It is not a strategy of standing still and taking punishment, which would be foolish, but a margin of safety for the moments when something gets through.

### Both hands work

In Sanchin, one hand is nearly always defending or controlling while the other prepares or strikes. The block holds while the punch drives; the pulling hand draws in while the other strikes. In application, this becomes a constant principle: one hand controls the opponent's arm or body, the other attacks.

### Breathing as protection

The Sanchin out-breath, with the trunk braced around it, is the moment of greatest resistance to a blow. Experienced practitioners learn to time this brace with incoming force. The habit of breathing out under pressure, rather than gasping or holding, also helps prevent being winded and keeps the mind steady.

## Interpreting the Techniques

### The double block and the guard

At its simplest, the double middle block is a guard. The forearms cover the head and upper body, the elbows protect the ribs, and the compact structure is very hard to break through. In application, the double block can be read as:

- a **covering guard** against an attacker's strikes to the head and body;
- a **frame** against an attacker who closes in, the forearms pressing against the attacker's chest or arms to control distance;
- a **double deflection** of both of an attacker's arms, for example against a two-handed grab or push.

### The single block

The single middle block, sweeping outward from the centre, deflects a straight attack to the body or head. Because Sanchin's block is short, with the elbow down and the arm connected to the back, it is strong and economical. It does not need to travel far to move the attacking arm off line.

### The pulling hand and the punch

The pulling hand is commonly interpreted as a grab and pull. The hand seizes the attacker's wrist, sleeve or clothing and draws it in towards your hip. This has several effects: it pulls the attacker off balance, it brings them onto your punch, and it controls their arm so they cannot use it to strike or defend.

The punch that follows is short, driven from the ground, at the centre line. At close range, the opponent's solar plexus or ribs are the natural target. Because the attacker is being pulled in by the other hand, the punch does not need to travel far to land with force.

### The double spear-hand

The double spear-hand thrust is usually interpreted as a strike with the fingertips to soft targets on both sides of the body, typically the floating ribs or the area below them. It can also be read as a means of breaking an opponent's two-handed hold, the spear-hands driving forward between the attacker's arms and forcing them apart.

Strikes with the fingertips require well-conditioned hands. In training, practise these applications with the flat of the hand or with light touch only.

### The circular block and tiger's mouth

The circular block is one of the most practical techniques in Goju-Ryu. The circling hands meet the attacker's arm or arms, redirect them and trap them, typically one hand pressing the attacking arm down and across while the other rises to control the elbow or upper arm. The tiger's-mouth push then drives forward with both palms, often to the attacker's chest and abdomen, or to the face and body, pushing them away or knocking them off balance.

Read this way, the closing sequence of Sanchin is a complete defensive response: deflect, trap and drive away.

### The step

Sanchin's crescent step can be read as an entering movement: closing distance to an attacker while keeping the groin protected and the stance rooted, and, at very close range, placing the front foot inside or outside the attacker's foot to unbalance them. The turns, in turn, can be read as changes of direction to face a second attacker, or as turning out of a hold.

## Kakie: Sanchin in Contact

The bridge between Sanchin and fighting, in many Goju-Ryu dojos, is a partner practice called *kakie*. Kakie is sometimes compared to the pushing-hands practices of Chinese martial arts. Two partners face each other in a stance, often sanchin dachi or a similar close stance, and place the backs of their wrists or forearms together. They then push and yield in turn, in a slow, circular pattern, each trying to find a gap in the other's structure while maintaining their own.

Kakie develops several qualities at once:

- **Structure under pressure.** As in Sanchin testing, the stance, trunk and shoulders must hold against the partner's push.
- **Sensitivity.** Through the point of contact, each partner learns to feel the other's intentions: where they are pushing, where they are weak, when they are about to change.
- **Softness.** To yield to the partner's push without losing structure, each must learn the soft, flowing control that Tensho develops.
- **Timing.** Kakie teaches the moment to yield and the moment to push, which is the essence of close-range fighting.

At its simplest, kakie is a cooperative exercise. As students advance, it becomes more freeform, with grabs, pulls, strikes and throws added, until it approaches close-range sparring.

Kakie is best learned from a teacher, because its details vary between lineages and the subtleties are hard to put into words. The basic drill below is a starting point.

![**Figure 26.3** The basic kakie position. Two partners face each other in sanchin dachi with the backs of their wrists in contact. They push and yield in turn in a slow circle, each maintaining their own structure.](figures/fig26_3.png)

::: drill
**Drill 26.1 – Basic Kakie**

*You need:* a partner and an instructor's guidance.

*What to do:* Stand facing your partner, each in sanchin dachi with the right foot forward, close enough that your right forearms can touch with the elbows bent. Place the backs of your right wrists together. Partner A pushes slowly forward towards B's chest. B yields, drawing the arm back and slightly round, keeping the elbow down and the stance rooted, until A's push is redirected past B's body. Then B pushes and A yields. Continue in a slow, continuous circle for one minute. Change sides.

*What to look for:* Both partners keep their Sanchin structure throughout: feet gripping, pelvis tucked, shoulders down, elbows down. Neither pushes so hard that the other must break their stance, and neither yields so much that the contact is lost.

*Why it matters:* Kakie is Sanchin brought into contact with another person. It shows very quickly whether your structure holds when someone is actually pushing on it, and it develops the sensitivity that turns structure into skill.
:::

## Partner Drills for Application

The following drills test Sanchin's techniques in simple, cooperative application. Practise them slowly at first and with light contact. Speed and force can increase only as both partners become confident and only with the instructor's agreement.

::: drill
**Drill 26.2 – Block, Pull and Punch**

*What to do:* Partner A steps in with a slow straight punch to B's body. B, in sanchin dachi, deflects it with a single block, then turns the blocking hand to grab A's wrist and pulls it towards B's hip, while punching lightly to A's ribs with the other hand. Ten times each side, then swap roles.

*Focus:* B's stance must not move as A's punch arrives. The pull should draw A slightly forward and off balance onto the punch.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 26.3 – Structure Under Push**

*What to do:* B stands in sanchin dachi with the double block. A places both hands on B's forearms and pushes steadily forward, trying to move B back. B holds the structure, breathing out as the push comes. Then A pushes from different angles: from the side, downward on the arms, upward under the elbows. B holds each.

*Focus:* This is testing in a more realistic form. If B's structure is sound, A will feel as though pushing a wall, and the harder A pushes, the more A unbalances themselves.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 26.4 – Circle and Push**

*What to do:* A reaches slowly towards B's chest with one arm, as if to grab or push. B performs the circular block, meeting A's arm with the circling hands and redirecting it down and across, then pushes A back gently with the tiger's mouth to A's chest and abdomen. Ten times each side, then swap.

*Focus:* The circles should meet A's arm smoothly, without clashing. The push is a push, not a strike, in training.
:::

## Safety in Application Training

Application practice brings two people into contact with intent, and that changes the risks. A few rules keep it productive.

**Agree the speed.** Begin every application drill at walking pace. Increase speed only when both partners can perform the drill smoothly and only by agreement. The attacker sets the speed; the defender never "wins" by suddenly going faster.

**Control the contact.** Strikes in application drills are touches, not blows, unless your instructor has set up a specific conditioning drill. Fingertip strikes, in particular, are only ever mimed.

**Protect the joints.** Grabs and pulls should be firm enough to be realistic but must never wrench the partner's wrist, elbow or shoulder. If a lock or a twist develops by accident, release it at once.

**Keep the stop signal.** The same signal agreed for testing applies here.

**Swap roles.** Both partners should practise both roles equally. Being the attacker teaches you how the application feels from the other side, which makes you a better defender.

## A Note on Self-Defence and Sport

The applications in this chapter are training tools. They develop principles, structure and sensitivity. They are not a self-defence course, and nothing in this book can prepare you for a real assault the way specialised, pressure-tested training with a qualified instructor can. The best self-defence is usually awareness, avoidance and leaving.

Equally, Sanchin's applications are not designed for sport karate, where rules, distance and scoring are very different. But students who compete often find that Sanchin training improves their balance, their resistance to being pushed out of position and their calm under the pressure of a match. The structure carries over even where the techniques do not.

## From Kata to Fighting

None of this means that you should fight in sanchin dachi or try to perform the techniques of Sanchin exactly as they appear in the kata. Real fighting is fast, messy and unpredictable. Stances shorten and widen, techniques are cut short, nothing looks quite like the form.

What carries over is the structure and the mind. A student with years of Sanchin behind them tends to stand differently in any stance: rooted, compact, hard to push over. They tend to punch differently: from the ground, through a connected body. They tend to receive pressure differently: absorbing and redirecting it rather than collapsing or flailing. And they tend to stay calmer, breathing out under stress rather than holding their breath.

These qualities are hard to see in a single technique. They show up over time, in sparring and in partner work, as a kind of solidity. Other students notice it before the practitioner does. That solidity is what Sanchin is for.

### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin is mainly a training method that builds rooting, connection, calm and breath under pressure. These qualities serve in any kind of fighting.
- Its techniques also have direct close-range applications. Bunkai varies between teachers; the interpretations here are common and useful, not definitive.
- Principles: close range, structure beats strength, absorb then respond, both hands work, breathing as protection.
- The double block serves as a guard, frame or double deflection; the pulling hand as a grab and pull onto the punch; the spear-hands as strikes to the ribs or a hold-breaker; the circular block as a deflect, trap and push.
- Kakie, the Goju-Ryu pushing-hands practice, brings Sanchin's structure into contact with a partner and develops sensitivity and timing.
- Practise applications slowly, with light contact and a cooperative partner, under supervision.

### Training Tasks

1. Practise Drill 26.2, block, pull and punch, ten times each side, twice a week for two weeks.
2. Practise Drill 26.3, structure under push, with a partner, from four directions.
3. Practise Drill 26.4, circle and push, slowly and cooperatively.
4. Ask your teacher to introduce kakie, and practise Drill 26.1 regularly once you have learned it.
5. In your next sparring or partner session, notice one moment when your Sanchin structure helped you. Write it down.

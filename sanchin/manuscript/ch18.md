# Partner Drills and Self-Testing

::: chapter-intro
Testing does not have to wait for the instructor. This chapter sets out a structured series of partner drills that take two students from light touch to firm, moving tests over twelve weeks, and a set of self-tests for anyone who trains alone, using walls, doorframes, resistance bands, a mirror and a camera. It ends with advice on keeping a testing journal, so that each test feeds your training rather than simply marking it.
:::

There is a limit to how much testing an instructor can give in a class. With fifteen students in the room and an hour on the clock, each student may receive a minute or two of hands-on attention. That is valuable, but it is not enough to change a habit. The change happens in the hours between classes, when the student practises the correction until it sticks.

The drills in this chapter are designed to fill those hours. The first half is for pairs of students, working together under the general supervision of their instructor. The second half is for people training alone. Both are built on the testing map of Chapter 17, and both follow the rules of Chapter 16. If you have not read those chapters, please read them first.

## Working With a Partner

A good testing partner is one of the most valuable things a Sanchin student can have. Ideally, your partner is of roughly similar size and experience, takes the rules seriously and is willing to give you honest feedback. Over time, the two of you will learn each other's structures very well, and you will notice changes in each other that neither of you would notice alone.

Agree a few things before you start.

- **The stop signal.** A word, or a tap on the thigh. Whoever gives it, the test stops at once.
- **The level.** Agree the maximum level for the session in advance. Do not exceed it, however well things are going.
- **The focus.** Decide which tests you will work on. Three or four per session is plenty.
- **Feedback.** Agree to tell each other briefly and specifically what you felt, without commentary on how hard the other person is trying.

## A Twelve-Week Partner Programme

The programme below takes two students from their first Level 1 tests to confident Level 2 testing, with an introduction to light Level 3 work in the final phase, over twelve weeks. It assumes two sessions a week of about fifteen minutes each, either in class time set aside by the instructor or before or after class.

![**Figure 18.1** The twelve-week partner programme. Each phase builds on the one before. Light Level 3 work appears only in the final phase and only with the instructor's agreement.](figures/fig18_1.png)

### Weeks 1 to 3: Touch

Every session, run the standard testing round from Chapter 17 at Level 1 only. The tester rests their hands at each test point and describes what they feel; the student adjusts in response and confirms whether the description matches what they feel from the inside. Swap roles halfway through.

The aim of this phase is not to test strength but to build a shared vocabulary and to train both partners' sensitivity. By the end of week 3, each partner should be able to locate every test point without the book and describe what they feel at each in a sentence.

### Weeks 4 to 6: Pressure in a fixed stance

Move to Level 2 for Tests 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 13 and 15 and the two whole-body pushes, with the student in a fixed stance in the double block. Keep the other tests at Level 1.

Use the pressure ladder below for every Level 2 test. Record the rung at which each test first gives way. Over the three weeks, you should see the rungs rise.

::: drill
**Drill 18.1 – The Pressure Ladder**

*What to do:* For any Level 2 test, the tester applies pressure in five steps, one step per out-breath:

1. **Contact.** The hand rests on the test point with no pressure.
2. **Light.** The weight of the hand and forearm.
3. **Moderate.** Roughly the force of pressing a stapler.
4. **Firm.** Roughly the force of pushing open a heavy door.
5. **Strong.** Roughly the force of pushing a loaded supermarket trolley up a slight slope.

At each step, the tester holds for the out-breath and releases on the in-breath. If the structure gives way at any step, the tester stops, reports the step and the way it gave way, and does not continue to the next step.

*Why it matters:* The ladder makes the force of testing consistent and measurable, so that progress can be tracked. It also stops enthusiastic testers jumping straight to strong pressure.
:::

### Weeks 7 to 9: Pressure in motion

In this phase, the tests move into the kata itself. The student performs a section of the kata, usually Frames 4 to 19, slowly, and the tester walks alongside, applying Level 2 pressure at particular moments.

The most useful moving tests are these:

- **Shoulder press during the step.** The tester walks behind the student with both hands resting on the shoulders, applying light pressure during each step. The shoulders should not rise and the head should not bob.
- **Elbow hold during the step.** The tester walks beside the student with a hand resting under one elbow. The elbow should stay still as the student steps.
- **Punch push-back after the step.** At the end of each punch, the tester applies the push-back test. Many students find their punch holds well in a fixed stance but gives way after a step, because the stance has not fully set.
- **Hip push during the set.** As the student's front foot lands and the stance sets, the tester applies a gentle sideways push to the hip. This tests whether the stance is complete before the next technique begins.

![**Figure 18.2** Testing in motion. The tester walks behind the student, hands resting on the shoulders, as the student steps forward in a crescent. Light pressure is applied throughout the step. The shoulders should stay down and the head level.](figures/fig18_2.png)

::: fault
**Problem: "My structure holds when I am standing still, but not when I move."**

*Check.* Ask your partner which moments in the movement show the weakness. Is it during the step, at the moment of landing, or at the start of the next technique?

*Cause.* During the step, the base is temporarily narrower and the attention is on the feet, so the upper body is often allowed to soften. At landing, the student often starts the next technique before the stance has set. Both are problems of timing and attention, not of strength.

*Correct.* Practise the step alone with a partner's hands resting on your shoulders, ten steps forward and back, focusing only on keeping the shoulders down and the head level. Then practise the landing: step, set, pause for one full breath, then begin the pulling hand. Gradually shorten the pause until the set is quick but complete.

*Confirm.* Run the moving tests again.
:::

### Weeks 10 to 12: Consolidation and light patting

In the final phase, run the full standard round at Level 2 at the start of each session, and the moving tests during a full performance of the kata. With your instructor's agreement, and only if both partners are comfortable, introduce light Level 3 patting on the large muscles: Tests 4, 9, 14, 16 and 17.

Level 3 patting should begin very lightly: a relaxed open hand, dropped onto the muscle from a few centimetres, timed with the student's out-breath. Increase the height of the drop gradually over the three weeks. The aim is not to hurt but to give the muscle a sharp, brief stimulus that it learns to meet with a well-timed contraction. If a pat causes pain, flinching or a held breath, reduce it.

At the end of week 12, compare the scorecards from week 1, week 6 and week 12. Most pairs find a marked improvement in the number of tests held and the rung reached on the pressure ladder.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

I have watched many pairs of students go through programmes like this one, and the most striking change is rarely in the student being tested. It is in the tester. Students who have spent twelve weeks feeling other people's shoulders, backs and knees develop a far more precise sense of their own. They begin to correct themselves before anyone else has to. Testing others is one of the most underrated ways of improving your own Sanchin.
:::

## Testing on Your Own

Not everyone has a training partner, and even those who do will spend most of their practice alone. The following self-tests use simple equipment to give you some of the feedback a partner would. None is a perfect substitute, but together they cover most of the testing map.

### The wall

A wall gives you a fixed, immovable reference.

- **Punch press.** As in Chapter 14, place your fist against the wall at the end of a punch and lean into it. Check the wrist alignment, the shoulder position and the feeling of the push travelling into your rear foot. This substitutes for Test 8.
- **Back to the wall.** Stand in sanchin dachi with your back an inch from a wall. Perform slow punches. If your bottom touches the wall, your pelvis is tilting; if your shoulder blades leave your awareness of the wall, your shoulders are reaching. This substitutes for Tests 3 and 12.
- **Hip to the wall.** Stand side-on to a wall in sanchin dachi, with your hip touching it. Press your hip gently into the wall for an out-breath, keeping your stance. This substitutes for Test 13, in reverse.

### The doorframe

A doorframe gives you two parallel surfaces at a useful width.

- **Block press.** Stand in the doorway in sanchin dachi with your arms in the double block. Press the outsides of your forearms outwards against the frame for an out-breath, keeping your elbows down and your shoulders down. This substitutes for Test 6.
- **Elbow press.** With your elbows just inside the frame, press them outward and slightly down for an out-breath. This trains the latissimus connection of Test 4.

### The resistance band

A long resistance band, of the kind sold for exercise, is one of the most useful pieces of equipment for solo Sanchin testing.

- **Shoulder press.** Stand on the middle of the band in sanchin dachi, with the ends looped over your shoulders, so that the band pulls down on your shoulders. Hold the double block and breathe. The band provides a steady downward pressure like Test 2, and you can increase it by shortening the band.
- **Punch resistance.** Anchor the band behind you at hip height, hold one end in your fist at the pulling-hand position, and punch forward against the band's resistance. This substitutes for the partner resistance punch of Drill 14.2.
- **Knee band.** Loop a light band around both knees in sanchin dachi, so that it pulls them outward. Hold the stance and press the thighs inward against it. This substitutes for Test 15.

![**Figure 18.3** Self-testing with a resistance band. Left: the band looped from under the feet over the shoulders, providing steady downward pressure. Right: the band anchored behind, providing resistance to the punch.](figures/fig18_3.png)

### The mirror and the camera

A mirror shows you shape, but only from one angle and only while you are looking at it, which changes your posture. A camera shows you what others see, from any angle, without your attention affecting it. Use both, but rely on the camera.

Set up a short routine of recordings and repeat it every two weeks:

1. Front view, double block, five breaths.
2. Side view, one punch-and-block cycle on each side.
3. Side view, one step and one turn.
4. Front view, the double spear-hand and one circular block.

Watch the recordings with the testing map beside you, and check the points you can see: shoulder height, head position, elbow position, fist height, punch path, pelvis angle, rear heel, knee alignment, head height during the step.

### Self-touch

Finally, your own hands can provide Level 1 feedback for several tests. Place a hand on your lower abdomen during the out-breath (Test 10), on your lower back during the in-breath (Test 12), on your own buttock or thigh during the stance (Tests 14 and 16), or on the opposite shoulder during a punch (Test 2, on the punching side). These are simple checks, but they keep your attention on the parts of the body you cannot see.

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Solo Round**

Using only a wall, a doorframe, a resistance band and your own hands, run this round in about five minutes.

1. Band over shoulders: hold the double block for five breaths. Do your shoulders stay down?
2. Doorframe block press: three out-breaths. Do your elbows stay down?
3. Back to the wall: five slow punches. Does your bottom touch the wall?
4. Wall punch press: one each side. Is your wrist straight?
5. Knee band: five breaths in sanchin dachi. Do your knees stay over your toes?
6. Self-touch: one out-breath with a hand on your lower abdomen. Is it firm?

Record each as held, partly held or gave way, in the same way as the partner round.
:::

## The Testing Journal

Testing is only useful if you act on it. A single test tells you where your structure is weak today. A series of tests, recorded and compared, tells you whether your training is working.

Keep a simple testing journal, either in your training notebook or on copies of the scorecard in Appendix B. For each session, record:

- the date;
- who tested you, or that the test was solo;
- the level;
- the result of each test: held, partly held, gave way, and for Level 2, the rung of the pressure ladder;
- one sentence on what you will work on next.

Every month, look back over the journal. Which tests have improved? Which have not? If a test has not improved after a month, your correction is not working. Go back to the Check and Cause steps of the loop from Chapter 1, and look for a different cause.

::: drill
**Drill 18.2 – Monthly Review**

*What to do:* On the first training day of each month, spend fifteen minutes with your testing journal and your most recent recordings. Answer three questions in writing:

1. Which test has improved most over the past month, and why?
2. Which test has improved least, and what might be causing it?
3. What single correction will I focus on this month?

*Why it matters:* The monthly review turns a collection of test results into a training plan. It is the Confirm step of the Check, Cause, Correct and Confirm loop, carried out at a larger scale.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- A testing partner of similar size and experience is very valuable. Agree a stop signal, the level, the focus and the style of feedback before each session.
- The twelve-week partner programme moves from touch (weeks 1–3) to pressure in a fixed stance (4–6), pressure in motion (7–9) and consolidation with light patting (10–12).
- The pressure ladder makes Level 2 testing consistent and measurable: contact, light, moderate, firm, strong, one step per out-breath.
- Moving tests often reveal weaknesses that fixed tests miss, particularly during the step and at the moment of landing.
- Solo testing uses a wall, a doorframe, a resistance band, a mirror, a camera and your own hands to cover most of the testing map.
- Keep a testing journal, review it monthly and change your approach if a test has not improved.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Find a testing partner and agree your stop signal, levels and feedback style.
2. Begin the twelve-week partner programme, recording each session in your testing journal.
3. If you train alone, buy or borrow a long resistance band and practise the three band tests in this chapter.
4. Set up the four-recording camera routine and repeat it every two weeks.
5. Complete the Solo Round test once a week for a month.
6. On the first day of next month, carry out Drill 18.2, the monthly review.
:::

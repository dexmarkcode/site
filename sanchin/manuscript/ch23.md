# Hojo Undo for Sanchin

::: chapter-intro
Okinawan Goju-Ryu has a long tradition of supplementary training with simple tools: stone weights on wooden handles, gripping jars, iron rings, wrist rollers and boxes of gravel. This tradition, hojo undo, was designed to build exactly the strength that Sanchin and the other kata demand. This chapter describes the main tools, how each one supports Sanchin, how to use them safely and what modern equipment can stand in for them if you have no access to the originals.
:::

Walk into a traditional Okinawan Goju-Ryu dojo and you will usually find, along one wall or in a corner of the yard, a collection of objects that look as though they belong in a builder's shed rather than a martial arts hall. Concrete weights set on wooden poles. Earthenware jars with thick rims. Heavy iron rings. A wooden barbell. A post wrapped in straw rope. These are the hojo undo tools, and in many dojos they are used before every class.

*Hojo undo* means supplementary exercise. The tools were developed, or adopted from Chinese practice, by the early teachers of Naha-te, and Chojun Miyagi organised and promoted their use as part of Goju-Ryu. They are simple, cheap and, used well, remarkably effective. Each one targets a particular quality: the grip, the wrist, the shoulder, the forearm, the legs, the trunk. Together they build a body that can hold Sanchin's structure for as long as the kata demands.

Using the tools well is a skill in its own right, and the movements are best learned from a teacher. The descriptions below give you enough to understand what each tool does and to start safely. Where your teacher's method differs, follow your teacher.

## Principles of Hojo Undo

A few principles apply to all the tools.

**Use the Sanchin structure.** Most hojo undo exercises are performed in a stance, often sanchin dachi or shiko dachi, with the same posture, breathing and connection that the kata requires. The tool adds load; the body responds with structure. This is what makes the training so specific.

**Breathe.** Breathe in the Sanchin way: in through the nose, out through the mouth, timed with the effort. Never hold the breath.

**Control.** Hojo undo is slow and deliberate. The tools are not swung for momentum. Each movement is controlled through its full range, and particularly on the way back.

**Start light.** Traditional tools are often heavy. Begin with a light version, or hold the tool closer to its centre of weight, and progress gradually. Tendons and joints, especially in the wrist and elbow, need time to adapt.

**Little and often.** A few minutes with one or two tools before each training session is better than an hour once a week.

## Chi Ishi: The Strength Stone

The *chi ishi* is a weight on the end of a wooden handle, rather like a hammer with a heavy head. Traditionally the weight was a stone; today it is usually concrete, cast around one end of a handle about forty to fifty centimetres long. Weights vary from about two to eight kilograms.

Because the weight is at the end of a lever, the chi ishi is much harder to control than a dumbbell of the same weight. Moving it in slow arcs builds the grip, the wrist, the forearm and the shoulder, and it particularly trains the small stabilising muscles around the shoulder blade, which are exactly the muscles that hold the Sanchin block and punch in place.

### For Sanchin

The chi ishi trains the connection between the hand and the back. When you hold it at arm's length and move it slowly, you can feel the effort travel from the fingers, through the wrist and forearm, into the shoulder blade and the latissimus. If the shoulder rises or the elbow flares, the stone wobbles. It is an honest teacher.

### Basic exercises

1. **Forward raise.** Stand in sanchin dachi holding the chi ishi by the end of the handle, arm down at your side, stone pointing forward. Raise the arm forward to shoulder height, keeping it almost straight and the shoulder down, then lower slowly. Breathe out on the lift. Five to ten repetitions each arm.
2. **Wrist turn.** Hold the chi ishi in front of you, elbow bent at a right angle and close to your side, as in the block. Slowly rotate the forearm so the stone moves from pointing up to pointing outward and back, then from pointing up to pointing inward and back. Five to ten each way.
3. **Block lever.** Hold the chi ishi in the Sanchin block position, handle vertical, stone above your fist. Slowly lower the stone forward, by bending the wrist, until the handle is horizontal, then raise it back. This trains the wrist and forearm to stay straight under load. Five to ten repetitions.
4. **Shoulder circle.** Hold the chi ishi at your side and make slow, controlled circles with the whole arm, forward and backward. Five each way, each arm. Advanced; start with a very light stone.

![**Figure 23.2** Two chi ishi exercises. Left: the forward raise, arm almost straight, shoulder down. Right: the block lever, with the forearm upright as in the Sanchin block and the stone lowered forward by bending the wrist.](figures/fig23_2.png)

::: fault
**Problem: "My elbow aches after using the chi ishi."**

*Cause.* Usually too heavy a stone, too far from the hand, too soon. The tendons at the elbow are being overloaded.

*Correct.* Stop for a week or two until the ache settles. Then return with a much lighter stone, or hold the handle closer to the weight, which shortens the lever. Reduce repetitions. Increase very gradually over months. If the ache persists, see a physiotherapist.
:::

## Nigiri Game: The Gripping Jars

The *nigiri game* are a pair of jars, traditionally earthenware, with a thick rim at the top. The student grips each jar by the rim with the fingertips, arms hanging at the sides, and then walks in sanchin dachi or shiko dachi, slowly, carrying the jars. As strength builds, the jars are filled progressively with sand, gravel or water to increase the load.

Of all the hojo undo tools, the nigiri game is the most directly connected to Sanchin. The student is performing the stepping of the kata while holding a load in the fingertips. The grip must hold, the shoulders must stay down, the trunk must stay upright and braced, and the stance must stay correct throughout.

### For Sanchin

The nigiri game builds finger and grip strength, forearm endurance, shoulder stability, trunk control and stance endurance, all at once and all in the Sanchin pattern. Many teachers regard it as the single most valuable hojo undo tool for the kata.

### Basic exercises

1. **Static hold.** Stand in sanchin dachi, holding the jars by the rims at your sides, arms straight, shoulders down. Hold for twenty to sixty seconds with Sanchin breathing.
2. **Sanchin walk.** Walk forward and back in sanchin dachi with the crescent step, holding the jars, for the length of the floor pattern. Two to four lengths.
3. **Arm raises.** Holding the jars by the rims, raise the arms slowly forward to shoulder height and lower them. Advanced; start with empty jars.

### Modern equivalent

Any container with a rim you can grip by the fingertips will work: small plastic buckets, paint tins, sturdy plastic tubs. Fill them with sand or water. Alternatively, hold weight plates by their rims ("plate pinches"). Start with empty containers.

![**Figure 23.3** The nigiri game walk. The jars are held by their rims in the fingertips, arms at the sides, as the student steps in sanchin dachi. The shoulders stay down and the trunk upright.](figures/fig23_3.png)

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

The first time most students try the nigiri game, they are shocked by how quickly their fingers give out, even with empty jars. Grip strength through the fingertips, rather than the whole hand, is something modern life hardly asks of us. Be patient. Start with the jars empty and short walks. Add a cup of sand each week. Within a couple of months, most students find not only their grip but their whole Sanchin feels more solid. The jars teach the body that the hands are connected to the back and the back to the legs, in a way that is very hard to teach in words.
:::

## Ishi Sashi: The Stone Padlocks

The *ishi sashi* are stone or iron weights shaped like old-fashioned padlocks, with a handle across the top. They are held one in each hand and used for a range of movements: punches, blocks, raises and presses, often in sanchin dachi or shiko dachi.

### For Sanchin

Performing the Sanchin punch and block slowly while holding the ishi sashi adds load directly to the techniques of the kata. It trains the shoulders to stay down under load and the elbow to stay in line.

### Basic exercises

1. **Loaded block.** In sanchin dachi, holding an ishi sashi in each hand, perform slow double blocks, ten repetitions.
2. **Loaded punch.** Perform slow alternating punches, five each side, keeping the wrist straight and the shoulder down. Start very light.

### Modern equivalent

Light dumbbells or kettlebells, held by the handle. A kettlebell held by the horns or the bell gives a different and useful challenge to the grip.

## Makiage Kigu: The Wrist Roller

The *makiage kigu* is a wooden bar with a cord tied to its centre and a weight on the end of the cord. The student holds the bar at arm's length and rolls it, winding the cord up until the weight reaches the bar, then unwinds it slowly.

### For Sanchin

The wrist roller builds forearm strength and endurance in both directions, which helps the fist stay firm and the wrist straight through the whole kata. It is particularly good for students whose blocks sag late in the kata.

### Basic exercise

Stand in sanchin dachi, arms forward at shoulder height, holding the bar with an overhand grip. Wind the weight up with alternate hands, then lower it slowly by unwinding. Two to four times. Then repeat with an underhand grip. Keep the shoulders down throughout.

### Modern equivalent

Easily made at home: a length of dowel, a cord and a small weight or a bottle of water.

## Jari Bako: The Gravel Box

The *jari bako* is a box or bucket filled with gravel, beans, rice or sand. The student thrusts the fingers into it, spear-hand fashion, and grips handfuls of the contents.

### For Sanchin

The jari bako strengthens the fingers and the small muscles of the hand, which helps the open-hand techniques at the end of the kata: the spear-hand and the tiger's mouth.

### Basic exercises

1. **Grabbing.** Plunge the open hand into a bucket of dried beans or rice, grip a handful firmly, lift and release. Twenty times each hand.
2. **Spear-hand thrusts.** With the fingers straight and together, thrust slowly into the bucket. Ten times each hand. Begin with soft material such as rice; do not progress to gravel until the fingers are well conditioned, and stop at once if the joints become painful.

### Modern equivalent

A bucket of rice is the classic home version. Rice bucket training is also used in other sports for hand and forearm strength.

## Other Tools

Several other traditional tools are used in Goju-Ryu dojos. They are less directly linked to Sanchin, but worth knowing about.

**Kongoken.** A large, heavy oval iron ring, used for lifting, carrying, rolling and partner exercises. It trains the whole body, especially the legs, trunk and grip. Its use is best learned from an experienced teacher.

**Tan.** A wooden or stone barbell, used for rolling along the forearms, presses and lifts. It conditions the forearms and builds general strength.

**Tetsu geta.** Iron clogs or sandals worn on the feet for leg raises and kicks. They are hard on the knees and hips and should be used only by experienced students, if at all.

**Makiwara.** A padded striking post. It conditions the knuckles and wrists and teaches the alignment of the fist on impact. Makiwara training is a large subject in its own right; it supports the Sanchin punch's alignment but is not part of Sanchin itself. Learn it from a teacher.

## Bodyweight and Band Exercises

Not everyone has access to traditional tools or a gym. The following exercises, which need only your body, a resistance band and a wall, cover most of the qualities Sanchin needs. They also form the backbone of the programme in Chapter 24.

**Legs and hips:** wall sit at the Sanchin angle; split squat with the knees tracking over the toes; glute bridge; side-lying leg lift; cushion squeeze in sanchin dachi.

**Trunk:** front plank; side plank; bird dog (on hands and knees, extend opposite arm and leg, keeping the trunk still); dead bug (on the back, extend opposite arm and leg slowly while keeping the lower back on the floor).

**Back and shoulders:** band pull-apart; straight-arm band pulldown; wall slide; press-up and press-up plus.

**Grip and forearms:** towel wring (twist a wet towel hard in both directions); fingertip press-ups against a wall; dead hang; rice bucket.

**Feet and balance:** towel gather; short foot; single-leg balance, eyes open then closed.

::: drill
**Drill 23.1 – Ten-Minute Hojo Circuit**

*What you need:* whatever tools you have, or the bodyweight and band alternatives.

*What to do:* Before your Sanchin practice, perform one round of the following, moving straight from one to the next with Sanchin breathing throughout:

1. Nigiri game walk or bucket walk in sanchin dachi: two lengths.
2. Chi ishi block lever or band pull-apart: ten repetitions.
3. Wall sit at the Sanchin angle: thirty to sixty seconds.
4. Makiage kigu or towel wring: one roll up and down, or twenty seconds each way.
5. Side plank: twenty to forty seconds each side.
6. Jari bako or rice bucket grabs: twenty each hand.

Rest for a minute, then begin your Sanchin.

*Why it matters:* This is the traditional pattern of hojo undo: a short, focused round of supplementary work that warms the body and loads exactly the structures the kata will use. Over months, it makes a large difference.
:::

## Safety With Traditional Tools

Traditional tools are simple, but they are not toys. A few rules:

- **Inspect them.** Check that weights are firmly fixed to handles, that cords are sound and that jars are not cracked. A chi ishi head that comes loose mid-swing is dangerous.
- **Clear space.** Leave plenty of room around you, especially with the chi ishi and kongoken.
- **Start light and progress slowly.** This cannot be said too often. Tendons in the wrist and elbow are especially vulnerable.
- **Keep the Sanchin structure.** If your posture breaks, the load is too heavy.
- **Breathe.** No breath-holding.
- **Children** should use only light, home-made versions under supervision.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Hojo undo is the traditional supplementary training of Okinawan karate, using simple tools to build the strength the kata needs.
- Principles: use the Sanchin structure, breathe, move with control, start light and train little and often.
- The chi ishi trains grip, wrist and shoulder-blade stability; the nigiri game trains fingertip grip, shoulders, trunk and stance endurance in the Sanchin pattern; the ishi sashi loads the block and punch; the makiage kigu builds forearm endurance; the jari bako strengthens the fingers for open-hand techniques.
- Modern equivalents are easy to find or make: buckets, dumbbells, kettlebells, a dowel and cord, a bucket of rice.
- Bodyweight and band exercises cover most of Sanchin's needs if no tools are available.
- A short hojo circuit before each Sanchin session is the traditional and most effective pattern.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Make or obtain at least two hojo undo tools or their modern equivalents: for example, a pair of buckets for the nigiri game and a dowel wrist roller.
2. Practise the nigiri game static hold and walk with empty containers three times a week for two weeks, then add a little weight each week.
3. Perform Drill 23.1, the ten-minute hojo circuit, before every Sanchin session for a month.
4. If your dojo has traditional tools, ask your teacher to show you the correct use of the chi ishi and nigiri game.
5. Note any change in how your Sanchin feels after a month of hojo work, particularly late in the kata.
:::

# How to Use This Book

This book is laid out so that you can read it from cover to cover the first time and then use it as a working manual afterwards. Every chapter follows the same pattern, so you always know where to look.

**Chapter introduction.** Each chapter opens with a short passage in italics setting out what it covers and why it matters.

**Main text.** The body of the chapter explains the subject in numbered sections. When a later chapter refers back to an earlier point, it gives the section number, so that, for example, "see 4.3" sends you to the third section of Chapter 4.

**Figures.** The illustrations are drawn as simple, consistent figures so that you can see the shape of a position without being distracted by clothing, faces or lighting. Solid figures show the position being described. Dashed grey figures show where the body has just come from. Arrows show the direction of movement. Where two figures are compared, a black circle with a cross marks a fault and a white circle with a tick marks the correction.

**Drill boxes.** Framed boxes with a heavy top rule set out a drill to practise: what you need, how to do it, how many repetitions and what to look for. Most can be done at home in a small space.

**Problem and fix boxes.** Shaded boxes with a heavy left rule describe a specific fault: what it looks like, why it happens and how to correct it. Part Seven gathers the most important faults together, but you will find these boxes throughout the book.

**Teacher's notes.** Passages between two thin rules give practical advice that does not sit neatly in the main argument: things I have noticed, traps to avoid and ways of explaining a point to other people.

**Test yourself.** Boxes with a dashed border give you a quick check you can carry out on your own, often with a wall, a mirror or a phone camera.

**Chapter summary.** A double-ruled panel at the end of each chapter lists the key points. If you are revising, start there.

**Training tasks.** Each chapter closes with a short list of questions and practical tasks. Some ask you to think; most ask you to stand up and do something.

## Suggested routes through the book

**If you are a beginner,** read Parts One and Two in order, then Part Four alongside your classes. Leave Part Six until your instructor has introduced testing in the dojo.

**If you are an experienced student,** start with Part Two, even if you think you know the stance, then go straight to Part Seven and find your own faults. Use Part Eight to plan your training.

**If you teach,** read the whole book once, then use Parts Four, Six and Seven as a reference for classes. The appendices contain checklists and score sheets you may copy for your own students.

**If you are returning after a break or training with an injury,** read Before You Begin again, then Chapters 8 and 27 before anything else.

## Equipment

You need very little: a space about three metres long, a full-length mirror or a phone that can record video, a wall, a broom handle or a length of dowel, and a notebook. Part Eight describes the traditional Okinawan training tools and simple ways to make or substitute them.

## Japanese terms

Japanese words are printed in italics the first time they appear in each chapter and are explained when they are first used. There is a glossary at the back of the book. Pronunciation is given only where it commonly causes confusion.

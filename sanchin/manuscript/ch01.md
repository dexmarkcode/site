# The Form at the Root of the Style

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is short, slow and simple to describe, and it takes a lifetime to do well. This chapter explains what the form is, why Goju-Ryu teachers place it first in the syllabus and last in their own practice, what it trains in the body and mind, and how this book will help you to work on it as a series of problems you can actually solve.
:::

Ask ten Goju-Ryu teachers what Sanchin is and you will get ten answers, all of them true. One will tell you it is a breathing exercise. Another will call it a stance drill with a few arm movements attached. A third will say it is the place where the style keeps its secrets, and a fourth that there are no secrets in it at all, only work. Someone will describe it as conditioning, someone else as meditation, and the oldest person in the room will probably smile and say that you will understand it in twenty years.

None of them is wrong. Sanchin is all of these things at once, which is exactly what makes it hard to learn. When you are a beginner, every one of those descriptions arrives as a separate instruction, and you have no way of knowing which matters most or how they fit together. You try to breathe properly and your stance collapses. You fix the stance and your shoulders rise. You drop the shoulders and forget to breathe. It can feel as though the kata has been designed to be impossible.

It has not. It has been designed to be whole. The purpose of this first chapter is to give you a map of that whole before we start building it piece by piece.

## What Sanchin Is

At its simplest, Sanchin is a *kata*, a prearranged sequence of movements practised alone. In the version most widely taught in Okinawan Goju-Ryu, it takes between two and four minutes to perform. The student stands in a narrow, inward-gripping stance called *sanchin dachi*, steps forward and back along a short straight line, turns once or twice depending on the lineage, and performs a small set of hand techniques: a middle-level block, a straight punch, a pulling-back action of the other hand, a double spear-hand thrust and a circular two-handed block. Every movement is accompanied by a deliberate breath, and the whole body is held under steady, controlled tension.

That is all. There are no kicks, no jumps, no throws, no fast combinations and no dramatic changes of direction. To a spectator who has never trained, Sanchin can look almost dull. To anyone who has tried it, it is one of the most demanding things in karate.

The name is usually translated as "three battles" or "three conflicts". The characters are *san*, meaning three, and *chin*, which in this context is generally read as battle, struggle or conflict. What the three battles are is a matter of interpretation, and Chapter 3 looks at the most common readings in some depth. The best known is that the kata unites three things that tend to pull against each other: the mind, the body and the breath. Another reading speaks of three parts of the body that must be brought together: the upper body, the trunk and the legs. A third talks about three qualities: strength, relaxation and timing. These are not competing theories so much as different ways into the same idea, which is that Sanchin trains you to make several things happen together that would rather happen separately.

The style itself takes its name from the same idea of combining opposites. *Go* means hard and *ju* means soft, and the name Goju-Ryu is usually traced to a line in an old Chinese text on martial arts, known in Okinawa as the *Bubishi*, which speaks of the way of breathing in and out being both hard and soft. Sanchin is where that combination is first taught. Most of the time it looks hard, but a well-performed Sanchin is never stiff. The hardness sits on top of a softness underneath: relaxed joints, a free breath, a calm face, a mind that is not fighting itself.

## Why Sanchin Comes First

In most Goju-Ryu dojos, Sanchin is either the first kata a student learns or one of the first two or three. It is also the kata senior teachers return to more than any other. Some practise it every day of their lives. That combination, first and last, is unusual. In most skills, the early exercises are scaffolding that you take down once the building is up. A pianist does not play five-finger exercises at a concert. A footballer does not demonstrate their first passing drill in a cup final. Why should a karateka who has trained for forty years still be practising their beginner's kata?

The answer is that Sanchin is not a beginner's kata. It is a foundation kata, which is a different thing. It teaches the body structure that every other technique in the style rests on, and that structure is never finished. It can always be a little more connected, a little more relaxed, a little more stable. A beginner learns the outline. An experienced student fills it in. A senior teacher refines it. All three are doing the same kata, and all three are working at the limit of their own ability.

There is a practical reason too. Sanchin is a diagnostic tool. Because it is slow, because it is done under tension and because it can be tested by hand, it shows up faults that fast movement hides. A student who leans slightly forward when they punch may never notice it in a fast combination. In Sanchin, held for several seconds under a teacher's hand, that lean is obvious, and the reason for it, usually a hip that is not tucked or a rear leg that is not pressing, can be found and fixed. When teachers say that Sanchin tells them everything about a student, this is largely what they mean. The kata is a slow-motion X-ray of how a person holds their body.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

When new students ask why we spend so long on a kata that barely moves, I sometimes ask them to stand on one leg with their eyes closed. Most wobble within a few seconds. Then I ask them to do it again, but this time to press the standing foot into the floor, tighten the buttock on that side and lengthen through the top of the head. Most become noticeably steadier. That small experiment is Sanchin in miniature: the same body, organised differently, becomes much more stable. The rest of the kata is simply that idea, taken much further.
:::

## What Sanchin Trains

It helps to be clear about what the kata is actually developing. In this book we break its training effect into five capacities. They overlap, of course, and you will not feel them as separate things when you perform the form. But naming them makes it easier to see where your own Sanchin is strong and where it needs work.

### A rooted stance

Sanchin dachi is designed to be hard to move. The feet grip the floor, the knees are drawn in, the thighs are braced, and the hips are tucked underneath the trunk so that the weight sinks down through the legs instead of hanging off the front of the body. A person standing well in sanchin dachi can be pushed from the front, from behind and from the sides without being displaced, even by someone much heavier. Part Two of this book builds the stance from the ground up.

### A connected trunk

In everyday life most of us move our arms and legs more or less independently of our trunk. We reach with the shoulder, we step with the knee, and the middle of the body comes along as a passenger. Sanchin trains the opposite habit. The muscles of the back, the abdomen, the sides and the hips are held together as one unit, so that force from the ground can pass up through the legs and out through the arms without leaking away at the waist. Teachers sometimes describe this as "closing" the body. You will find it described in detail in Chapter 6 and tested in Chapter 17.

### Breath control

Sanchin breathing is slow, full and deliberate. It teaches you to breathe deeply into the lower trunk, to keep breathing while under muscular tension, and to coordinate the breath with movement. Over time this carries over into fighting and into ordinary life. A student who has practised Sanchin for a few years tends to stay calmer under pressure, partly because they have spent so many hours keeping their breath steady while their body was working hard. Chapter 7 deals with breathing in full.

### Tension and relaxation

This is perhaps the most misunderstood part of the kata. Sanchin is often taught as an exercise in maximum tension, and some students finish each performance red-faced and trembling. That is not the aim. The aim is to learn to apply the right amount of tension in the right places at the right moment, and then to release it. The hardness of Sanchin is a skill, not a contest. Chapter 8 explains how to develop it without damaging yourself.

### Focus and endurance

Finally, Sanchin trains the mind. Holding a demanding posture for several minutes, breathing deliberately, while a teacher presses on your shoulders or slaps your thighs, requires concentration. It also requires a particular kind of patience: the willingness to stay in discomfort without either giving up or forcing your way through. Many senior practitioners say this is the most valuable thing the kata has given them. We look at it in Chapters 3 and 21.

## Sanchin and the Rest of the Style

Goju-Ryu as it is generally taught today has twelve core kata. Two of them, Sanchin and *Tensho*, are usually described as *heishu* kata, or "closed-hand" forms, in the sense that they are performed with closed, tense breathing and slow movement. Some teachers use the term to mean forms that develop the internal body rather than the external technique. The other ten are *kaishu* or "open-hand" kata, performed at more natural speed with a wide variety of techniques. In order of teaching, they are typically Gekisai Dai Ichi and Gekisai Dai Ni, which were composed in the twentieth century as introductory forms, followed by the older kata: Saifa, Seiyunchin, Shisochin, Sanseiru, Sepai, Kururunfa, Seisan and Suparinpei. Some associations teach a slightly different order, and some include additional forms of their own, but the core list is widely shared.

Sanchin sits underneath all of them. Its stance appears directly in several of the older kata, and its posture, its way of rooting and its way of connecting the arm to the trunk appear in all of them, even where the stance itself does not. When a teacher corrects your Seisan or your Kururunfa by telling you to "put more Sanchin in it", they are not asking you to stand in sanchin dachi. They are asking you to bring the same structure into a different shape.

![**Figure 1.2** Sanchin as the root of Goju-Ryu. The two heishu kata, Sanchin and Tensho, develop the body and breath. The kaishu kata build technique on top of that foundation. The order shown is a common teaching order; associations vary.](figures/fig1_2.png)

Tensho deserves a special mention. It was composed by Chojun Miyagi, the founder of Goju-Ryu, in the early twentieth century, and it is usually described as the soft partner to Sanchin's hard. Where Sanchin uses straight lines and closed fists, Tensho uses flowing, circular open-hand movements. Where Sanchin teaches the body to hold, Tensho teaches it to yield and redirect. Most teachers do not introduce Tensho until Sanchin is reasonably settled, because Tensho uses the same stance and breathing and builds on them. Chapter 25 is devoted to the relationship between the two.

## What Sanchin Is Not

It is worth clearing away a few misunderstandings early, because they cause a good deal of wasted effort and a fair number of injuries.

**Sanchin is not a strength contest.** Students who think the aim is to be as tense as possible end up with red faces, raised shoulders, held breath and stiff joints. They are often less stable than a student who uses moderate tension well, because rigidity is brittle. A good Sanchin is strong, but the strength comes from structure and connection, not from squeezing everything as hard as you can.

**Sanchin is not just a breathing exercise.** The breathing is important, and it is the part people notice, but a student who breathes beautifully in a collapsed stance has learned very little. The breath is meant to support the posture, not to replace it.

**Sanchin is not a performance.** In competitions and demonstrations Sanchin is sometimes performed with dramatic, loud breathing and visible shaking. There is nothing wrong with demonstrating, but the theatrical version and the training version are not the same thing. In the dojo, the breath should be audible but not exaggerated, and the effort should be visible but not strained.

**Sanchin testing is not bullying.** The tradition of checking a student's Sanchin by pressing and striking has, at times and in some places, been abused. Done properly, testing is a teaching tool. It gives the student honest feedback about whether their structure is holding, and it is always proportionate to their level. Chapter 16 sets out the purpose and the rules.

**Sanchin is not only for the young and strong.** It can be adapted for almost anyone. Older practitioners, people recovering from injury and people with certain health conditions can practise it with natural breathing and moderate tension and still gain much of its benefit. Chapter 27 explains how.

::: fault
**Problem: "I come out of Sanchin exhausted and dizzy."**

*What is happening.* You are almost certainly holding your breath during the tension phases and bearing down hard against a closed throat. This sharply raises the pressure in your chest and can reduce blood flow back to the heart for a moment, which is why you feel light-headed.

*Fix.* For the next two weeks, perform Sanchin with your mouth slightly open and breathe out continuously through every tense movement, making a soft "haa" sound rather than a grunt. Reduce your tension to roughly half of what you have been using. You should finish the kata warm and breathing a little harder than normal, not dizzy. Rebuild the intensity gradually using the method in Chapter 8.
:::

## A Problem-Solving Approach

This book treats Sanchin as a series of problems to solve. That is not because the kata is a puzzle, but because that is how improvement actually happens. You notice that something is not right. You find out why. You change something. You check whether the change worked. Then you notice the next thing.

Most students do the first and third steps and skip the second and fourth. They notice that their teacher keeps correcting their shoulders, so they try harder to keep them down. Next week the shoulders are up again, so they try harder still. They never find out *why* the shoulders rise, which is often not a shoulder problem at all but a breathing problem or a back problem, and they never check whether their correction has stuck. Months go by and the same correction keeps coming.

To avoid that, every fault in this book is set out in the same four steps. We will call them **Check, Cause, Correct and Confirm**.

1. **Check.** Find out exactly what is happening. Use a mirror, a video, a partner's hand or a simple test. Be specific: not "my stance is weak" but "my front knee drifts outwards when I punch with the opposite hand".
2. **Cause.** Work out why. Faults in Sanchin very often start somewhere other than where they show. A knee that drifts out may be caused by the foot rolling, by the hip not being engaged, or by the trunk twisting with the punch. Each has a different fix.
3. **Correct.** Change one thing at a time, using a specific drill. If you change three things at once you will not know which one helped.
4. **Confirm.** Repeat the original check. If the fault has gone, carry on practising the correction until it holds without your attention. If it has not gone, go back to step two.

You will see this loop again and again. It is the backbone of Part Seven, which catalogues the most common faults, and it runs through the testing drills in Part Six. Once you are used to it, you will find yourself using it automatically, in Sanchin and in the rest of your karate.

::: drill
**Drill 1.1 – The Baseline Recording**

*You need:* a phone or camera, a space about three metres long, and good light.

*What to do:* Set the camera at about hip height, side on to the line you will walk along, roughly three metres away. Perform Sanchin once, exactly as you currently do it, without trying to improve anything. Then move the camera so it faces you from the front and perform it again.

*What to look for:* Do not analyse the recording yet. Label the files with today's date and keep them. As you work through Parts Two to Seven, you will return to these recordings to check specific points. In three months, you will record yourself again and compare.

*Why it matters:* You cannot see your own Sanchin while you are doing it. A mirror helps, but it only shows you one angle, and looking at it changes your posture. A recording shows you what your teacher sees.
:::

## How Long Does It Take?

Students often ask how long it takes to learn Sanchin. The honest answer depends on what you mean by "learn".

You can learn the sequence of movements in a few lessons. Most beginners can walk through the kata without prompting by the end of their first month.

Learning to perform it with a reasonable stance, coordinated breathing and moderate, even tension typically takes between six months and two years of regular practice, depending on how often you train and how good your teaching is. This is roughly the point at which a teacher will begin testing the kata with more than light pressure.

Learning to perform it well, so that it holds up under firm testing, the breathing is free, the tension rises and falls smoothly and the face and mind stay calm, usually takes many years. Most experienced teachers would say they are still learning it.

That may sound discouraging. It should not. The point is not to finish Sanchin but to keep improving it, and the improvements are real and noticeable at every stage. A student who practises the kata properly for six months will stand differently, breathe differently and move differently from the way they did before. A student who practises it for ten years will be different again. There is always another layer.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

A useful way to think about your progress is to ask what your teacher is correcting. In the first months, the corrections are about the sequence: which hand, which foot, where to turn. Then they move to the stance: feet, knees, hips. Then to the trunk and shoulders. Then to the breath and the timing. Eventually they become very small and very specific, perhaps a single finger or the angle of the head. If your teacher has stopped correcting your feet and started correcting your breath, you have made progress, even if it does not feel like it.
:::

## How Often to Practise

Sanchin is demanding, but it is short. Even at a moderate level of effort, three to five repetitions are enough to give a good training effect, and a single careful performance can teach you a great deal. For most students, the following pattern works well:

- **In the dojo,** follow your teacher's programme. Most Goju-Ryu classes include Sanchin at least once a week, and many include it in every session.
- **At home,** perform the kata three to five times, three or four days a week, with full attention. On other days, practise individual elements: the stance, the breathing, a single step and punch. Ten minutes of focused work is worth more than an hour of tired repetition.
- **Before a grading or test,** increase the frequency rather than the intensity. Short, frequent practice settles the kata. Long, hard sessions in the last few days mainly make you tired.

Part Eight sets out a full twelve-week programme that combines kata practice with supplementary strength training. You do not need to follow it to benefit from this book, but many students find that a clear structure makes them more consistent.

## A Word About Your Teacher

This book is not a substitute for a teacher, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Sanchin, more than any other kata, is learned through correction by hand. A teacher who presses on your shoulder, taps your knee or adjusts your hip gives you information that no diagram can. They can also see faults you do not know you have, and they can judge how much testing your body is ready for.

What this book can do is help you make better use of your teacher. It gives you vocabulary, so that you understand their corrections more quickly. It gives you drills, so that you can work on those corrections between classes. It gives you a way of checking yourself, so that you arrive at the next class with the fault already improved and can move on to the next one.

If you train without a teacher, perhaps because you live far from a Goju-Ryu dojo or because you are returning to training on your own, the book will still help you. But please do two things. First, take the safety guidance at the front of the book seriously, particularly on breathing and tension. Second, find a qualified teacher to check your Sanchin as soon as you can, even if only occasionally. A single afternoon's instruction can correct faults that would otherwise take months to find.

### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin is a short, slow kata performed in a narrow, inward-gripping stance with deliberate breathing and controlled tension. Its name is usually translated as "three battles" or "three conflicts".
- It is the foundation kata of Goju-Ryu: taught first and practised for life, because the body structure it develops is never finished.
- Sanchin trains five capacities: a rooted stance, a connected trunk, breath control, tension and relaxation, and focus and endurance.
- It sits beneath all the other kata of the style, and its structure appears in all of them. Tensho is its soft partner.
- It is not a strength contest, not just a breathing exercise and not a test of endurance for its own sake.
- Every fault in this book is solved with four steps: Check, Cause, Correct and Confirm. The second and fourth are the ones students skip.
- Short, frequent, focused practice is more effective than long, exhausting sessions.
:::

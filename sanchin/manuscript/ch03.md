# The Three Battles

::: chapter-intro
The name Sanchin promises three battles, and teachers have read that promise in several ways. This chapter looks at the main interpretations and draws from them the working principles you will use throughout the book: rooting, closing, centring, breathing and the rhythm of hard and soft. It ends with the inner battle, the one fought in the mind, which is often the hardest to win.
:::

A student once told me, quite seriously, that she had worked out what the three battles were. The first was with her legs, which wanted to straighten. The second was with her shoulders, which wanted to rise. The third was with her teacher, who kept telling her about the first two. We both laughed, but she was closer to the truth than she knew. Sanchin does feel like a series of struggles, and many of them are between parts of your own body that want to do different things.

The name is an invitation to think, not a definition to memorise. Nobody left behind a written statement of what the three battles were meant to be, and the interpretations that have grown up around the name are best treated as teaching aids. Each one draws attention to something real in the kata. Taken together, they give you a clear picture of what Sanchin is trying to train.

## Three Ways to Read the Name

### Mind, body and breath

The most common reading is that the three battles are fought by, or between, the mind, the body and the breath. In ordinary life these three run more or less independently. You can think about one thing while your body does another and your breathing carries on by itself. In Sanchin they have to work together. The mind directs the body. The breath supports the movement. The body holds the structure that lets the breath work. If any one of the three drifts away, the others suffer.

You will notice this quickly in practice. The moment your attention wanders, your stance softens and your breath shortens. When your breath catches, your shoulders rise and your mind becomes anxious. When your legs begin to burn, your thoughts turn to the pain and your timing falls apart. Sanchin teaches you to bring all three back into line, again and again, until they stay there of their own accord.

### Upper, middle and lower body

A second reading takes the three battles to be the three regions of the body: the upper body, meaning the arms, shoulders and head; the middle body, meaning the trunk; and the lower body, meaning the hips and legs. Each region has its own job. The legs root and drive. The trunk connects and transmits. The arms deliver and defend. The battle is to make the three work as one unit, so that force can travel from the floor to the fist without being lost on the way.

This reading is very practical. When something goes wrong in your Sanchin, it usually goes wrong at a junction between two regions: at the hip, where the legs meet the trunk, or at the shoulder, where the trunk meets the arm. Much of the corrective work in Part Seven is about those junctions.

### Strength, speed and timing, or hardness, softness and change

A third family of readings sees the three battles as three qualities that have to be balanced. Different teachers name them differently: strength, speed and timing; hardness, softness and the change between them; tension, relaxation and breath. What these versions have in common is the idea that Sanchin is not about any single quality but about the relationship between several. A Sanchin that is all strength is rigid. One that is all softness collapses. The art lies in the movement between them.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

When I teach beginners, I use the second reading, upper, middle and lower body, because it gives them something they can see and feel. When I teach more experienced students, I move to the first, mind, body and breath, because by then the physical structure is settled enough that the mind and breath become the limiting factors. The third reading, about the rhythm of hard and soft, I save for students who are already tense enough. Telling a beginner to relax tends to produce a collapsed stance; telling an advanced student to relax often unlocks their whole kata.
:::

## From Interpretation to Principle

Interpretations are useful for understanding, but in training you need something more concrete. The rest of this chapter turns the three readings into five working principles. Each one names something you can feel, check and correct. They will reappear throughout the book.

1. **Rooting**: the connection between your feet and the floor.
2. **Closing**: the bracing of the body into a single connected unit.
3. **Centring**: the organisation of the body around the lower abdomen and pelvis.
4. **Breathing**: the use of the breath to support structure and movement.
5. **Rhythm**: the alternation of hard and soft, tension and release.

## Rooting

Rooting is what makes Sanchin hard to move. It comes from three things working together: the grip of the feet, the bracing of the legs and the sinking of the weight.

The feet grip the floor not by curling the toes, which tends to lift the arches and weaken the contact, but by spreading the weight evenly across three points of each foot: the heel, the ball of the big toe and the ball of the little toe. The toes then press gently down, as if trying to pull the floor towards you. You should feel the arches lift slightly and the whole sole come alive.

The legs brace the stance by drawing the knees inwards and slightly forward while the feet stay planted. This creates a kind of spiral tension running up each leg, from the foot through the knee to the hip. The thighs press towards each other without the knees actually touching. In older descriptions this is sometimes compared to holding a tray between your knees or gripping a horse's flanks while riding.

The weight sinks through the stance because the hips are tucked under and the knees are bent. Instead of hanging forward off the front of the body, as it does when you stand with a hollow back, the weight drops straight down through the pelvis, the legs and the feet into the floor. A stance with good rooting feels heavy and settled, as if your feet had grown into the ground.

Part Two builds the stance in detail. For now, simply notice that rooting is not the same as stiffness. A well-rooted stance can still move, and it must, because Sanchin steps. The root is in the connection, not in the rigidity.

## Closing

The Japanese word *shime* means to tighten, to close or to fasten, and it has two uses in Sanchin. It describes the testing of the kata by a teacher's hands, which is the subject of Part Six. And it describes the state of the body that the testing checks: braced, connected, closed. In this book, when we talk about closing, we mean the second.

Closing the body means bringing its parts together into a single unit. The shoulder blades draw down and slightly together. The large muscles of the back, the *latissimus dorsi*, press down and outwards, connecting the arms to the trunk. The abdomen firms. The buttocks tighten. The thighs press inwards. The armpits close, so that the upper arms feel connected to the sides of the chest. Nothing is loose, and nothing is clenched beyond what it needs.

A closed body behaves very differently from an open one. When a partner pushes on the fist of an open body, the arm folds or the shoulder gives way. When they push on the fist of a closed body, the force travels through the arm, into the back, down through the hips and legs and into the floor. The partner feels as though they are pushing a wall.

The key word is connection. Closing is not the same as tensing every muscle as hard as possible. It is about joining the parts so that force can travel between them. You can close the body at quite moderate effort, and when you are learning, you should.

## Centring

Centring is about the organisation of the body around its middle, the area of the lower abdomen and pelvis that Japanese martial arts call the *tanden* or *hara*. In practical terms, it means three things.

First, the pelvis is set in a neutral to slightly tucked position, so that the lower back is long rather than hollowed. This places the trunk directly over the base of the stance and allows the weight to sink.

Second, the lower abdomen is active. Not sucked in, which raises the diaphragm and shortens the breath, and not pushed out, which loosens the trunk, but firm, as if bracing gently against a belt. Much of the breathing work in Part Three is about learning to keep this area active while still breathing freely.

Third, movement is initiated from the centre. When you step in Sanchin, the step begins with a slight shift of the hips, not with the foot. When you punch, the force begins in the stance and the hips, not in the shoulder. This takes time to feel, but once you have felt it, your Sanchin changes noticeably. The movements look smaller and more powerful at the same time.

Older teachers often describe the centre in more abstract terms, as the seat of the breath or the source of energy. You are welcome to think of it in those ways if it helps you. In this book, I will stick to describing what you can feel and check: the angle of the pelvis, the firmness of the lower abdomen, and the point from which movement starts.

::: test
**Test Yourself: Finding the Centre**

Stand with your feet hip-width apart and your knees slightly bent. Place one hand flat on your lower abdomen, just below the navel, and the other on your lower back.

1. Hollow your back as far as you comfortably can. Notice what happens under both hands.
2. Now tuck your tailbone under as far as you can. Notice again.
3. Find the point between these two where your lower back feels long and your lower abdomen feels gently active under your hand. Breathe in slowly through your nose. You should feel your lower abdomen and the sides of your waist expand slightly into your hands, front and back.

That middle position, with the breath reaching both hands, is the centred posture you will build on in Part Two.
:::

## Breathing

Breathing is the principle most closely associated with Sanchin, and it has a whole part of this book to itself. Here it is enough to set out the role it plays.

In Sanchin, the breath does three jobs. It supports the structure, because a full breath into the lower trunk, held under gentle pressure, stiffens the middle of the body and helps it to transmit force. It sets the rhythm of the kata, because each movement is paired with an inhalation or an exhalation, and the timing of the breath governs the timing of the technique. And it steadies the mind, because a slow, controlled breath is one of the most reliable ways we have of calming ourselves under stress.

The breath must also be safe. Hard exhalation against a narrowed throat raises the pressure in the chest and abdomen, which raises blood pressure. Held breath does the same, more sharply. Neither is necessary to perform Sanchin well, and both can cause dizziness or worse. Chapter 7 sets out a breathing method that keeps the benefits and avoids the risks.

## Rhythm: Hard and Soft

This brings us back to the name of the style. Goju-Ryu is the hard-soft school, and Sanchin is where you learn the rhythm between hard and soft.

Each movement in Sanchin has a phase of preparation, in which the body is relatively soft and the breath comes in; a phase of execution, in which the technique is performed with increasing tension and the breath goes out; and a brief phase of completion, in which the tension peaks and then settles. Then the cycle begins again.

The most common mistake is to stay hard all the time. Students who do this finish the kata exhausted, with a pounding head and aching joints, and their technique tends to be slow and stiff. A muscle that never relaxes cannot contract any further, so their final tension is no stronger than their starting tension. All they have done is spend energy.

The opposite mistake, staying soft all the time, is less common in Sanchin but does happen, especially with students who have been told too often to relax. Their kata looks calm but collapses under the lightest testing.

The aim is a wave. Tension builds smoothly to a peak at the end of each technique, then eases off, never entirely to nothing, as the next breath comes in. Throughout, the structure stays closed. What changes is the intensity. Experienced practitioners describe this as being "soft inside, hard outside", or as a strong body carrying a relaxed mind.

::: fault
**Problem: "My teacher says I am too stiff, but when I relax, my stance collapses."**

*Check.* Ask a partner to push gently on your fist while you hold the block position. First, use full tension. Then relax completely. Then try about half tension. Notice which version holds best.

*Cause.* You are treating tension as a single dial, turned up or down for the whole body. In fact, Sanchin needs different amounts of tension in different places. The legs, buttocks, back and lower abdomen should stay moderately firm all the time. The face, neck, throat, upper shoulders and hands should be relatively relaxed, except at the very end of a technique.

*Correct.* Practise standing in the block position for one minute at a time with your attention on two areas only: firm through the legs and back, soft through the face and the tops of the shoulders. Breathe slowly. Repeat five times. Then add the punch, letting the tension rise in the arm and back only as the punch completes.

*Confirm.* Ask your partner to push again at half tension. If the structure holds and your face and shoulders stay soft, you have found the starting point.
:::

## The Inner Battle

The last of the battles is the one you cannot see. Sanchin is uncomfortable. Your legs burn. Your forearms ache. Your breath feels strange. If you are being tested, someone is pressing on your shoulders or slapping your thighs. Your mind responds to all of this in predictable ways: it wants to escape, to hurry, to tense up, to give up or to fight back.

Learning to stay in that discomfort without reacting is one of the most valuable things Sanchin can teach. Not to ignore the discomfort, which is unhealthy and sometimes dangerous, but to notice it and carry on calmly, with your attention on the task. This is the kind of steadiness people need under pressure in many areas of life, and Sanchin trains it very directly.

Japanese martial arts have several words for related ideas. *Zanshin*, often translated as "remaining mind", describes alert awareness that continues after a technique is finished. *Fudoshin*, "immovable mind", describes a mind that is not shaken by fear, surprise or pain. You do not need to use these terms to benefit from the idea. In practical terms, the inner battle is about three habits:

- **Keep your eyes steady.** Look straight ahead at a fixed point at the height of your own eyes. Do not drop your gaze to the floor when the kata gets hard, and do not glance at the person testing you. The eyes lead the mind.
- **Keep your face calm.** A grimacing face is a sign of tension spreading where it does not belong. Let the jaw rest, the forehead soften and the eyes stay open but relaxed. This will feel unnatural at first.
- **Keep counting your breath.** When the mind starts to panic or wander, bring it back to the breath. In, out. One movement at a time.

Chapter 21 deals with faults of the eyes, face and mind in detail, and offers specific drills for working on them. For now, simply notice them. The next time you practise Sanchin, pay attention not only to what your body is doing but to what your mind is doing. You may be surprised.

::: drill
**Drill 3.1 – The Three-Minute Stand**

*You need:* a quiet space, a timer and a fixed point on the wall at eye height.

*What to do:* Step into your Sanchin stance with your arms in the double block. Set the timer for three minutes. Breathe slowly through your nose in and out through your mouth, at a comfortable pace. Keep moderate tension in your legs, back and lower abdomen; keep your face, throat and the tops of your shoulders soft. Keep your eyes on the fixed point.

*What to look for:* Notice the moment your mind first tries to leave. It may be a thought about something else, an urge to check the timer, a desire to shift your feet or a wave of discomfort in your legs. When it happens, do not judge it. Just bring your attention back to your breath and your fixed point.

*Progression:* Increase to five minutes over a few weeks. Once you can stand calmly for five minutes, add a slow single punch on every fifth breath, without letting the face or shoulders tense.

*Why it matters:* This is the inner battle in its simplest form. It trains the same calm attention the full kata needs, without the complication of moving.
:::

## Putting the Principles Together

The five principles are not separate techniques to be practised one after another. They are aspects of a single state. When you are rooted, closing becomes easier because the floor gives you something to brace against. When you are centred, breathing becomes easier because the lower trunk has room to expand. When your breath is steady, the rhythm of hard and soft comes naturally. And when all of these are working, the mind tends to settle.

The reverse is also true. A fault in one principle almost always shows up as a fault in another. A student who is not rooted will often compensate by tensing the shoulders. One who is not centred will usually breathe high in the chest. One who holds the breath will usually lose the rhythm. This is why the Check and Cause steps of our problem-solving loop matter so much. The place a fault shows up is often not the place it starts.

In Part Two we start building. We will begin, as Sanchin does, with the feet.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The "three battles" can be read as mind, body and breath; as upper, middle and lower body; or as a balance of hard, soft and the change between them. All three readings are useful.
- Five working principles follow from these readings: rooting, closing, centring, breathing and rhythm.
- **Rooting** comes from even weight on three points of each foot, inward-bracing legs and the weight sinking through a tucked pelvis.
- **Closing** (*shime*) joins the body into a connected unit so that force passes from the floor to the fist and back. It is about connection, not maximum tension.
- **Centring** organises the body around a neutral-to-tucked pelvis and an active lower abdomen, and starts movement from the centre.
- **Breathing** supports structure, sets the rhythm and calms the mind. It must never involve holding the breath or bearing down hard.
- **Rhythm** is the wave of hard and soft. Tension builds with each exhalation and eases, but never disappears, with each inhalation.
- The inner battle is the habit of staying calm and attentive in discomfort: steady eyes, a calm face and attention on the breath.
:::

### Training Tasks

1. Choose the reading of the three battles that makes most sense to you at the moment. Write a short paragraph explaining why, and what you think it asks of you in practice.
2. Carry out the Finding the Centre test once a day for a week. Note any change in how easily you find the middle position.
3. With a partner, compare a push on your fist in the block position at full tension, no tension and half tension, as described in the problem box. Record which holds best.
4. Complete Drill 3.1 four times this week. After each attempt, write down the moment your mind first tried to leave and what it was thinking about.
5. Watch the side-on baseline recording you made in Chapter 1. Try to identify where your tension stays flat and where it rises and falls. Sketch your own version of Figure 3.3.
6. Ask yourself which of the five principles is weakest in your current Sanchin. Keep that question in mind as you read Part Two.

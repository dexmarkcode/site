# Faults of the Feet and Legs

::: chapter-intro
This is the first of three chapters that gather the most common Sanchin faults into a working catalogue. Each fault is set out as a problem to solve, using the Check, Cause, Correct and Confirm loop from Chapter 1. This chapter covers the faults that begin at ground level: the length, width and angles of the stance, the contact of the feet, the bend and direction of the knees, the distribution of weight and the faults of stepping.
:::

Throughout the book so far, faults have appeared one at a time, alongside the techniques they spoil. That is the right way to learn them. But when you are training, and something is wrong, you do not always know which chapter to look in. You know only that your stance feels weak, or that your teacher has said your knees are wrong again, or that the push-back test keeps giving way.

The three chapters of Part Seven are designed for that situation. They gather the faults by region of the body and set each one out in the same way, so that you can find it, understand it and fix it. Each chapter begins with a short diagnostic guide to help you identify the likely fault from the symptom you have noticed.

A word of encouragement before you start. Reading a catalogue of faults can be discouraging, especially if you recognise several of them in your own Sanchin. Please do not be discouraged. Every practitioner, at every level, has faults. The difference between a beginner and an expert is not that the expert has none, but that the expert knows what theirs are and is working on them. Finding your faults is the first and most important step.

## How the Faults Are Set Out

Each fault in Part Seven is presented as follows:

- **Looks like.** What you, your teacher or a camera would see.
- **Check.** A specific test or observation that confirms the fault.
- **Causes.** The usual causes, most common first. Remember that the cause is often somewhere other than where the fault shows.
- **Correct.** A specific drill or change, with a reference to where it is explained in full.
- **Confirm.** How to tell whether the correction has worked.

## Faults of Length, Width and Angle

::: fault
**Fault 19.1 – Stance Too Long**

*Looks like.* The front foot is well ahead of the rear toes, so that the stance resembles a short forward stance. The hips often sit back and the front knee bends more than the rear.

*Check.* Look down: the front heel should be roughly level with the rear toes. Or use the floor template from Drill 4.1.

*Causes.* Habit from other stances, particularly forward stance; trying to look strong; stepping with momentum rather than control.

*Correct.* Practise stepping onto the floor template, twenty steps a day for two weeks (Drill 4.1). Practise the crescent step slowly, stopping the moving foot as it passes the standing foot, then placing it deliberately.

*Confirm.* Re-measure on the template. Run the hip push test (Test 13): a stance that is too long is weak sideways, and the test should improve as the stance shortens.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.2 – Stance Too Wide**

*Looks like.* The feet are well beyond shoulder width, so the stance looks like a short horse stance. The knees tend to point forward rather than inward.

*Check.* Measure the distance between the heel lines and compare it with the width of your shoulders.

*Causes.* Trying to feel more stable; confusing sanchin dachi with stances from other kata.

*Correct.* Narrow to shoulder width on the floor template. Practise Drill 5.2, the cushion squeeze, to rediscover the inward bracing that a wide stance loses.

*Confirm.* The knee-out test (Test 15) should improve; the thighs should feel more engaged.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.3 – Stance Too Narrow**

*Looks like.* The feet are almost on a single line, one behind the other, like walking on a tightrope.

*Check.* Ask a partner to give a gentle sideways push to the hip. A narrow stance sways easily.

*Causes.* Over-squeezing the knees inwards, which draws the feet towards the centre line during the step; stepping in a crescent that curves too far inward and does not come back out.

*Correct.* Practise the crescent step with a strip of tape on the floor marking each foot's line. Let the moving foot finish on its own line, not on the centre.

*Confirm.* Repeat the sideways push.
:::

![**Figure 19.2** Stance length and width faults seen from above, compared with the correct stance. From left: too long, too wide, too narrow, correct. The dashed lines show the heel lines and the rear toe line.](figures/fig19_2.png)

::: fault
**Fault 19.4 – Front Foot Not Turned In (or Turned In Too Far)**

*Looks like.* The front foot points straight ahead, or almost so; or it is turned in so far that the toes point across in front of the rear foot.

*Check.* Look down at your front foot; compare its angle with the floor template. Watch the front knee: if the foot is not turned in, the knee tends to drift out; if it is turned in too far, the knee tends to be twisted inward.

*Causes.* Not turning the foot as it lands, then forgetting to adjust; an exaggerated idea of the stance from photographs; ankle or hip stiffness.

*Correct.* Practise turning the toes in as the foot arrives, during the last part of the crescent, not after it lands. If you have stiff hips or ankles, use the lower end of the angle range, about twenty degrees, and follow Chapter 27's advice on adapting the stance.

*Confirm.* Film from above, if possible, or check against the template.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.5 – Rear Foot Turned Out**

*Looks like.* The toes of the rear foot point outward, away from the centre line.

*Check.* Look down, or check the template.

*Causes.* Habit from other stances; pushing off the rear foot during the step and letting it swivel; stiffness in the hip.

*Correct.* As the stance sets, check that the rear toes point forward or slightly in. If the foot swivels out during the step, practise the step with extra attention on keeping the rear foot gripping until the moving foot has landed.

*Confirm.* The rear knee should now track over the rear toes, and the knee-out test on the rear leg should improve.
:::

## Faults of Contact

::: fault
**Fault 19.6 – Toes Curling**

*Looks like.* The toes bunch up, the tops of the toe joints rise into little hills and the balls of the feet may lift.

*Check.* The Coin Test from Chapter 4: a coin slides easily under the pads of the toes.

*Causes.* A misunderstanding of "grip the floor"; weak foot muscles that compensate by clenching.

*Correct.* Practise Drill 4.2, foot wake-up, daily. In the stance, think of pressing the toe pads down and spreading the toes, not curling them.

*Confirm.* Repeat the Coin Test. Calf cramps during the kata should also become less frequent.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.7 – Weight on the Outer Edges, or Collapsed Arches**

*Looks like.* With weight on the outer edges, the ankles roll out and the soles tilt so that the inside edges lift. With collapsed arches, the ankles roll in and the inner edges of the feet flatten onto the floor.

*Check.* Look at your ankles from behind, using a camera: they should be vertical above the heels. Or use the Coin Test on the ball of the big toe (for outer-edge weight) and the inner arch (for collapse).

*Causes.* Outer-edge weight usually goes with knees drifting out; collapsed arches with knees collapsing in. Both can come from weak foot muscles or from long-standing habits of standing and walking.

*Correct.* For outer-edge weight: press through the ball of the big toe and draw the knee gently inward over the second toe. For collapsed arches: perform the short-foot exercise from Drill 4.2, lifting the arch, and let the knee move slightly out until it is over the second or third toe.

*Confirm.* Ankles vertical on camera; Coin Test passes at all three points.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.8 – Rear Heel Lifting**

*Looks like.* The heel of the rear foot comes off the floor, usually during the punch or as the student leans forward.

*Check.* Film from the side during several punches. Or ask a partner to watch the rear heel only.

*Causes.* Driving the punch forward instead of pressing the rear foot into the floor; leaning forward; a stance that is too long; a rear knee that is over-bent.

*Correct.* Practise punches with your attention on pressing the rear heel down as the punch arrives. Check your stance length (Fault 19.1) and posture (the stick test, Drill 6.1). Practise the punch push-back test (Test 8), which will tend to drive the heel down if the force is going to the right place.

*Confirm.* Film again; the heel should stay down through all the punches.
:::

## Faults of the Knees

::: fault
**Fault 19.9 – Knees Too Straight**

*Looks like.* The legs are almost straight, the hips high and the stance upright and stiff.

*Check.* The Spring Test from Chapter 5: you cannot bounce without first bending your knees.

*Causes.* Habit from high stances; tall students who find a bent-knee stance tiring; weak thighs.

*Correct.* Lower the stance until you can perform the Spring Test. Build thigh strength with the wall sit and other exercises in Drill 5.3.

*Confirm.* Repeat the Spring Test; run the shoulder press test (Test 2), which should now be absorbed by the legs rather than jarring the knees.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.10 – Stance Too Deep**

*Looks like.* The student sits low, thighs working hard, knees well forward. The stance looks strong but steps are heavy and the posture deteriorates as the kata goes on.

*Check.* The Spring Test: you cannot bounce without first rising.

*Causes.* Belief that lower is stronger; trying to look impressive; habit from horse stance or other low stances.

*Correct.* Raise the stance by a few centimetres. Practise the whole kata at the higher stance for a week and note how much easier the steps and turns become.

*Confirm.* Posture should hold better at the end of the kata. Knee discomfort, if any, should ease.
:::

![**Figure 19.3** Leg faults seen from the side. From left: knees too straight, stance too deep, rear heel lifting, and the correct stance for comparison.](figures/fig19_3.png)

::: fault
**Fault 19.11 – Knees Drifting Out**

*Looks like.* The knees point outward, away from the centre, and the groin opens. The feet may roll onto their outer edges.

*Check.* The knee-out test (Test 15); or watch from the front and draw an imaginary line from hip to knee to foot.

*Causes.* Inner thighs not engaged; feet not turned in enough; weak hip muscles; loss of attention during steps and turns.

*Correct.* Drill 5.2, the cushion squeeze; Drill 5.1, knee tracking. Check the front foot angle (Fault 19.4).

*Confirm.* Repeat the knee-out test and film from the front.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.12 – Knees Collapsing In**

*Looks like.* The knees fall inside the line of the feet, sometimes almost touching. The arches collapse and the knees may be twisted.

*Check.* Watch from the front during a slow bend and straighten; the kneecaps move inward past the second toe. The knee-in test (Test 15) also shows it.

*Causes.* Over-interpreting "knees in"; weak muscles at the side of the hip; collapsed arches.

*Correct.* Reset the knees over the second or third toe. Strengthen the side of the hip with the side-lying leg lift in Drill 5.3. Lift the arches with the short-foot exercise. Remember: the squeeze comes from the thighs, not from collapsing the knees.

*Confirm.* Knee tracking on film; any knee discomfort should ease. If knee pain persists, see a physiotherapist.
:::

## Faults of Weight and Stepping

::: fault
**Fault 19.13 – Uneven Weight**

*Looks like.* Most of the weight is on one foot, usually the front. The student rocks back when pushed from the front, or forward when pushed from behind, much more easily in one direction than the other.

*Check.* Two bathroom scales, one under each foot. Or the front push and back push tests.

*Causes.* Leaning forward with the upper body; a stance that is too long; anticipation of the punch.

*Correct.* Stand on the scales and adjust until the readings are within a few kilograms of each other. Memorise the feeling. Check the upper body with the stick test.

*Confirm.* Front and back push tests should feel equally solid.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.14 – Stance Drifting During the Kata**

*Looks like.* The stance is correct at the start of the kata but gradually changes: steps get shorter, feet narrower, knees straighter. By the end the student is in a different stance.

*Check.* Film the kata from the side and from above if possible. Compare the stance at Frame 6 with the stance at Frame 31 and Frame 41.

*Causes.* Fatigue; loss of attention; the student "feeling" the stance rather than checking it.

*Correct.* Build endurance with the strength work in Part Eight. In practice, use the single-check method from Chapter 6, attending only to the stance for the whole kata. Use the "Where did you finish?" test from Chapter 12 to catch drift in step length.

*Confirm.* Compare the stance at the same frames on film after four weeks.
:::

::: fault
**Fault 19.15 – Stepping Faults**

*Looks like.* The moving foot travels in a straight line instead of a crescent; lifts off the floor and is stamped down; or the head bobs during the step.

*Check.* Film from the front for the path, from the side for lifting and bobbing.

*Causes.* Not understanding the crescent; rushing; straightening the standing leg as the feet pass.

*Correct.* Practise the crescent step slowly on the floor template (Chapter 4). Slide the foot so that it brushes the floor. Practise with the tape at head height on a mirror, and with a partner's hand on your head (Drill 11.2).

*Confirm.* Film again.
:::

## Working Through the List

You will probably recognise more than one fault in this chapter. That is normal. Do not try to fix them all at once. Instead, use this method.

1. **List your faults.** Use your recordings, your testing journal and your teacher's corrections. Write down every lower-body fault you think you have.
2. **Find the root.** Look for the fault lowest down in the body, or the one most likely to cause the others. Foot and stance-length faults usually come first; knee faults often follow from them.
3. **Fix one at a time.** Work on the root fault for two to four weeks, using its correction drill daily.
4. **Re-check the list.** When the root fault is fixed, check the others. Some will have disappeared on their own.
5. **Move to the next.** Choose the next root fault and repeat.

This is slow, but it works. Students who try to fix five faults at once usually fix none of them. Students who fix one fault at a time find, a few months later, that their stance has transformed.

::: drill
**Drill 19.1 – Lower-Body Audit**

*You need:* your recordings from the side, the front and, if possible, above; a partner for the tests; your testing journal.

*What to do:* Work through Faults 19.1 to 19.15 in order. For each, carry out the Check and write down "yes", "no" or "sometimes". Then circle the lowest-numbered "yes". That is your starting point.

*Why it matters:* A systematic audit replaces a vague sense that "my stance is weak" with a specific, prioritised list. It is the Check step of the loop, carried out for the whole lower body at once.
:::

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Part Seven gathers the most common faults by body region, each set out as looks like, check, causes, correct and confirm.
- Faults of the feet and legs fall into four groups: length, width and angle; contact; knees; weight and stepping.
- Many lower-body faults are linked. Stance length and foot angle faults often cause knee faults; weak foot muscles often cause contact faults.
- Work on one root fault at a time, for two to four weeks, then re-check the others.
- Use the lower-body audit to build a prioritised list.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Carry out Drill 19.1, the lower-body audit, and identify your starting fault.
2. Work on that fault daily for two weeks, using its correction drill. Record progress in your testing journal.
3. After two weeks, repeat the relevant Check and the whole audit.
4. Ask your teacher which lower-body fault they see most often in your Sanchin. Compare their answer with your audit.
5. Help a training partner carry out their own audit.
:::

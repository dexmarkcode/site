# Breathing in Sanchin

::: chapter-intro
The breath is the most recognisable part of Sanchin and the part most often done badly. This chapter explains what happens in your body when you breathe, how Sanchin breathing differs from ordinary breathing, the two traditional breathing methods known as ibuki and nogare, and a five-stage progression that will take you from natural breathing to a full Sanchin breath safely. It also deals frankly with the risks of forceful breathing and how to avoid them.
:::

Most people who watch Sanchin for the first time remember the sound. The long, rasping exhalations, sometimes accompanied by a visible tightening of the whole body, are unlike anything else in karate. They can sound dramatic, even alarming, and it is easy to assume that the louder and harder the breath, the better the kata.

That assumption has caused a good deal of harm over the years. It has led students to hold their breath, to strain against a closed throat and to finish the kata dizzy, red-faced and sometimes with a pounding headache. It has also led them to neglect the parts of the breath that matter more: where it goes in the body, how it supports the posture and how it is timed with the movement.

This chapter tries to put the breath back in its proper place. It is important, but its importance lies in its quality, not its volume.

## What Happens When You Breathe

It helps to know a little about the mechanics. You do not need to become an anatomist, but a basic picture will make the instructions in the rest of the chapter much easier to follow.

### The diaphragm

Your main breathing muscle is the diaphragm, a dome-shaped sheet of muscle that sits under your lungs and separates your chest from your abdomen. When you breathe in, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards. This creates more room in the chest, and air flows into the lungs. As the diaphragm moves down, it pushes gently on the contents of the abdomen, which have to go somewhere: the belly moves out slightly at the front, the sides and the back.

When you breathe out normally, the diaphragm relaxes and rises back into its dome, and air flows out. When you breathe out forcefully, the muscles of the abdomen and the ribs help by squeezing the trunk.

### Chest breathing and belly breathing

Most adults, most of the time, breathe partly with the diaphragm and partly by lifting the ribcage with muscles in the neck and upper chest. When stressed or tired, many shift almost entirely to this upper-chest breathing. It is quick and shallow, it lifts the shoulders, and it creates tension in the neck. It is the breathing of anxiety.

Sanchin trains the opposite habit: breathing low, with the diaphragm doing most of the work, so that the expansion is felt in the lower trunk, around the belt, rather than in the upper chest. This is often called abdominal or belly breathing, though as we will see, in Sanchin the expansion should be felt all the way round the trunk, not just at the front.

### Pressure in the trunk

When the abdominal muscles firm around a full breath, the pressure inside the abdomen rises. This pressure acts like air in a tyre: it stiffens the trunk and helps to protect the spine under load. Weightlifters use it deliberately when they lift heavy weights, and Sanchin uses it too. It is one reason why a student with good breathing can resist a push to the trunk much better than one who breathes shallowly.

But pressure has a cost. When it rises sharply, especially if the throat is closed so that no air can escape, the pressure in the chest rises too. This squeezes the large veins returning blood to the heart and, for a moment, the heart has less blood to pump. Blood pressure in the arteries can rise steeply, and then fall. This is known as the Valsalva manoeuvre, and it is the main reason for the dizziness, headaches and occasional fainting that badly performed Sanchin can cause.

## The Sanchin Breath

With that background, here is a description of the Sanchin breath as taught in this book. Your own teacher may describe it differently, and associations differ in emphasis, but the principles below are widely shared.

### The in-breath

Breathe in through your nose, quietly and steadily. The breath should go low: the first movement you feel is a gentle widening around your belt line, at the front, at the sides and at the back. The upper chest and shoulders stay still or nearly still. The in-breath is relatively soft; the trunk remains toned, but it is allowed to expand.

A useful image is that you are filling a barrel from the bottom up, and that the barrel widens in every direction as it fills. Another is that your belt is being gently stretched all the way round, not only at the buckle.

The in-breath does not need to be huge. Filling your lungs to the very top pulls the breath into the upper chest and lifts the shoulders. Aim for a full, comfortable breath, perhaps three-quarters of what you could take if you tried.

### The out-breath

Breathe out through your mouth, slowly and steadily, with the throat partly narrowed so that the breath makes a sound. The sound is often written as "haa", "hoo" or "huu", and it varies between teachers. It should be audible but not forced: a strong sigh, not a roar.

As you breathe out, the trunk firms. The abdominal muscles tighten around the breath, the pelvis stays tucked, the back stays wide. This is where the tension of the technique builds. By the end of the out-breath, the body is at the peak of its tension for that movement.

Crucially, **air keeps moving throughout the out-breath**. The throat is narrowed, not closed. You are making a controlled exhalation against light resistance, not pushing against a locked door. And you do not empty your lungs completely. Stop the out-breath with a little air still in reserve, so that the next in-breath can begin smoothly.

### The pause

Between the end of one out-breath and the start of the next in-breath there may be a brief pause, a fraction of a second, in which the body settles. It is not a held breath. It is the moment when tension eases from its peak to its baseline before the next cycle begins.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

A simple test of whether your out-breath is safe is to try to hum during it. If you can switch from your "haa" to a hum and back again without stopping, air is moving and your throat is open enough. If you cannot, or if the hum sounds strangled, you are closing your throat too far. Some teachers ask students to exhale with the mouth in a small "o" shape for exactly this reason: it keeps the throat from closing.
:::

## Ibuki and Nogare

Goju-Ryu teachers often describe two kinds of breathing, *ibuki* and *nogare*. The terms are used slightly differently by different teachers, but the general distinction is clear.

**Ibuki** is the strong, audible breath most people associate with Sanchin. The exhalation is forceful, made against a narrowed throat, and accompanied by strong tension in the trunk. It is the "hard" breath. It trains the muscles of the trunk, builds the ability to hold structure under pressure and, in application, is associated with the moment of absorbing or delivering a strike.

**Nogare** is the soft, quiet breath. The exhalation is smooth and nearly silent, made through a relaxed throat, with moderate tension. It is the "soft" breath. It is associated with recovery, with movement and with keeping the breathing steady during prolonged effort. Some teachers describe it as the breath you would use in a real fight, where loud, forceful breathing would tire you quickly and tell your opponent exactly when you were about to strike.

In practice, Sanchin training uses both. Many teachers ask beginners to use something close to nogare for months or even years before introducing the full ibuki breath, so that the posture and the timing are established first. Others use ibuki from the start but at low intensity. The Goju-kai lineage in Japan is particularly known for strong ibuki breathing in Sanchin, while some Okinawan lines use a quieter, less forceful breath. Neither is wrong. They reflect different emphases.

Tensho, the soft partner kata discussed in Chapter 25, gives more room to nogare. Some teachers explain the pair of kata as ibuki and nogare made into forms: Sanchin for the hard breath, Tensho for the soft.

## The Breath and the Movement

In Sanchin, every movement is paired with a breath. The general rule is simple: **breathe in during preparation, breathe out during execution**.

- When the hand draws back to the hip before a punch, you breathe in.
- As the punch drives forward and tightens, you breathe out.
- As the arm returns to the block position, you may breathe in, or in some lines you may breathe out again; follow your teacher.
- When you step, the in-breath usually comes during the step and the out-breath as you settle into the new stance or perform the next technique.

The timing matters more than the volume. The out-breath should finish exactly as the technique reaches its peak. If the breath runs out before the punch arrives, the end of the technique has no support. If the punch arrives before the breath has finished, the tension and the breath are out of step, and the trunk is not braced at the moment of greatest effort.

Part Four gives the breath for every frame of the kata. Chapter 9 looks in more detail at rhythm and timing. For now, practise the principle on a single technique, as in the drill below.

![**Figure 7.2** Breath and movement through one punch-and-block cycle. The in-breath (shaded) accompanies the pulling hand; the out-breath accompanies the punch, ending as the punch reaches full tension; a second, shorter exchange accompanies the return to the block.](figures/fig7_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill 7.1 – One Punch, One Breath**

*You need:* a mirror and a quiet room.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your arms in the double block. Draw your left hand back to your hip as you breathe in through your nose, slowly, for a count of three. Then punch slowly forward as you breathe out through your mouth for a count of five, timing it so that the breath ends just as the punch arrives and tightens. Hold the punch for a moment, letting the tension ease very slightly. Return the arm to the block. Repeat with the right hand.

*What to look for:* In the mirror, check that your shoulders stay down and your chest does not lift on the in-breath. Listen to the out-breath: it should be steady from start to finish, not loud at the beginning and fading at the end.

*Progression:* Ten repetitions each side, three times a week, for two weeks. Then extend the out-breath to a count of seven without letting the punch become jerky.
:::

## Breathing All the Way Round

We have mentioned several times that the Sanchin breath should expand the trunk all the way round, not only at the front. This is worth practising on its own, because it is the key to keeping the posture while breathing.

If the breath goes only into the front of the belly, the abdomen pushes forward, the lower back tends to arch, and the pelvis loses its tuck. The posture you built in Part Two is undone by every in-breath. If the breath goes all the way round, the abdomen widens at the sides and the back as well as the front, and the pelvis and lower back can stay where they are.

Many people find the back and sides of the breath difficult to feel at first. The following drill helps.

::: drill
**Drill 7.2 – Belt Breathing**

*You need:* your hands, or a belt tied snugly around your waist at the level of your navel.

*What to do:* Stand in a natural stance. Place your hands on your sides, just above the hip bones, thumbs towards the back and fingers towards the front. Breathe in slowly through your nose and try to push your hands apart with your breath, sideways. Breathe out and feel them come back. Ten breaths.

Then move your hands to the back, either side of your spine just above the belt, and breathe into them. This is harder. Ten breaths.

Finally, tie a belt snugly around your waist and breathe so that you feel it tighten evenly all the way round, not just at the front. Ten breaths.

*Why it matters:* Breathing into the sides and back lets the trunk expand without disturbing the pelvis. It also creates a more even pressure around the spine.
:::

## Breathing Safely

We now come to the part of this chapter that matters most. Sanchin breathing, done with strong ibuki, raises blood pressure. For most healthy adults, a brief rise during exercise is harmless; the same happens when lifting a heavy box or climbing a steep flight of stairs. But a very sharp rise, repeated many times, can be dangerous for some people, and for everyone it is unnecessary.

The following rules keep the breath safe. They do not reduce its benefit.

1. **Never hold your breath.** Air should always be moving, in or out, except for the brief natural pause between breaths. If you notice yourself holding, stop and breathe normally.
2. **Never close your throat completely.** Narrow it to make the sound, but keep air flowing. Use the hum test from the Teacher's Note.
3. **Do not empty your lungs completely.** Finish each out-breath with some air in reserve.
4. **Keep your face soft.** If your face is turning red, your eyes are bulging or you can feel your pulse in your head, you are working too hard. Ease off at once.
5. **Build intensity gradually.** Use the five-stage progression below. Do not jump straight to full ibuki.
6. **Know your health.** If you have any of the conditions listed in Before You Begin at the front of the book, speak to your doctor before practising forceful breathing, and use natural breathing with moderate tension until you have advice.
7. **Stop if you feel dizzy, light-headed or develop a sudden headache.** Kneel or sit, breathe normally, and do not stand up quickly.

::: fault
**Problem: "I see stars at the end of the kata."**

*Stop first.* Seeing stars, greying of the vision or feeling faint are signs that your blood pressure has dropped sharply after a period of strain. Sit or kneel at once and breathe normally. Do not continue that session.

*Cause.* Almost always breath-holding or near-breath-holding during the out-breaths, often combined with very strong tension and a long kata. The problem is usually worst at the end of the kata, or immediately after it, when the strain stops and blood pressure falls.

*Correct.* Return to Stage 2 of the progression below for at least two weeks. Breathe out through a soft "haa" with the mouth slightly open. Use the hum test on every out-breath. Reduce your tension to about half. Finish the kata slowly, and stand still, breathing normally, for half a minute before moving off.

*Confirm.* If the problem recurs at the lower intensity, see your doctor before continuing to train Sanchin breathing.
:::

## The Five-Stage Progression

The following progression takes you from ordinary breathing to a full Sanchin breath. Spend at least a week at each stage, and longer if you need it. Do not move on until the current stage feels easy and natural. If at any point you feel dizzy or uncomfortable, go back a stage.

**Stage 1: Natural breathing.** Perform Sanchin with your ordinary breath, in and out through the nose, not coordinated with the movements. Attend only to the posture. This is the right stage for complete beginners, for children and for anyone advised to avoid forceful breathing.

**Stage 2: Low, quiet breathing.** Breathe in through the nose and out through the mouth, low and all the way round, with a soft, almost silent out-breath. Coordinate the breath with the movement: in on preparation, out on execution. Use light to moderate tension. This is essentially nogare.

**Stage 3: Audible breathing.** Keep the same pattern, but narrow the throat slightly on the out-breath so that it makes a quiet "haa". Moderate tension, building towards the end of each out-breath. Use the hum test.

**Stage 4: Firm breathing.** Lengthen and strengthen the out-breath, with firmer tension in the trunk at the end of each technique. The sound becomes stronger but should still be steady and never strangled. Keep the face soft.

**Stage 5: Full Sanchin breath.** The full ibuki breath, as your teacher teaches it, with strong tension at the peak of each technique and a steady, audible out-breath. Even at this stage, the throat stays partly open and air keeps moving. Most practitioners use Stage 5 for a small part of their practice and spend most of their time at Stages 3 and 4.

![**Figure 7.4** The five-stage breathing progression. Spend at least a week at each stage; move back a stage if you feel dizzy or strained. Most experienced practitioners do most of their training at Stages 3 and 4.](figures/fig7_4.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: Which Stage Are You At?**

Perform your Sanchin once at your normal intensity, then answer these questions honestly.

1. Did you hold your breath at any point?
2. Could you have hummed during your out-breaths?
3. Did your shoulders rise on the in-breaths?
4. Did your face redden or your vision change at any point?
5. Did each out-breath finish as the technique finished?

If you answered yes to question 1 or 4, go back to Stage 2. If you answered yes to question 3, practise Drill 7.2 until the shoulders stay down, then return to your current stage. If you answered no to question 2 or 5, stay at your current stage until both are yes.
:::

## Common Breathing Faults

The following faults are the ones I see most often. Part Seven deals with breathing faults in more detail in Chapter 21.

**Breathing too high.** The chest and shoulders rise on the in-breath. Usually a habit carried over from everyday life. Correct with Drill 7.2 and the lying-down practice in the problem box in Chapter 6.

**Noisy in-breath.** Some students sniff loudly or gasp on the in-breath. The in-breath should be quiet. A noisy in-breath usually means it is too fast or too large. Slow it down and make it smaller.

**Running out of breath.** The out-breath finishes before the technique does. Either the out-breath is too forceful at the start, emptying the lungs too quickly, or the in-breath was too small. Practise a steady out-breath at even volume from start to finish, and take a slightly fuller in-breath.

**Breath and movement out of step.** The breath and the technique start or finish at different times. Practise Drill 7.1 slowly, counting aloud in your head, until the two are synchronised.

**Collapsing on the in-breath.** The posture softens as the student breathes in, so that the stance sags, the pelvis tilts and the shoulders round. Keep a baseline of tension in the legs, buttocks and back throughout, and breathe into the sides and back rather than only the front.

**Theatrical breathing.** The breath is exaggerated for effect, often with dramatic sounds and shaking. In the dojo, the breath should be functional, not theatrical. If you are performing for a demonstration, that is a different matter, but do not let the performance version become your training version.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The diaphragm is the main breathing muscle. Sanchin trains low breathing that expands the lower trunk at the front, sides and back.
- Pressure in the trunk stiffens it and protects the spine, but a sharp rise with a closed throat raises blood pressure and can cause dizziness or worse.
- The Sanchin in-breath is quiet, through the nose, low and all the way round. The out-breath is through the mouth, steady and audible, against a narrowed but never closed throat, ending with some air in reserve.
- *Ibuki* is the strong, hard breath; *nogare* is the soft, quiet breath. Training uses both.
- Breathe in during preparation and out during execution. The out-breath ends as the technique reaches its peak.
- Never hold the breath, never close the throat completely and stop at once if you feel dizzy or develop a sudden headache.
- Build up through five stages from natural breathing to the full Sanchin breath, spending at least a week at each.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Lie on your back with one hand on your chest and one on your abdomen, and practise low breathing for five minutes a day for a week.
2. Practise Drill 7.2 daily for a week, paying particular attention to breathing into your back.
3. Carry out the Which Stage Are You At? test and decide which stage you will train at for the next two weeks.
4. Practise Drill 7.1 three times a week. In the last session, record yourself and listen back. Is the out-breath steady from start to finish?
5. Use the hum test on every out-breath of one complete kata. Note any movements where you could not hum.
6. Ask your teacher whether your dojo emphasises ibuki or nogare in Sanchin, and at what stage students are expected to use the full breath.
:::

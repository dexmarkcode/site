# Dynamic Tension Without Damage

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is practised under muscular tension, and that tension is one of its great training tools. Used badly, it is also the source of most Sanchin injuries. This chapter explains what tension is, how to grade it on a simple scale, where it belongs in the body and where it does not, how to sequence it from the ground up and how to build it over weeks and months without straining your joints, tendons or circulation.
:::

There is a particular look that students get when they first discover tension in Sanchin. Their face reddens, their neck cords stand out, their fists turn white and their whole body begins to tremble. They look, and feel, as though they are working tremendously hard. Some of them come off the floor convinced they have done the best Sanchin of their lives.

Usually they have done one of the worst. All that visible strain is tension in the wrong places: the face, the neck, the tops of the shoulders, the jaw. Meanwhile the places that should be working, the legs, the buttocks, the back and the deep muscles of the trunk, are often doing less than they should, because the student's attention and effort have gone elsewhere. The trembling is not a sign of strength. It is a sign that muscles are fighting each other.

Good tension in Sanchin looks rather undramatic. The body is clearly firm, the techniques clearly strong, but the face is calm, the shoulders are low and nothing is shaking. Learning to produce that kind of tension is one of the main tasks of the kata.

## What Tension Is

When a muscle contracts, it pulls. If the pull moves a joint, as when you bend your elbow to lift a cup, the contraction produces movement. If the pull is matched by an equal pull from the other side, or by an immovable object, nothing moves, but the muscle is still working hard. This is called an isometric contraction. Much of the tension in Sanchin is of this kind.

In Sanchin we also use what is called co-contraction: muscles on both sides of a joint contracting at the same time. When you hold your arm in the block and tense it, the muscles that bend the elbow and the muscles that straighten it are both working. The arm becomes stiff and stable, like a mast held by stays on every side.

Co-contraction is useful because it makes the body resistant to outside force. It is the reason a well-tensed Sanchin can absorb a push or a strike. But it is also expensive. It burns energy quickly, it restricts movement and, if overdone, it puts heavy compressive load on the joints. A joint squeezed from both sides at maximum effort is under a great deal of pressure. Do that repeatedly, for years, and the joints may complain.

## The Tension Scale

To work with tension intelligently, you need some way of describing how much you are using. This book uses a simple scale from zero to ten.

- **0** is complete relaxation, as if lying asleep.
- **1–2** is the light tone of ordinary standing, or of a relaxed arm held in front of you.
- **3–4** is gentle, steady firmness: enough that someone pressing on the muscle would feel it resist, but easy to hold for minutes.
- **5–6** is moderate effort, the level of carrying a heavy shopping bag for a short distance.
- **7–8** is strong effort, which you could hold for a few seconds at a time.
- **9–10** is maximum effort, which you can hold only very briefly and which cannot be repeated many times without a long rest.

The scale is subjective, and your seven may not be the same as someone else's. That does not matter. What matters is that you use it consistently for yourself, so that you can say "I did that kata at a five" and know what you mean.

::: drill
**Drill 8.1 – The Tension Dial**

*You need:* nothing but your own body and a mirror.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your arms in the double block. Perform one slow punch at each of the following levels, with a full breath for each: 2, 4, 6, 8. Rest for three breaths between each punch. Then perform one punch at 5.

*What to look for:* Watch your face and shoulders in the mirror. At what level do they begin to tense? For most beginners, the face and shoulders start to join in somewhere around 5 or 6. That level is your current "spill point", the point beyond which tension spreads to places it should not go.

*Progression:* Over the next few weeks, aim to raise your spill point by one level. Practise punches at one level below it, keeping the face and shoulders soft, until that level feels easy. Then move up.

*Why it matters:* The spill point is one of the most useful numbers in your Sanchin training. It tells you how much tension you can currently use well.
:::

## Where Tension Belongs

Tension in Sanchin is not uniform. Different parts of the body need different amounts at different times. Broadly, the body divides into three zones.

### The baseline zone

The feet, legs, buttocks, pelvic floor, lower abdomen and the large muscles of the back hold a steady baseline of tension throughout the kata, around three or four on our scale. This is what keeps the stance rooted, the pelvis tucked and the trunk connected. The baseline does not drop to zero during the in-breath or between techniques. If it does, the structure collapses and has to be rebuilt each time, which is slow and inefficient.

### The peak zone

The arms, the latissimus muscles, the chest, the sides of the trunk and the abdomen build from the baseline up to a peak at the end of each technique, typically between five and eight on our scale. At the peak, the whole body is firm, but the greatest increase is in the muscles directly involved in the technique: for a punch, the arm, the shoulder blade muscles and the trunk on that side. After the peak, these muscles ease back towards the baseline as the next in-breath begins.

### The soft zone

The face, jaw, throat, the front and sides of the neck, the tops of the shoulders and, between techniques, the hands remain relatively soft throughout, at one or two on our scale. Even at the peak of a technique, they should not exceed about three or four. This is the hardest part of Sanchin tension to achieve, because these are the muscles that most readily join in when we exert ourselves. But it is essential. Tension in the soft zone does nothing to strengthen the structure, wastes energy, disturbs the breath and, in the neck and throat, contributes to the rise in blood pressure.

::: fault
**Problem: "I shake when I tense."**

*Check.* Notice where the shaking starts. Is it in the arms, the legs, the whole body? Does it start at a particular level on the tension scale?

*Cause.* Trembling under tension usually has one of three causes. The most common is simply working above your current capacity: you are asking for more tension than the muscles can hold steadily. The second is fatigue: the shaking starts late in the kata, not at the beginning. The third is co-contraction that is badly balanced, with opposing muscles fighting rather than cooperating; this often shows in the arms.

*Correct.* Drop your peak tension by two levels on the scale. If the shaking stops, you have found the cause. Train at that level for two to three weeks, then raise it by one level. If the shaking only appears late in the kata, the answer is endurance: see Part Eight. If it persists even at low levels in the arms, check your block position (Chapter 13); a forearm held at an awkward angle often makes the muscles fight.

*Confirm.* A steady, unshaking peak at a moderate level is far more valuable than a trembling peak at a high one.
:::

## Sequencing Tension From the Ground Up

Tension in Sanchin does not switch on everywhere at once. It builds in a sequence, from the ground upwards and from the centre outwards. Learning this sequence is what turns tension from a static squeeze into the dynamic, connected force that teachers talk about.

The sequence for a punch, for example, runs roughly as follows:

1. The feet press into the floor, the rear foot slightly more.
2. The legs brace, the thighs pressing inwards.
3. The pelvis firms, the buttocks and lower abdomen engaging.
4. The trunk firms around the out-breath, the abdomen and the sides.
5. The back spreads, the latissimus muscles drawing the shoulder blade down and connecting the arm.
6. The arm extends and firms, the elbow straightening without locking.
7. The fist closes fully at the very end, the last two fingers tightening first.

All seven happen within the space of a single out-breath, so they overlap, and to an outside observer they look almost simultaneous. But the order matters. If the fist tightens first, the tension tends to stay in the arm and shoulder, and the back and legs never properly engage. If the feet and legs start first, the tension travels upwards and arrives at the fist with the whole body behind it.

![**Figure 8.3** Sequencing tension for a punch, from the ground up. The numbers show the order in which the regions engage during a single out-breath. In practice they overlap, but the order matters: tension that starts in the fist stays in the arm.](figures/fig8_3.png)

::: drill
**Drill 8.2 – Slow-Motion Sequence**

*You need:* a partner, if possible.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your left hand at the hip, ready to punch. On a very long out-breath, about ten seconds, perform a single punch while consciously switching on each of the seven stages in order, one or two seconds apart: feet, legs, pelvis, trunk, back, arm, fist. If you have a partner, ask them to place a hand lightly on each region in turn, from the rear foot upwards, so that you have something to focus on.

*What to look for:* At each stage, the new region should engage without the earlier ones letting go. By the end of the punch, everything from the feet to the fist should be firm, and the face and neck should still be soft.

*Progression:* Practise five punches on each side, three times a week. Over several weeks, gradually shorten the out-breath to the normal kata length, keeping the order intact.

*Why it matters:* This drill makes the sequence conscious so that, eventually, it can become automatic. Most students who practise it for a month report that their punches in the kata feel noticeably more connected.
:::

## The Fist

A short word about the fist, because it is the place where many students' tension goes badly wrong.

A Sanchin fist is firm but not clenched to the point of strain. The fingers roll tightly into the palm, and the thumb locks across the first two fingers. The strongest grip is in the little and ring fingers, which connect, through the muscles of the forearm, to the large muscles of the back. The index finger and thumb are firm but less tense. This matters because a fist gripped hardest at the index finger and thumb tends to send tension up the front of the arm into the shoulder, while a fist gripped hardest at the little finger tends to send it along the underside of the arm into the back, where we want it.

Between techniques, the fist can soften slightly. It should not open, but it does not need to be at peak grip throughout the kata. Holding a maximum grip for three minutes will exhaust the forearm, may cause tendon irritation at the elbow and contributes to the spread of tension into the shoulders.

## Joints, Tendons and Circulation

Three kinds of damage can come from badly managed tension. It is worth knowing about each, because the warning signs are different.

### Joints

Strong co-contraction compresses joints. In Sanchin, the joints most at risk are the knees, the elbows and the lower back. The knees are at risk if they are also out of alignment, as discussed in Chapter 5. The elbows are at risk if they are locked straight under tension at the end of a punch; always keep a very slight bend. The lower back is at risk if the pelvis is tilted forward, so that the compressive load falls on an arched spine.

The warning sign is joint pain: an ache or sharp pain in the joint itself, as opposed to the burning in the muscles around it. Joint pain during or after Sanchin should never be trained through. Reduce the intensity, check the alignment and, if it persists, get it looked at.

### Tendons

Tendons connect muscles to bones, and they adapt to load more slowly than muscles do. A student who increases their tension rapidly, especially in the grip and forearm, can develop irritation where the forearm tendons attach at the elbow, similar to tennis elbow or golfer's elbow. The same can happen at the shoulder.

The warning sign is a dull, nagging pain near a joint, often worse at the start of a session or the next morning, that eases as you warm up and returns afterwards. Do not ignore it. Tendon problems that are caught early usually settle with a few weeks of reduced load. Tendon problems that are trained through can last for months.

### Circulation

We dealt with this in Chapter 7. Strong tension combined with forceful breathing raises blood pressure. The warning signs are a flushed face, a pounding head, dizziness and visual disturbance. The answer is to reduce both tension and breathing intensity, never to hold the breath, and, for anyone with relevant health conditions, to take medical advice.

## Building Tension Over Time

Like any training load, tension should be increased gradually. The body adapts to what it is asked to do, but it adapts on its own timetable, and that timetable is slower for tendons and joints than for muscles or for willpower.

A sensible approach is to treat the peak tension level of your Sanchin as one of your training variables, like the weight on a barbell. Start low, increase it slowly and vary it from session to session.

| Phase | Typical peak level | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning | 2–4 | First 2–3 months | Focus on posture and sequence; natural or quiet breathing |
| Building | 4–6 | Next 3–6 months | Introduce audible breathing; work on the spill point |
| Consolidating | 5–7 | Following 6–12 months | Firm breathing; testing becomes firmer |
| Mature practice | Varies, mostly 4–7 | Ongoing | Occasional sessions at 8; rarely higher |

: **Table 8.1** A rough guide to building tension over time. Individual progress varies widely; use your spill point and your body's responses as the real guide.

Within any week, vary your intensity. A useful pattern for students practising Sanchin three or four times a week is one hard session (peak at the upper end of your current range), one or two moderate sessions (the middle of your range) and one light session (the bottom of your range, with full attention on posture and breath). The light session is not wasted. It is where much of the refinement happens.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

Some of the finest Sanchin I have ever seen was performed at what I would guess was a four or five on our scale. The structure was so well connected that, when tested, the body felt like stone, yet the face was calm and the breathing quiet. High tension is not the goal. Connection is the goal, and high tension is sometimes a useful way of training it. Once the connection is there, you need much less effort to achieve the same result.
:::

## Relaxation as a Skill

Because Sanchin emphasises tension, it is easy to forget that releasing tension is equally a skill, and one that many people find harder. The Go of Goju-Ryu is incomplete without the Ju.

After each technique, the peak tension should ease back to the baseline smoothly, not collapse. After the kata, the whole body should be able to let go. Many students find that they carry Sanchin tension out of the dojo with them: in their shoulders, their jaw or their grip. That is a sign that the relaxation half of the training needs work.

::: drill
**Drill 8.3 – Contract and Release**

*You need:* a mat or a quiet place to lie down. Do this after Sanchin practice, or on its own.

*What to do:* Lie on your back. Starting with your feet, tense each region of the body in turn to about level six for five seconds, then release completely for ten seconds, noticing the difference. Work upwards: feet, calves, thighs, buttocks, abdomen, back, hands, forearms, upper arms, shoulders, neck, face. Breathe slowly throughout; do not hold the breath during the tensing.

*What to look for:* Notice any region that does not release fully, that feels as though it is still holding a little tension after you have let go. Those are the areas that tend to carry tension in your Sanchin too.

*Why it matters:* This is a version of a well-known relaxation technique, adapted to Sanchin. It teaches you the difference between tension and release, which makes it much easier to control both in the kata.
:::

::: fault
**Problem: "My jaw aches after training."**

*Cause.* You are clenching your teeth during the out-breaths. This is very common and is part of tension spilling into the soft zone.

*Correct.* For the next two weeks, practise Sanchin with your teeth very slightly apart and your tongue resting on the roof of your mouth. Check your jaw at the end of every out-breath: is it soft? If you find it clenching, reduce your peak tension until it stops. You may also find that practising the out-breath with the mouth in a small "o" helps.
:::

## Tension and Age

Finally, a word for older practitioners and for those returning to training. Tendons and joints become less tolerant of high loads with age, and blood pressure tends to rise. Neither of these is a reason to stop practising Sanchin. Both are reasons to practise it with more intelligence.

If you are over fifty, or returning after a long break, I would suggest keeping your peak tension at five or below for most sessions, using Stage 2 or 3 breathing, and placing more emphasis on the structure and the connection than on effort. You will find that a well-connected Sanchin at moderate tension gives you most of the benefit, with far less risk. Chapter 27 has more on practising for life.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Much of the tension in Sanchin is isometric co-contraction, which stabilises the body but compresses joints and burns energy.
- Use a ten-point scale to describe tension. Find your spill point, the level at which tension spreads to the face and shoulders, and work just below it.
- The body divides into three zones: a baseline zone (legs, hips, back) at 3–4 throughout; a peak zone (arms, lats, trunk) building to 5–8 at the end of each technique; a soft zone (face, jaw, neck, tops of shoulders) staying at 1–2.
- Tension builds from the ground up: feet, legs, pelvis, trunk, back, arm, fist. Tension that starts in the fist stays in the arm.
- The fist grips hardest at the little and ring fingers and softens slightly between techniques.
- Watch for joint pain, tendon pain and circulatory warning signs, and respond to each.
- Build peak tension gradually over months, and vary it within each week. Relaxation is a skill to be trained alongside tension.
:::

### Training Tasks

1. Carry out Drill 8.1 and record your current spill point.
2. Practise Drill 8.2 three times a week for a month. Note any change in how connected your punches feel.
3. For one week, record the peak tension level of every Sanchin you perform, and how you felt afterwards. Look for patterns.
4. Practise Drill 8.3 after each training session for two weeks.
5. Check your jaw, your grip and the tops of your shoulders three times a day, away from training, for a week. Do you carry tension there in daily life?
6. Using Table 8.1, decide which phase you are in, and plan your peak levels for the next four weeks: one hard, one or two moderate and one light session each week.

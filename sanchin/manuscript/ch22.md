# Conditioning for Sanchin: Principles and Assessment

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin builds strength, but it also demands it. This chapter explains which physical qualities the kata depends on, the training principles that develop them safely, and a ten-part assessment you can use to measure your starting point and track your progress. The traditional Okinawan tools and the full twelve-week programme follow in the next two chapters.
:::

There comes a point in every Sanchin student's training when technique is no longer the limiting factor. They know where their feet go, how their pelvis should sit, what their shoulders should do and how to breathe. And yet, two minutes into the kata, the stance starts to drift, the shoulders creep up and the breath becomes ragged. They know what to do. Their body simply cannot keep doing it.

At that point, more Sanchin is not always the answer. Performing the kata repeatedly while tired mostly trains you to perform it badly. What is needed is capacity: strength in the legs, hips and back, endurance in the muscles that hold the posture, grip and forearm strength for the fists, and enough mobility in the hips, ankles and upper back to reach the positions without strain.

Okinawan teachers understood this well. The old Naha-te tradition surrounded Sanchin with supplementary training, *hojo undo*, using stone weights, gripping jars and other simple tools to build exactly the strength the kata needed. Chapter 23 describes these tools and their modern equivalents. This chapter lays the foundation: what to train, how to train it and how to measure it.

## What Sanchin Asks of the Body

Sanchin's physical demands fall into five groups.

**Leg and hip strength and endurance.** The stance must be held, slightly bent and braced, for several minutes while stepping and turning. The thighs, buttocks and the muscles around the hip do most of this work. Under testing, they must also resist pushes from every direction.

**Trunk strength and control.** The trunk must stay braced around the breath, resist pushes and transmit force between the legs and arms. This depends on the deep abdominal muscles, the muscles of the lower back and the obliques at the sides.

**Back and shoulder-girdle strength.** The shoulder blades must stay drawn down and flat, and the latissimus muscles must hold the arms connected to the trunk, for the whole kata.

**Grip and forearm endurance.** The fists must stay firm, and the wrists straight, through dozens of techniques. The open hands at the end of the kata need finger strength.

**Mobility.** The hips must allow the knees to draw in without strain, the ankles must allow the knees to bend forward over the feet, and the upper back must allow the chest to open and the shoulders to settle. Stiffness in any of these forces compensation elsewhere.

There is also a sixth demand, less physical: **breathing capacity**, the ability to sustain long, controlled exhalations under effort. This improves with Sanchin practice itself and with general aerobic fitness.

## Principles of Training

Supplementary training for Sanchin follows the same principles as any sensible strength programme. They are worth stating, because many martial artists, keen to work hard, ignore them.

### Specificity

The body adapts to what you ask of it. To get better at holding a bent-knee, inward-braced stance, train in positions close to that stance: wall sits at the Sanchin knee angle, split squats with the knees tracking over the toes, isometric holds with the thighs squeezing. General leg training helps too, but specific training helps more.

### Progressive overload

To keep improving, the demand must gradually increase. This can mean more weight, more repetitions, longer holds, more sets, harder variations or less rest. Increase only one of these at a time, and only by a small amount, typically five to ten per cent a week at most.

### Recovery

Muscles, tendons and joints adapt during rest, not during training. Strength work for a given muscle group should usually be separated by at least forty-eight hours. Sleep, food and hydration matter as much as the exercises.

### Consistency over intensity

Three moderate sessions a week, kept up for months, will do far more than occasional heroic efforts. The Okinawan tradition, in which students used the hojo undo tools for a few minutes before every class, reflects this. Little and often wins.

### Joint and tendon health

Tendons adapt more slowly than muscles (Chapter 8). Increase loads gradually, especially for the grip and forearms, which are particularly prone to overuse problems. Any joint pain is a signal to reduce the load and check technique.

### Isometric and dynamic

Sanchin is largely isometric: muscles working hard without much movement. Isometric training is therefore very specific to the kata. But dynamic training, where muscles move through a range under load, builds strength more broadly, keeps joints healthy and improves mobility. A good programme uses both.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

The single most common mistake I see in martial artists who start strength training is doing too much too soon. They are used to pushing through discomfort in the dojo, and they bring that attitude to the weights. Within a few weeks they have a sore elbow, a stiff back or an aching knee, and they stop. Start lighter than you think you need to. Finish each session feeling you could have done more. Come back two days later and do a little more. You will be surprised how far that takes you in three months.
:::

## Warming Up

Every strength session, and every Sanchin session, should begin with a warm-up of five to ten minutes. The aim is to raise the body temperature, take the joints through their range and wake up the muscles that will be used.

A simple warm-up for Sanchin and its supplementary training:

1. **Marching or easy jogging on the spot,** one to two minutes.
2. **Joint circles:** ankles, knees, hips, waist, shoulders, elbows, wrists, neck, five to ten each way.
3. **Bodyweight squats** to a comfortable depth, knees tracking over the toes, ten slow repetitions.
4. **Hip openers:** standing on one leg, draw the other knee up and out in a circle, five each way, each leg.
5. **Arm swings** and **band pull-aparts**, ten each.
6. **Three slow Sanchin breaths** in a natural stance, at Stage 2.

## The Ten-Part Assessment

Before you begin any programme, measure where you are. The assessment below takes about thirty minutes, needs only a wall, a timer, a bar or sturdy branch to hang from and a partner to count and watch form. Repeat it every four to six weeks.

Each test has three rough benchmarks: **foundation**, **developing** and **strong**. These are not scientific norms and they do not take account of age, size or sex. They are practical targets drawn from experience of what Sanchin needs. Use them as a guide to which areas need most work, not as a judgement of your worth as a karateka. If you are older, recovering from injury or new to exercise, the foundation benchmarks are an excellent goal in themselves.

Stop any test as soon as your form breaks, and record the time or number at that point.

![**Figure 22.3** The remaining two physical tests: the glute bridge hold, with the body straight from knees to shoulders, and the press-up, with the body straight and the elbows close to the sides as in the Sanchin punch.](figures/fig22_3.png)

### Test A: Sanchin stance hold

Stand in sanchin dachi in the double block, at moderate tension and Stage 2 or 3 breathing. A partner watches your stance, pelvis and shoulders. Record the time until any of them visibly deteriorates.

Foundation: 1 minute. Developing: 3 minutes. Strong: 5 minutes.

### Test B: Wall sit at the Sanchin angle

Back against a wall, knees bent to roughly the Sanchin angle (not a deep squat), feet hip-width, knees over toes, pelvis gently tucked. Record the time until you cannot hold the position.

Foundation: 45 seconds. Developing: 90 seconds. Strong: 3 minutes.

### Test C: Front plank

On forearms and toes, body in a straight line from head to heels, pelvis gently tucked so that the lower back does not sag. Record the time until the hips sag or rise.

Foundation: 30 seconds. Developing: 60 seconds. Strong: 2 minutes.

### Test D: Side plank

On one forearm and the side of one foot, body in a straight line. Record the time on each side until the hips drop.

Foundation: 20 seconds each side. Developing: 45 seconds. Strong: 90 seconds. Note any difference between sides of more than about a quarter.

### Test E: Single-leg balance, eyes closed

Stand on one foot, the other foot lifted beside the standing ankle, hands on hips. Close your eyes. Record the time until you put the foot down, move the standing foot or open your eyes. Have a partner stand close by for safety.

Foundation: 10 seconds each leg. Developing: 20 seconds. Strong: 30 seconds or more.

### Test F: Glute bridge hold

Lying on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Lift the hips until the body is straight from knees to shoulders, squeezing the buttocks. Record the time until the hips drop.

Foundation: 30 seconds. Developing: 60 seconds. Strong: 2 minutes.

### Test G: Dead hang

Hang from a bar with an overhand grip, arms straight, shoulders gently engaged rather than hunched to the ears. Record the time until the grip fails. If you cannot hang, record a supported hang with your feet on a low step, taking some of the weight.

Foundation: 20 seconds. Developing: 45 seconds. Strong: 90 seconds.

### Test H: Press-ups

From the knees or the toes (record which), lower the chest to a fist's height from the floor and push back up, body straight, elbows close to the sides, as in the Sanchin punch. Record the number of good repetitions.

From knees: foundation 10, developing 20, strong 30. From toes: foundation 5, developing 15, strong 25.

### Test I: Mobility checks

Three quick checks, each scored as "easy", "possible" or "difficult":

- **Ankle:** kneeling lunge, front foot flat, can you move the front knee forward past the toes by a hand's width without the heel lifting?
- **Hip:** lying on your back, knees bent, can you let one knee fall in towards the other foot comfortably? (This is the inward rotation the Sanchin knees need.)
- **Upper back:** the stick test from Chapter 6: can the stick touch head, upper back and sacrum without strain?

### Test J: Out-breath duration

Stand in a natural stance. Take a full, comfortable breath in through the nose. Breathe out through a soft "haa", steadily, at the volume you would use in Sanchin at Stage 3. Record the time until the out-breath ends naturally. Do not force it to the very end of your lungs.

Foundation: 8 seconds. Developing: 12 seconds. Strong: 15 seconds or more.

## Reading the Results

The assessment results often explain faults that technique alone cannot. Some common patterns:

**Stance hold much shorter than wall sit.** Your legs are strong enough, but your technique is costing you energy. Look for tension in the soft zone and excess depth (Faults 19.10 and 21.15).

**Wall sit and stance hold both short.** Leg endurance is limiting your kata. Prioritise the leg work in Chapter 24.

**Planks short, especially the side plank.** Trunk endurance is limited. Expect the pelvis to tilt and the trunk to lean as you tire in the kata (Faults 20.1 and 20.5).

**Large difference between sides on the side plank or balance.** Expect asymmetry in the kata (Fault 20.14). Give the weaker side extra work.

**Balance poor.** Foot strength and control are likely weak. Prioritise the foot work in Drill 4.2 and the balance progressions in Chapter 24.

**Dead hang short.** Grip and forearm endurance are limited. Expect the blocks to sag and the fists to soften late in the kata. Chapter 23's grip training will help.

**Ankle or hip mobility difficult.** Expect knee alignment problems (Faults 19.11 and 19.12) and possibly knee discomfort. Address mobility before increasing stance work.

**Out-breath short.** Breathing capacity is limiting the rhythm of your kata. Expect running out of breath (Fault 21.4). Include aerobic work and the breathing drills in Chapter 24.

::: fault
**Problem: "My assessment results are all at foundation level or below. Should I stop Sanchin until I am stronger?"**

*No.* Keep practising Sanchin, but adjust it to your current capacity: lighter tension, Stage 2 breathing, fewer repetitions per session and a slightly higher stance. Use the kata to train skill and the supplementary programme to build capacity. As your capacity grows, the intensity of your Sanchin can grow with it. Many students find that their kata improves noticeably within six to eight weeks of starting supplementary training, even without any change to their technique.
:::

## Safety in Strength Training

The general safety guidance at the front of the book applies here too. In addition:

- **Breathe during strength exercises.** Do not hold your breath during lifts or holds. Breathe out during the hardest part of each repetition.
- **Form first.** Stop each set as soon as your form breaks. Repetitions with bad form train bad habits and risk injury.
- **Pain is a stop sign.** Muscular burning is normal; sharp pain, joint pain or pain that lasts into the next day is not.
- **Seek advice** if you have any existing injury or health condition, especially of the back, knees or shoulders, or any of the conditions in Before You Begin.
- **Children and teenagers** benefit from bodyweight strength work, balance and mobility training, but should not use heavy traditional tools such as the chi ishi or heavy jars without expert supervision.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- When technique is no longer the limiting factor, capacity is. More Sanchin is not always the answer; supplementary training is.
- Sanchin needs leg and hip strength and endurance, trunk control, back and shoulder-girdle strength, grip and forearm endurance, mobility and breathing capacity.
- Training principles: specificity, progressive overload, recovery, consistency over intensity, care for joints and tendons, and a mix of isometric and dynamic work.
- Warm up for five to ten minutes before every session.
- The ten-part assessment measures stance hold, wall sit, front and side planks, balance, glute bridge, dead hang, press-ups, mobility and out-breath duration.
- Assessment results often explain faults in the kata. Use them to set priorities.
- Breathe during strength exercises, put form first and treat pain as a stop sign.
:::

# Preface

Most karate students meet Sanchin in the same way. Somewhere in their first year, often on an ordinary weeknight when nothing special seems to be happening, the teacher lines the class up and announces that tonight they will begin the most important kata in the style. Then, for the next forty minutes, nobody moves very much.

You stand with your feet turned in and your knees squeezed towards each other. You are told to tuck your hips, drop your shoulders, tighten your back and breathe in a way that feels like a cross between a sigh and a grunt. You step forward so slowly that the person next to you seems to be standing still. By the end of the class your thighs are burning, your forearms ache and you are fairly sure you have done all of it wrong.

You probably have. Everybody does, at first. That is not a failure. It is how Sanchin begins.

I have written this book because I think Sanchin deserves better teaching material than it usually gets. It is the root of Goju-Ryu karate. Its stance, its breathing and its way of holding tension and releasing it appear in every other kata of the system. Senior teachers have long said that a student's Sanchin tells you almost everything about their karate. Yet the form is often taught in fragments: a correction here, a demonstration there, a few lines in a syllabus booklet. Students go home with a head full of instructions and no clear way to check whether they are following them.

This book tries to give you that way of checking. It is organised as a textbook and, more than that, as a problem-solving manual. Every element of the kata is broken down into parts you can test. The stance is built in layers, from the soles of your feet to the crown of your head, with a measurement or a check for each layer. The whole form is laid out in numbered frames, each with a diagram, its breath and the points a teacher would look for. There is a map of the places on the body where Sanchin is traditionally tested, with drills to test them with a partner or on your own. There is a catalogue of the faults that turn up again and again in the dojo, with their causes and their corrections. And there is a strength programme, drawn from the old Okinawan supplementary training and from modern sports science, to give your body the capacity the kata asks of it.

I have tried throughout to be honest about two things.

The first is variation. Sanchin is practised in many lineages, and the details differ. Some groups perform it with open hands, others with closed fists. Some turn during the kata and some do not. The number of steps, the exact path of the hands and the style of breathing all change from one association to another. I have chosen one widely taught version to describe in detail, and I say clearly where other groups do things differently. If your own teacher tells you something that contradicts these pages, listen to your teacher. They can see you. I cannot.

The second is safety. Sanchin is often described as a health exercise, and practised sensibly it can be one. But hard breathing against a tight throat and strong muscular tension do raise blood pressure, and partner testing can bruise or injure if it is done carelessly. I have not softened that. You will find clear guidance on who should take care, how to scale the practice and when to stop. A form that has been practised for more than a century should be able to look after the people who practise it.

A word about who this book is for. If you are a beginner, it will give you a reference to return to between classes, and it will save you a lot of the confusion I see in new students. If you are a coloured or black belt, it will help you to clean up habits that have crept into your kata over the years, and it will give you a structured way to work on strength. If you are an instructor, I hope it gives you language, diagrams and drills that make your teaching clearer. And if you have come back to karate after a long break, perhaps with a body that is older and stiffer than the one you trained with before, I hope it shows you that Sanchin is still yours to practise.

You will not learn Sanchin from a book. Nobody does. You learn it by standing in it, being corrected and standing in it again, many hundreds of times. What a book can do is make each of those times count for a little more. That is what this one is for.

Dr Beverly J. Stivers

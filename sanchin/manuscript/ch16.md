# Why Teachers Test

::: chapter-intro
In Goju-Ryu, Sanchin is checked by hand while it is being performed. This practice, called shime, has been part of the kata from the beginning, and it is one of the best teaching tools in karate when it is done well. Done badly, it is a source of injury and resentment. This chapter explains what testing is for, sets out the rules that keep it safe, describes four levels of testing from light touch to firm striking, and explains how to give and receive a test properly.
:::

The first time a teacher tests your Sanchin can be unsettling. You are standing in the stance, breathing as you have been taught, concentrating on your posture, and someone steps up behind you and presses down hard on your shoulders. Or slaps the backs of your thighs. Or pushes on your stomach as you breathe out. Your instinct is to flinch, to brace, to hold your breath or to look round. All of those are wrong, and you will probably do all of them.

That is the point. Testing shows you, instantly and unarguably, where your structure holds and where it does not. A teacher can tell you a hundred times that your shoulders are rising, and you may not believe them. When they press on your shoulders and you feel them give way, you believe it at once.

Testing is also one of the parts of Sanchin most open to misuse. In some places and at some times, it has slipped into something closer to an endurance contest or a rite of passage, with students hit harder and harder to prove their toughness. That is not what it is for, and it has caused real injuries. This chapter sets out what testing is for, and the rules that keep it useful and safe.

## What Testing Is For

Testing, *shime*, serves four purposes. They overlap, but it helps to keep them separate in your mind, because the right kind of test depends on which purpose you are serving.

### Feedback

The most important purpose of testing is to tell the student, and the teacher, whether the structure is correct. A gentle push on the shoulder tells you whether the shoulder blade is drawn down. A hand on the lower back tells you whether the pelvis is tucked. A press on the fist at the end of a punch tells you whether the arm is connected to the back and the back to the legs.

This kind of testing is informational. It does not need to be hard. Often, the lightest touch is the most informative, because it shows the structure as it is, without provoking the student into bracing.

### Correction

Testing also corrects. A teacher's hand on the shoulder does not just reveal that it is rising; it guides it down. A tap on the inside of the knee tells the student where to press. Much of the original teaching of Sanchin, as described in the accounts of Higaonna and Miyagi, seems to have been of this kind: the teacher shaping the student's posture by hand.

### Conditioning

Firmer testing, particularly slapping and striking the muscles of the trunk, arms and legs, conditions the body. It accustoms the muscles to impact, teaches them to tighten at the right moment and toughens the skin and tissues. This is the purpose most people think of when they think of Sanchin testing. It is real and valuable, but it is only one of the four, and it is the one that carries the most risk.

### The mind

Finally, testing trains the mind. Standing calmly, breathing steadily and keeping your structure while someone presses or strikes you is a direct exercise in the inner battle described in Chapter 3. The student learns not to flinch, not to panic and not to over-react. Many senior practitioners say that this is the most lasting benefit of testing.

## The Four Levels of Testing

In this book, testing is described at four levels. These are not universal terms, and your dojo may organise testing differently. But they give a useful way of thinking about how hard to test, and who should be tested how.

### Level 1: Touch

The tester places a hand lightly on part of the student's body: the shoulder, the lower back, the inside of the knee, the elbow. There is little or no pressure. The purpose is to check position and to draw the student's attention to a part of the body. Level 1 testing is suitable for everyone, including complete beginners and children, from the first lesson.

### Level 2: Pressure

The tester applies steady pressure, building over two or three seconds and released smoothly. Pushes on the shoulders, the fists, the elbows, the knees and the hips are typical. The purpose is to test whether the structure holds under load. Level 2 testing is suitable for any student who has learned the basic stance, usually within the first few months. Most of the testing in Chapter 17 is at this level.

### Level 3: Patting and slapping

The tester strikes the student's muscles with an open hand, using a light to moderate slap or pat. Typical targets are the tops of the shoulders, the large muscles of the back, the upper arms, the thighs and the calves. The purpose is partly to test, since a muscle that is not engaged gives under the slap, and partly to condition. Level 3 testing is suitable for students who are settled in the kata, can breathe correctly under tension and have been introduced to testing at Levels 1 and 2. It should always begin gently.

### Level 4: Firm striking

The tester strikes with firm open-hand slaps, the edge of the hand, the forearm or, in some lineages, the fist, to the large muscles of the trunk and limbs. The purpose is conditioning and the training of the mind under stress. Level 4 testing is suitable only for experienced students, typically at senior coloured-belt or black-belt level, and only when the tester is experienced and under the supervision of a senior instructor. Many dojos use Level 4 testing rarely or not at all, and there is no shame in that.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

When I was a young student, I thought the hardest testing was the best testing. It took me years to understand that most of what I learned about my Sanchin came from my teacher's lightest touches: a fingertip on my shoulder blade, a hand resting on my lower back. The hard testing toughened me, which was useful. But the light testing taught me, which was more useful. If you are a teacher, test lightly most of the time. You will learn more about your students that way, and they will learn more about themselves.
:::

## The Rules

Testing is safe when everyone involved knows and follows a clear set of rules. The rules below are those I use and recommend. Your dojo may have its own; if so, follow those.

### 1. Supervision

Testing above Level 1 should take place only in a class, under the supervision of a qualified instructor. Students should not test each other at Levels 3 and 4 without an instructor present.

### 2. Consent

Nobody should be tested without having agreed to it. In practice, in a dojo where testing is part of the curriculum, consent is usually given by joining the class and is renewed each time by the student taking up the stance when the instructor announces testing. But any student must be free to decline testing, or a particular level of testing, on a given day, without having to explain why and without any penalty. Injuries, illness, pregnancy and simple tiredness are all good reasons.

### 3. Proportion

The force of the test must suit the student's level, size, age and condition, and the purpose of the test. A beginner is tested at Level 1 or 2. A child is tested only at Level 1, with a light Level 2 at most. A heavier, stronger tester must adjust for a lighter student. Testing is never a contest between tester and student.

### 4. Build gradually

Within any single test, start light and build. The first touch or push should be the gentlest. Within a student's training, start at Level 1 and progress over months and years, not days.

### 5. No-go areas

Certain areas of the body must never be struck or pushed hard in testing, regardless of level:

- **the head, face and neck**, including the throat and the back of the neck;
- **the spine**, along the middle of the back;
- **the kidneys**, in the lower back on either side of the spine, below the ribs;
- **the groin**;
- **the joints themselves**: the knees, elbows, wrists and the front of the shoulder joint, especially from the side or against their natural direction of bending;
- **the floating ribs** at the lower edge of the ribcage, with anything more than light pressure;
- **the solar plexus** and **the lower abdomen**, with anything more than steady pressure during the out-breath.

### 6. Timing

Testing pressure is applied during the out-breath, when the structure is braced, and eased during the in-breath. Striking a student during the in-breath, when the trunk is softer, is both less useful and more likely to hurt them. A tester should watch and listen to the student's breath and time the test accordingly.

### 7. Communication

The student must have a clear way of saying "stop". A spoken word, agreed in advance, or a tap on the thigh both work. When the signal is given, the tester stops at once, without discussion. Equally, the tester should tell the student, before they begin, what they are going to test.

### 8. Stop signs

The tester stops at once if the student shows any sign of distress: holding the breath, a reddening or greying face, wobbling, wincing or tears. The student stops at once if they feel any of the warning signs in Before You Begin: dizziness, a sudden headache or sharp joint pain.

### 9. Health

Students with any of the health conditions listed at the front of the book should not take part in testing above Level 2 without medical advice. Students who are pregnant should not be tested on the trunk at all.

::: fault
**Problem: "I hate being tested. I tense up before the tester even touches me."**

*Check.* Notice when the tension starts. Is it when the instructor announces testing? When the tester approaches? When they touch you?

*Cause.* Anticipation. Your body is bracing in advance for something it expects to be unpleasant. This is entirely natural, and it is exactly what testing is meant to train out of you. Pre-bracing spoils the test, because it shows your effort rather than your structure, and it is tiring.

*Correct.* Ask your instructor if you can be tested at Level 1 only for a few weeks, with the tester telling you each time what they are about to touch. Keep breathing. Let the touch come to you. Over time, increase to Level 2. The aim is to learn that the test is not a threat, so that your body responds to it rather than anticipating it.

*Confirm.* After a few weeks, notice whether the anticipatory tension has eased.
:::

## Being Tested

Receiving a test well is a skill. Here are the principles.

**Keep breathing.** The breath should continue exactly as it would without the test. Do not hold it, gasp or change its rhythm. If the test makes you hold your breath, tell the tester to ease off.

**Do not anticipate.** Respond to the test when it arrives, not before. If you know the tester is about to press your shoulders, do not hunch them in advance. Keep your structure as it is and let it meet the pressure.

**Do not push back.** A test is not a pushing contest. If the tester presses on your shoulders, you do not push upwards against them; you keep your shoulders where they are, drawn down by your back. If they push your fist, you do not shove forward; you hold the punch in place. Pushing back shows effort, not structure.

**Keep your eyes forward.** Do not look round at the tester. Keep your gaze on your fixed point. This is part of the inner battle.

**Keep your face calm.** A grimace under testing is tension spilling into the soft zone.

**Say if it hurts.** Pain in a joint, a sharp pain anywhere or any of the warning signs means stop. Give the agreed signal. A good tester will thank you for it.

## Giving a Test

Testing others is also a skill, and an important one. It trains your eye and your hands, and it deepens your understanding of your own Sanchin. Here are the principles.

**Know what you are testing.** Before you touch the student, decide what you are checking and why. A random push tells nobody anything.

**Stand well.** Your own stance must be stable, close enough to apply force without reaching and positioned so that you will not lose your balance if the student's structure gives way. Many testers stand in a short, natural stance or in sanchin dachi themselves.

**Use the right part of the hand.** For pushes, use the flat of the palm or the fingertips, depending on the target. For slaps, use a relaxed open hand. Never use the fingertips to jab.

**Build slowly.** Start with the lightest possible contact and increase steadily. Watch the student's response. Stop increasing as soon as the structure gives, or the student shows any sign of strain.

**Time it with the breath.** Apply pressure on the out-breath, ease it on the in-breath.

**Release smoothly.** Do not snatch your hand away; release the pressure gradually so the student is not thrown off balance.

**Give feedback.** After the test, tell the student what you found, briefly and specifically. "Your right shoulder rose when I pressed; your left held well."

**Respect the student.** Testing is something you do *for* the student, not *to* them. If you find yourself wanting to see how hard you can push, stop testing.

::: drill
**Drill 16.1 – Tester Training**

*You need:* a partner and an instructor's permission.

*What to do:* Take turns. The student stands in sanchin dachi in the double block, breathing at Stage 2. The tester carries out five Level 1 touches, one at a time: the tops of the shoulders, the lower back, the inside of each knee and one elbow. For each, the tester says what they are touching and then describes what they feel: is the shoulder drawn down, is the back long, is the knee pressing in? The student confirms or disputes. Swap roles.

*Why it matters:* This drill trains testers to feel rather than push, and to put what they feel into words. It is the foundation for all the testing in Chapter 17.
:::

## Testing and Trust

Testing depends on trust. The student has to trust that the tester will not hurt them, will respect the stop signal and will test them for their benefit. The tester has to trust that the student will be honest about their limits and will tell them if anything is wrong. The instructor has to trust both, and to watch both.

In a good dojo, this trust grows over time, and testing becomes one of the most valued parts of training: a shared practice in which students help each other to improve. In a poor dojo, it never develops, and testing becomes something to be endured or avoided.

If you are a student in a dojo where testing feels unsafe, where the rules in this chapter are routinely ignored, where students are tested harder than their level warrants, where the stop signal is not respected or where testing seems to be about the tester's ego rather than the student's progress, you have every right to decline it and to raise your concerns with the senior instructor. If nothing changes, you have every right to find another dojo. Good karate does not require you to be hurt.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Testing, *shime*, has four purposes: feedback, correction, conditioning and the training of the mind. Feedback and correction are the most important.
- Testing can be graded in four levels: touch, pressure, patting and slapping, and firm striking. Most testing should be at Levels 1 and 2.
- The rules are: supervision, consent, proportion, building gradually, observing the no-go areas, timing with the breath, clear communication, responding to stop signs, and taking health into account.
- Never strike the head, neck, spine, kidneys, groin, joints, floating ribs, solar plexus or lower abdomen.
- When being tested: keep breathing, do not anticipate, do not push back, keep your eyes forward and your face calm, and say if it hurts.
- When testing: know what you are checking, stand well, build slowly, time with the breath, release smoothly, give feedback and respect the student.
- Testing depends on trust. If testing in your dojo feels unsafe, you are entitled to decline it and to raise concerns.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Learn the no-go areas in Figure 16.2 well enough to point to them on your own body without the book.
2. Agree a stop signal with your training partners and your instructor.
3. Practise Drill 16.1, tester training, at Level 1 with a partner, once a week for a month.
4. Next time you are tested, notice whether you anticipate the test. Write down when the tension started.
5. Write down, in your own words, the difference between pushing back against a test and holding your structure.
6. If you teach, review the testing practice in your class against the rules in this chapter. Is there anything you would change?

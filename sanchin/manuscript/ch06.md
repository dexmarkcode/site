# The Spine, Shoulders and Head

::: chapter-intro
Above the pelvis, Sanchin asks for a long spine, a settled ribcage, shoulders drawn down into the back, arms connected to the trunk and a head carried level with the eyes forward. This chapter builds the upper body on top of the stance, explains what each part is doing and why, and gives you tests and drills for the faults that show up most often above the belt: raised shoulders, flared ribs, a poking chin and a rounded or arched back.
:::

When people first watch Sanchin, the upper body is what they notice. The arms held in the block, the punches driven out slowly, the chest and shoulders visibly working. It is natural to assume that this is where the effort goes. In fact, a good Sanchin upper body is defined as much by what it does not do as by what it does. The shoulders do not rise. The chest does not puff out. The neck does not stiffen. The face does not strain. The arms do not move on their own, independent of the trunk.

Getting the upper body right is therefore largely a matter of restraint. You are asking the parts of your body that most want to help, the shoulders, the neck and the jaw, to stay quiet, while the parts that rarely get much attention, the back, the sides and the deep muscles of the trunk, take over the work.

## The Long Spine

The spine is not straight. Seen from the side, it has three gentle curves: a forward curve in the neck, a backward curve in the upper back and a forward curve in the lower back. These curves act like a spring, absorbing shock and distributing load. Sanchin does not try to remove them. It tries to lengthen them, reducing the exaggerated curves that most of us carry, so that the spine is as tall and as evenly loaded as it can be.

In Chapter 5 you lengthened the lower back by tucking the pelvis. Now continue that length upwards. Imagine a thread attached to the crown of your head, the highest point of the skull, slightly towards the back, drawing you gently upwards. At the same time, your tailbone is drawing gently down. The spine lengthens between these two points, like a bow being strung.

The lengthening should be gentle. You are not trying to stand as tall as possible, which tends to lift the shoulders and stiffen the neck. You are trying to remove slack. A useful check is that after lengthening you should feel slightly taller, perhaps by a centimetre, but no stiffer.

### Stacking

When the spine is long, the main weight-bearing parts of the body stack on top of each other. Seen from the side, a vertical line, the plumb line, should pass close to the ear, the point of the shoulder, the middle of the hip and the area just in front of the ankle of the rear leg. Of course, in sanchin dachi, with one foot forward and both knees bent, the legs are not directly under the trunk in a simple line. But the head, shoulders and hips should still stack: head above shoulders, shoulders above hips.

The most common faults of stacking are a forward head, where the ear is well in front of the shoulder; a forward lean of the whole trunk, where the shoulders are in front of the hips; and a backward lean, where the shoulders are behind the hips, usually caused by over-tucking the pelvis.

::: drill
**Drill 6.1 – The Stick Test**

*You need:* a broom handle or a length of dowel about a metre and a half long, and a partner if possible.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi. Hold the stick vertically behind you, one hand behind your neck and the other behind your lower back, so that it lies along your spine. Adjust your posture until the stick touches three points: the back of your head, the upper back between your shoulder blades and the flat bone at the base of your spine, the sacrum. There should be a small gap, roughly the thickness of your hand, between the stick and your lower back, and a smaller one behind your neck.

*What to look for:* If the back of your head will not touch the stick without tilting your face upwards, your head is carried too far forward. If your lower back gap is much larger than your hand, your pelvis is tilted forward. If there is no gap at all at the lower back, you are probably over-tucked.

*Practice:* Hold the position for five slow breaths. Remove the stick and try to keep the same alignment for five more breaths. Repeat five times.

*Why it matters:* The stick gives you an objective reference that no mirror can. It is one of the oldest and simplest posture checks there is.
:::

## The Ribcage

The ribcage sits on top of the lumbar spine like a basket. In Sanchin it should neither flare up and forward, which is what happens when people "stand up straight" by puffing their chest out, nor sink down and collapse, which is what happens when people slump.

The flared ribcage is the more common fault in martial arts students, partly because of a mistaken idea that a big chest looks strong. When the lower ribs flare forward, the upper back tends to arch, the abdomen loses its tone and the breath rises into the upper chest. The trunk becomes a weak link between the hips and the shoulders.

The correction is to let the lower ribs settle down and slightly in, towards the pelvis. Place your hands on the lowest ribs at the front of your body. Breathe out slowly, and feel the ribs move down and in under your hands. Keep them there, more or less, as you breathe in again, letting the breath widen the ribs sideways and backwards rather than lifting them forwards. The distance between your lowest ribs and the front of your hip bones should stay roughly the same throughout.

This ribcage position works together with the tucked pelvis. The ribs settle down, the pelvis tucks up, and the abdomen between them is gently compressed and firm. This is the structure that the breathing of Chapter 7 will fill.

::: fault
**Problem: "When I breathe in, my chest rises and my shoulders go up."**

*Cause.* You are breathing into the upper chest, using the muscles of the neck and shoulders to lift the ribcage. This is extremely common, especially in people who are anxious or who have spent years sitting at a desk.

*Correct.* Lie on your back with your knees bent. Place one hand on your upper chest and the other on your abdomen, just below the ribs. Breathe in slowly through your nose so that the lower hand rises and the upper hand stays still. Then place your hands on the sides of your lower ribs and breathe so that the ribs widen sideways into your hands. Practise for five minutes a day for a week. Then try the same in sitting, and finally in sanchin dachi. Chapter 7 develops this in full.
:::

## The Shoulders

If there is one correction you will hear more than any other in Sanchin, it is "drop your shoulders". Raised shoulders are almost universal among beginners, and they persist in a surprising number of experienced students.

### Why the shoulders rise

The shoulders rise for several reasons, and it is worth knowing which applies to you, because the corrections are different.

**Effort spreading.** When you tense your arms, the tension tends to spread up into the large muscles across the top of the shoulders and the base of the neck, the upper trapezius. This is a natural reflex, and it has to be trained out.

**Breathing.** As we have just seen, breathing into the upper chest lifts the ribcage and the shoulders with it.

**Posture.** If the upper back is rounded, the shoulder blades slide forwards around the ribcage and the shoulders tend to rise and roll forward.

**Anxiety.** Under stress, most people lift their shoulders protectively. Being watched or tested in Sanchin is stressful.

### Drawing the shoulders down

The correction is not simply to push the shoulders down, which tends to create tension of a different kind. It is to draw the shoulder blades down and slightly together, using the muscles of the middle and lower back, so that the tops of the shoulders can relax.

Try this. Stand normally and lift your shoulders up towards your ears. Now let them drop. Now, without lifting them again, draw your shoulder blades gently down your back, as though sliding them into your back pockets. You should feel the muscles below and between your shoulder blades engage, and the tops of your shoulders soften. The neck should feel longer.

This is the shoulder position for Sanchin. The shoulder blades are drawn down, slightly together and flat against the ribcage. The tops of the shoulders are relaxed. The distance between the tops of your shoulders and your earlobes is as long as it comfortably can be.

## Connecting the Arms to the Trunk

With the shoulder blades drawn down, the next step is to connect the arms to the trunk. This is what makes the block in Sanchin so hard to move and the punch so solid. Without it, the arm is an independent lever hanging off the shoulder joint. With it, the arm becomes an extension of the back.

The muscle that does most of this work is the *latissimus dorsi*, the broad, flat muscle that runs from the lower and middle back, around the side of the ribcage, to the front of the upper arm near the armpit. When it engages, it draws the upper arm down and back and presses it towards the side of the body. In Sanchin, it is held in steady, moderate contraction throughout the kata.

You can feel it working. Stand with your arms in the double block. Place the fingertips of your right hand into your left armpit, just at the back, on the thick fold of muscle there. Now press your left elbow gently down and in, as though trying to squeeze a sheet of paper between your upper arm and your ribs. You should feel the muscle under your fingers firm up. That is the connection.

Teachers have several ways of describing this: "closing the armpits", "spreading the back", "pressing the elbows down". All of them point to the same thing. When it is right, the sides of the back feel wide and firm, the elbows feel heavy and anchored, and the arms feel as though they are growing out of the back rather than the shoulders.

### The position of the elbows

In the Sanchin block, the elbows are held about one fist's width away from the sides of the body, roughly in front of the lowest ribs. They should not flare out to the side, which disconnects the arm from the back, and they should not press tightly against the ribs, which restricts the breath and pulls the forearm out of position. The elbows point down and slightly inwards.

Chapter 13 looks at the block in detail. For now, simply notice the relationship: elbows down and in, armpits closed, back wide, shoulders down.

![**Figure 6.3** The back, seen from behind. The shoulder blades draw down and slightly together (inner arrows); the large muscles of the back spread and press the upper arms towards the body (outer arrows). The tops of the shoulders stay soft.](figures/fig6_3.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Elbow Push**

Stand in sanchin dachi with your arms in the double block. Ask a partner to stand in front of you and place their palms under your elbows, then push slowly upwards, building pressure over three seconds. Your elbows should stay down, held there by your back. If they rise easily, or if your shoulders rise as they push, your arms are not connected to your trunk. Draw the shoulder blades down, close the armpits and try again.

Now ask your partner to push your elbows slowly outwards from the inside. They should not flare. If they do, focus on pressing the upper arms gently towards your sides.
:::

## The Neck and Head

The final element of the upper body is the head. In Sanchin, the head sits level and balanced on top of a long neck. The chin is drawn very slightly in, as if making a small double chin, so that the back of the neck lengthens. The eyes look straight ahead at the horizon.

### The forward head

The most common fault is a forward head, in which the chin pokes forward and the head is carried in front of the shoulders. It is extremely common in modern life, the result of hours spent looking at screens, and it creates tension in the back of the neck and the tops of the shoulders. In Sanchin, it also weakens the structure: when a teacher tests the shoulders, the head nods forward with each push, and the neck takes strain it should not.

The correction is a gentle chin tuck: draw the chin straight back, not down, so that the head glides backwards over the shoulders. The back of the neck should lengthen and the front of the throat should soften. You may feel a mild stretch at the base of the skull. Do not overdo it. You should still be able to swallow comfortably and breathe freely.

### The eyes

The eyes look straight ahead, level, at a fixed point roughly at the height of your own eyes. They do not drop to the floor, which tips the head forward and tends to round the upper back. They do not look up, which tilts the head back and compresses the neck. And they do not wander.

Steady eyes help the posture and the mind at the same time. Japanese martial arts have a well-known principle sometimes described as "looking at a distant mountain", in which the gaze rests softly on a point in front while remaining aware of everything in the peripheral vision. In Sanchin, this soft, steady gaze is the outward sign of the calm attention described in Chapter 3.

### The face and jaw

The jaw should be relaxed, with the teeth lightly together or very slightly apart and the tongue resting gently against the roof of the mouth behind the upper teeth. Some teachers place more emphasis on the tongue position than others; follow your own teacher's guidance.

The face should be calm. The forehead is smooth, the eyes are open and relaxed, and the mouth is not grimacing. This is surprisingly hard. Tension in the face is closely linked to tension in the neck and shoulders, and both are linked to the breath. When the face relaxes, the shoulders often relax too.

![**Figure 6.4** The head and neck seen from the side. Left: chin poking forward, head ahead of the shoulders, neck shortened at the back. Right: chin drawn slightly back, head balanced over the shoulders, neck long, eyes level.](figures/fig6_4.png)

::: fault
**Problem: "My neck aches after Sanchin."**

*Check.* Film yourself from the side and look at your head position, especially during the exhalations. Notice also whether your face is grimacing and whether your shoulders are rising.

*Cause.* Usually a combination of a forward head, raised shoulders and forceful exhalation through a tense throat. These three feed each other.

*Correct.* Work on them in this order: first the chin tuck (Drill 6.2 below), then the shoulder draw-down, and only then the breath. Reduce the force of your exhalation for a few weeks while the posture settles. A soft "haa" with the mouth slightly open is enough.

*Confirm.* The ache should ease within a week or two. If it does not, or if you have pain running into the arm or hand, see a physiotherapist.
:::

::: drill
**Drill 6.2 – Upper Body Set**

Do these in order, standing in sanchin dachi, with slow breathing throughout.

1. **Chin glide.** Without tilting your head, glide your chin straight back as far as is comfortable, hold for three seconds, then let it come halfway forward. That halfway point is your Sanchin head position. Ten repetitions.
2. **Shoulder slide.** Lift your shoulders to your ears, then draw them down and back into your "back pockets". Hold for five seconds, then relax the tops of the shoulders while keeping the blades down. Ten repetitions.
3. **Armpit close.** With your arms in the double block, press your elbows gently down and in towards your sides for five seconds, feeling your back widen. Relax slightly, but not fully. Ten repetitions.
4. **Rib settle.** Place your hands on your lowest ribs. Breathe out slowly and feel them move down and in. Breathe in, widening them sideways and back. Five breaths.
5. **Gaze.** Fix your eyes on a point at eye height. Hold for five breaths with a calm face.

*Why it matters:* This sequence assembles the upper body of Sanchin in a logical order, from the head down to the ribs, finishing with the eyes. Do it before each practice session for a month.
:::

## Bringing the Whole Posture Together

You now have every part of the static Sanchin posture: the feet, knees and hips from Chapters 4 and 5, and the spine, ribs, shoulders, arms and head from this chapter. Put them together and you have the posture shown in Figure 1.1 at the start of the book.

It is a lot to hold at once, and nobody holds it all perfectly. The trick, in my experience, is to build it in the same order every time, from the ground up, and to check a few points rather than trying to check everything. Here is a checklist that works well for most students. Run through it each time you step into the stance, until it becomes automatic.

| Area | Check |
|---|---|
| Feet | Shoulder width, one foot length, front toes in, three points, toes flat |
| Knees | Slight bend, kneecaps over second or third toes, thighs pressing gently in |
| Pelvis | Slightly tucked, buttocks and lower abdomen gently engaged, hips level |
| Ribs | Lowest ribs settled down and in, not flared |
| Shoulders | Blades drawn down into the back, tops relaxed |
| Arms | Elbows a fist's width from the body, armpits closed, back wide |
| Head | Chin slightly back, neck long, crown lifting |
| Eyes and face | Level gaze, calm face, relaxed jaw |

: **Table 6.1** The ground-up posture checklist.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

When I watch a student who is struggling with the upper body, I often find the answer is lower down. A student who cannot keep their shoulders down usually has a pelvis that is tipping forward or knees that are not bracing, so their upper body is working to compensate for an unstable base. Fix the base, and the shoulders often drop by themselves. This is the Cause step of our loop in action: look below the fault before you look at it directly.
:::

## Holding the Posture Over Time

A posture you can find is not the same as a posture you can hold. Most students can assemble a good Sanchin posture after a few weeks of practice with the checklist. Holding it through a three-minute kata, while stepping, turning, breathing and punching, takes much longer.

There are two reasons. The first is endurance: the muscles holding the posture tire, and as they tire, the posture slips. The second is attention: as you concentrate on the next movement, you forget to maintain the last correction. Both improve with time, but both can also be trained directly.

For endurance, the strength exercises in Chapter 5 and the full programme in Part Eight are the answer. For attention, the best method is what I call the "single check" practice. Each time you perform the kata, choose just one item from Table 6.1 and keep your attention on it throughout, from the first bow to the last. Next time, choose another. Over a few weeks, you will cycle through every item many times, and each one will gradually become something you no longer need to think about.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- A good Sanchin upper body is defined largely by restraint: the shoulders, neck, chest and face stay quiet while the back and trunk do the work.
- The spine lengthens gently between the crown and the tailbone. Head, shoulders and hips stack above one another.
- The lower ribs settle down and in. The breath widens the ribs sideways and backwards rather than lifting the chest.
- The shoulder blades draw down and slightly together, letting the tops of the shoulders relax.
- The *latissimus dorsi* connects the arms to the trunk: elbows down and a fist's width from the body, armpits closed, back wide.
- The chin draws slightly back, the neck lengthens and the eyes look level and steady. The face and jaw stay relaxed.
- Faults in the upper body often begin lower down. Check the stance and pelvis before working on the shoulders.
- Build the posture from the ground up every time, using the checklist, and work on holding it with the single-check practice.
:::

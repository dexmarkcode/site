# The Punch and the Pulling Hand

::: chapter-intro
The straight punch is performed nine times in Sanchin, always slowly and always under tension, and every punch is paired with the pulling hand that prepares it. This chapter examines the fist, the path of the punch, the timing of its rotation, the work of the pulling hand and the way the whole body drives the technique from the ground. It ends with the faults that most often spoil the Sanchin punch and with tests to check your own.
:::

In most of karate, the punch is about speed. In Sanchin it is about structure. Performed slowly, under tension, against the resistance of your own breath, the Sanchin punch is a very different exercise from the fast, snapping punch you might throw at a pad. It is closer to pushing a heavy object along a track than to throwing a stone.

That slowness is what makes it such a good teacher. At speed, momentum hides the details. The fist can wobble on the way out, the shoulder can lift, the elbow can swing wide, and the punch will still land with some force. At Sanchin pace, every one of those faults is visible and every one of them weakens the technique. A student who learns to punch well in Sanchin finds that their fast punches improve too, because the path, the alignment and the connection to the body are the same.

## The Fist

The punch begins with the fist, and the fist begins with the fingers.

To form the standard karate fist, *seiken*, start with the hand open and the fingers together. Curl the fingers in at their middle and end joints so that the fingertips press into the top of the palm, just below the base of the fingers. Then fold the curled fingers down into the palm. Finally, lay the thumb across the front of the index and middle fingers, between their first and second joints, and press it down. The thumb should not stick out, and it must never be tucked inside the fingers, where it can be broken on impact.

The striking surface is the front of the first two knuckles, those of the index and middle fingers. When the fist is correctly formed and aligned, these two knuckles sit in a straight line with the bones of the forearm. Force travelling up the arm passes through them without bending the wrist.

As we noted in Chapter 8, the grip of a Sanchin fist is strongest in the little and ring fingers. This connects, through the muscles on the underside of the forearm, to the back, and it helps keep the wrist straight. The index finger and thumb are firm but should not be clenched so hard that the tension runs up into the front of the shoulder.

Centre and right: the wrist bent upwards and downwards. A bent wrist leaks force and can be injured on impact.](figures/fig14_1.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Wall Press**

Make a fist and place the first two knuckles against a wall at shoulder height, arm almost straight. Lean gently into the wall so that some of your weight goes through the fist. Look at your wrist from the side, or ask a partner to. It should be straight. If it bends, adjust the fist until the force goes straight up the forearm. Hold for ten seconds, breathing normally. Repeat with the other hand.

This gives you the feeling of an aligned fist under load, which is exactly what you want at the end of each Sanchin punch.
:::

## The Path of the Punch

The Sanchin punch travels from the side of the body, where the pulling hand has placed it, to the centre line in front of the body, at the height of your own solar plexus. The path is as straight as the anatomy allows.

### Starting position

The fist starts at the side of the body, just above the hip bone, palm facing up, elbow pointing straight back. This is the end position of the pulling hand. The forearm is roughly horizontal.

### The line

From there, the fist travels forward and slightly inward. Because it starts at the side of the body and finishes at the centre line, its path is not exactly parallel to the direction you are facing; it angles in. But it is straight. It does not swing out and come back in, and it does not dip or rise.

The elbow follows the fist closely, brushing past the side of the body as the arm extends. It stays pointing down and back for as long as possible, turning only in the last part of the movement as the fist rotates. Teachers sometimes say that the elbow should "slide along the ribs". If the elbow flares out sideways early in the punch, the path of the fist becomes curved and the arm loses its connection to the back.

### The target

The punch finishes on the centre line of your own body, at the height of your solar plexus, the soft area just below the breastbone. In application, this would be the opponent's centre line at the same height. The arm is almost straight at full extension, with a very slight bend in the elbow. The fist is directly in front of you, not across to the other side.

Some lineages place the target slightly higher, at the level of the lower edge of the breastbone, or slightly lower. The differences are small. What matters is that every punch in the kata goes to the same place.

The dashed paths show two common faults: swinging out and in, and crossing beyond the centre line.](figures/fig14_2.png)

::: drill
**Drill 14.1 – Wall Slide**

*You need:* a wall or a doorframe.

*What to do:* Stand side-on to a wall in sanchin dachi, so that the arm nearest the wall is the punching arm and your elbow, at the pulling-hand position, is just touching the wall. Punch slowly forward, keeping the elbow lightly in contact with the wall for as much of the punch as you can. The elbow will leave the wall only in the last part of the punch, as the fist moves in to the centre line and rotates.

*What to look for:* If the elbow leaves the wall early, it is flaring out. If it scrapes hard against the wall, you may be holding it too tight; it should brush, not press.

*Repetitions:* Ten slow punches on each side, three times a week for two weeks.

*Why it matters:* The wall gives the elbow a physical reference and teaches the close, straight path that keeps the punch connected.
:::

## The Rotation

The Sanchin punch starts palm up and finishes palm down. Somewhere along the way the fist rotates through half a turn. When that rotation happens matters a good deal.

In the version taught in this book, as in most Goju-Ryu teaching, the fist rotates during the last third of the punch. For the first two-thirds it stays palm up, or turns only slightly towards palm-inward. In the last third it turns smoothly over to palm down, completing the rotation exactly as the punch arrives and the tension peaks.

There are several reasons for this timing. Keeping the palm up for most of the path keeps the elbow down and close to the body, because rotating the forearm early tends to lift the elbow out to the side. The late rotation adds a final tightening of the forearm and the back at the moment of greatest tension, which helps to lock the structure. And in application, a late rotation is said to add a slight twisting force at impact, though how much that matters is debated.

Whatever the reasons, the practical rule is clear: do not rotate early. A fist that turns palm down halfway through the punch is one of the most common faults in the kata, and it usually goes with a flaring elbow.

## The Pulling Hand

Every punch in Sanchin is prepared by a pulling hand, *hikite*. The hand that is about to punch is drawn back from the block to the side of the body. In other kata, and in basic training, hikite is also the hand that pulls back while the other hand punches, so that the two arms work in opposition. In Sanchin, because the other arm is holding the block, the pulling hand is mainly a preparation. But it is still a technique in its own right, and it deserves care.

### The path

From the block, the fist opens slightly or stays closed, depending on your teacher, and draws back and down in a smooth curve to the side of the body. As it arrives, it closes fully, palm up, just above the hip bone. The elbow draws straight back, close to the body.

### The position

The finished hikite sits at the side of the body, not in front of it and not behind it. The forearm is roughly horizontal. The elbow points straight back, and the upper arm is close to the side of the ribs. The shoulder is down and the shoulder blade is drawn back slightly, but not so far that the chest opens and the shoulder rolls back.

### Its purpose

In application, hikite is commonly interpreted as a grab and pull: the hand seizes an opponent's arm or clothing and draws it in, pulling them onto the punch that follows or off balance. Chapter 26 looks at this in more detail. In training, hikite also teaches the arm to draw back under control, keeping its connection to the back, so that the punch can be launched from a stable, loaded position.

::: fault
**Problem: "When I pull my hand back, my shoulder rolls forward."**

*Check.* Watch yourself in a mirror from the side during the pulling hand. Does the front of your shoulder move forward as the elbow goes back?

*Cause.* The elbow is being pulled back by the muscles at the front of the shoulder and the upper arm alone, rather than by the shoulder blade drawing back and down. Sometimes the fist is also being pulled too far back, behind the body, which forces the shoulder to compensate.

*Correct.* Practise the pulling hand slowly, ten times on each side, with your opposite hand resting on the front of the pulling shoulder. Initiate the movement by drawing the shoulder blade back and down; only then let the elbow follow. Stop the fist at the side of the body, not behind it.

*Confirm.* Watch in the mirror again. The front of the shoulder should stay still or move slightly back.
:::

## Driving the Punch From the Ground

We saw in Chapter 8 that tension in Sanchin builds from the ground up: feet, legs, pelvis, trunk, back, arm, fist. The punch is where this sequence matters most. A punch driven from the shoulder alone is weak and disconnected, however strong the shoulder. A punch driven from the ground carries the whole body behind it.

In practice, driving the punch from the ground means three things.

**The rear foot presses.** As the punch begins, you increase the pressure of the rear foot into the floor, as though pushing the floor away behind you. This must not lift the rear heel or move the foot. It is pressure, not movement.

**The hips stay square.** The pressure from the rear leg travels up through the hip on that side. In many karate techniques the hips rotate to add power to the punch. In Sanchin, they stay almost square, with at most a very slight turn. The force is transmitted through the pelvis, not generated by twisting it.

**The back delivers the arm.** The latissimus muscle and the muscles around the shoulder blade engage as the arm extends, so that the arm is pushed out from the back rather than reaching from the shoulder. You should feel the back on the punching side widen as the punch arrives.

When these three work together, a partner pressing back against your fist at the end of the punch will feel the resistance come all the way from your rear foot. When they are missing, the partner will feel only the arm and shoulder, and a firm push will fold your arm or rock you back.

![**Figure 14.4** The punch driven from the ground. The rear foot presses into the floor, the force travels through the square hips and the widening back, and the arm is delivered from the back. The pulling hand on the other side, in kata other than Sanchin, works in opposition; in Sanchin, the other arm holds the block.](figures/fig14_4.png)

::: test
**Test Yourself: The Push-Back**

Stand in sanchin dachi and punch slowly to full extension. Ask a partner to place their palm against your fist and push back towards you, slowly, building pressure over three seconds, along the line of your forearm.

- If your elbow bends, your arm is not braced; check the fist alignment and the tension in the arm.
- If your shoulder is pushed back or up, the arm is not connected to the back; work on the shoulder blade draw-down.
- If your whole body rocks back, your rear leg is not pressing and your stance is not rooted; work on the rear foot pressure and the pelvic lock.
- If none of these happens and you feel the push in your rear foot, the punch is connected.
:::

## Common Faults of the Punch

### Reaching with the shoulder

The shoulder on the punching side moves forward with the punch, often rising at the same time. This is the most common fault of all. It disconnects the arm from the back, twists the trunk and, in testing, makes the punch easy to push back.

*Correction.* Keep the shoulder blade drawn down and back throughout the punch. Practise with a partner's hand resting on the front of your punching shoulder; it should not be pushed forward.

### Elbow flaring (the "chicken wing")

The elbow swings out sideways early in the punch, so that the fist travels in a curve. The punch is weak and the path is long.

*Correction.* Drill 14.1, the wall slide. Delay the rotation of the fist until the last third.

### Early rotation

The fist turns palm down early in the punch, often in the first third. It usually causes the elbow to flare.

*Correction.* Practise the punch in three distinct parts, slowly: one-third out with palm up, one-third out with palm up, one-third out with rotation. Then smooth them together.

### Crossing the centre line

The punch travels past the centre line, so that the fist ends in front of the opposite shoulder. The trunk usually twists to allow it.

*Correction.* Place a vertical strip of tape on a mirror at your centre line and punch to it. Check that your hips stay square.

### Bent wrist

The wrist bends up, down or sideways at the end of the punch. See Figure 14.1 and the Wall Press test.

### Locked elbow

The elbow snaps fully straight or hyperextends at the end of the punch. Under tension, this places a heavy load on the joint.

*Correction.* Stop the punch a fraction short of full straightness. The arm should look straight but have a very slight softness at the elbow.

### Leaning

The upper body leans forward with the punch, so that the shoulders move ahead of the hips.

*Correction.* Practise with the stick test from Chapter 6, the stick resting along your spine. It should not leave the back of your head or your sacrum during the punch.

### Rear heel lifting

The rear heel comes off the floor as the punch drives forward.

*Correction.* Reduce the forward drive and increase the downward pressure of the rear foot. Check the rear knee is not over-bent.

::: drill
**Drill 14.2 – Partner Resistance Punch**

*You need:* a partner.

*What to do:* Stand in sanchin dachi with your fist at the pulling-hand position. Your partner stands in front of you and places their palm flat against your fist. Punch slowly forward while your partner provides gentle, steady resistance, allowing your fist to move but making you work for it. The punch should take about five seconds, on a full out-breath.

*What to look for:* Your partner watches your shoulder and elbow. If the shoulder reaches or the elbow flares, they reduce the resistance until you can punch correctly against it.

*Progression:* Over several weeks, your partner can gradually increase the resistance, but never to the point where your technique breaks down.

*Why it matters:* Resistance makes the connection between the punch and the body unmistakable. It is one of the best ways to learn to drive the punch from the ground.
:::

## The Punch in the Kata

In the kata, each punch is one part of a cycle that also includes the pulling hand and the return to the block. The three must flow together. The pulling hand prepares, the punch executes, the return recovers, and each leads smoothly into the next.

A useful way to think about the cycle is as a single continuous loop drawn by the fist: back and down to the hip, straight forward to the centre, back and up to the block. If you could trace the path of the fist through the whole cycle, it would form a closed shape, with no sharp corners and no backtracking. Students who think of the cycle in this way tend to avoid the stop-start movement described in Chapter 9.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- The Sanchin punch is an exercise in structure, not speed. Slowness reveals faults that speed hides.
- The fist is formed with the thumb locked across the first two fingers. The first two knuckles align with the forearm. The grip is strongest in the little and ring fingers.
- The punch travels in a straight line from the side of the body to the centre line at solar-plexus height, the elbow brushing past the ribs.
- The fist rotates from palm up to palm down during the last third of the punch.
- The pulling hand draws the fist back to the side of the body, palm up, elbow straight back, shoulder down. In application it is usually read as a grab and pull.
- The punch is driven from the ground: the rear foot presses, the hips stay square, the back delivers the arm.
- Common faults are reaching with the shoulder, flaring the elbow, rotating early, crossing the centre line, bending the wrist, locking the elbow, leaning and lifting the rear heel.
:::

::: practice
### Training Tasks

1. Practise the Wall Press test on both hands daily for a week.
2. Practise Drill 14.1, the wall slide, three times a week for two weeks.
3. Practise ten punches on each side in three distinct parts, as described under early rotation, then ten smooth punches with the rotation in the last third.
4. With a partner, carry out the Push-Back test on both sides and record which part gave way, if any.
5. Practise Drill 14.2, partner resistance punch, twice a week for a month.
6. Watch your latest recording of the whole kata and count how many of the nine punches show any of the faults listed in this chapter. Note which fault appears most often.
:::

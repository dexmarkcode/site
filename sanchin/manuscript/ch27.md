# Training for Life

::: chapter-intro
Sanchin is a kata you can practise for the whole of your life, and many people do. This final chapter looks at how the practice changes over the years: how to adapt it for age, injury and health conditions; how to teach it to children, adults and older beginners; what teachers look for at gradings; how to keep the practice fresh after thousands of repetitions; and what a lifetime of Sanchin can give you.
:::

There is a photograph that circulates among Goju-Ryu students of an elderly teacher performing Sanchin. He is small and thin, his gi hangs loosely on him and his hair is white. And yet his stance looks as though it has been poured in concrete. His shoulders are low, his face calm, his eyes steady on some point beyond the camera. Everything about him says that nothing short of a lorry could move him.

Whoever he was, he had probably been practising Sanchin for sixty years or more. His muscles were no longer those of a young man, but his structure had been refined by tens of thousands of repetitions into something that no longer needed muscle in the way a young person's does. That is one of the gifts of Sanchin: a practice that becomes more refined as the body becomes less powerful.

This chapter is about getting there.

## Sanchin Through the Decades

Over a lifetime of practice, Sanchin tends to change in character, roughly in step with the body.

**In the early years,** whatever your age when you begin, the focus is on learning the shape and the sequence, building the stance and finding the breath. The practice is often effortful and sometimes frustrating. Progress is fast but uneven.

**In the developing years,** typically from a year or two in to around ten years, the focus moves to strength, endurance and testing. Students push their tension higher, take harder testing and build the physical capacity the kata demands. This is the period when Sanchin is most "hard".

**In the mature years,** after a decade or more, the focus shifts again, to refinement. Tension becomes more selective, the breath quieter, the movements more economical. Testing reveals fewer faults, and those it does reveal are subtle. Many practitioners say that in this phase Sanchin becomes more about the mind than the body.

**In later life,** the practice often becomes gentler still: lower tension, natural or Stage 2 breathing, perhaps a slightly higher stance. Yet the structure, built over decades, remains. The elderly teacher in the photograph was in this phase.

These are not fixed stages, and people move through them at different speeds. But it helps to know that the practice is meant to change. A sixty-year-old trying to perform Sanchin as they did at twenty-five is likely to get hurt. A twenty-five-year-old performing it as a gentle mature practice is likely to miss out on the strength and conditioning their body could gain. Each phase has its own right way.

## Practising With Age

If you are over fifty, or come to Sanchin later in life, the following adaptations will help you practise for many years.

**Keep tension moderate.** Most of your practice should be at a peak tension of five or below on the scale from Chapter 8. Occasional sessions at six are fine if your health allows.

**Keep breathing gentle.** Use Stages 2 and 3. Avoid hard ibuki breathing unless your doctor is happy with your blood pressure and heart health, and never hold the breath.

**Raise the stance slightly.** A stance a few centimetres higher than a younger student's reduces the load on the knees and hips without losing the essential structure.

**Prioritise mobility and balance.** These decline with age faster than strength, and they matter enormously for staying safe and independent. Do the mobility routine from Chapter 24 and the balance work from the assessment every day.

**Test gently.** Levels 1 and 2 are entirely sufficient. There is no need for Level 3 or 4 testing.

**Recover well.** Allow more time between hard sessions. Sleep, eat well and keep hydrated.

**Keep going.** Consistency matters even more as you age. A short, gentle Sanchin practice most days is far better than occasional intense sessions.

::: note
**Teacher's Note**

Some of my most rewarding students have been people who started karate in their fifties and sixties. They often arrive nervous, convinced they are too old, and within a year they are standing taller, moving with more confidence and finding that everyday tasks such as climbing stairs and carrying shopping are easier. Sanchin, practised sensibly, is wonderfully suited to older bodies. It builds leg strength, balance, posture and breath control, all things that matter more as we age, and it does so without jumping, running or high-impact movements.
:::

## Practising With Injuries and Health Conditions

Many people practise Sanchin with injuries or long-term health conditions. With sensible adaptations, this is often possible and beneficial. The key is to work with a health professional who knows your condition, to tell your instructor, and to listen to your body.

Some common situations and typical adaptations are listed below. They are general guidance only; your own health professional's advice comes first.

**Knee problems.** Use a higher stance; reduce the inward angle of the front foot; pay meticulous attention to knee tracking (Drill 5.1); avoid knee testing; strengthen the hips and thighs gradually.

**Lower back problems.** Focus on the neutral-to-slightly-tucked pelvis and avoid over-tucking; keep tension moderate; strengthen the trunk with planks and bird dogs; avoid testing the lower back.

**Shoulder problems.** Reduce tension in the arms; keep the block slightly lower if raising the arm to shoulder height is painful; strengthen the shoulder blade muscles gently; avoid testing the affected shoulder.

**High blood pressure, heart conditions, glaucoma or a history of stroke.** Natural or Stage 2 breathing only; moderate tension; no breath-holding ever; no hard testing. Discuss with your doctor before starting and at regular intervals.

**Pregnancy.** Many people continue gentle Sanchin practice during pregnancy with their midwife's or doctor's agreement, using natural breathing, light tension, a comfortable stance and no testing on the trunk. Avoid any bearing down. Stop if anything feels uncomfortable. After birth, return gradually, with attention to the pelvic floor and abdominal muscles, and seek professional advice before resuming firm breathing or tension.

**Pelvic floor problems.** Avoid forceful breathing and bearing down; practise gentle pelvic floor engagement as part of the pelvic tuck; seek advice from a specialist physiotherapist.

## Teaching Sanchin

If you teach, or will teach, Sanchin to others, the following principles may help.

### Teaching children

Children can learn the shape of Sanchin and benefit from its posture, balance and concentration. But their joints, tendons and cardiovascular systems are still developing, and Sanchin for children must be gentle.

- Use natural breathing only. No ibuki and no forceful exhalation.
- Use light tension only, perhaps two or three on the scale.
- Use Level 1 testing only: light touch to check posture.
- Keep sessions short and varied. Children's attention spans are short, and Sanchin is demanding.
- Make it playful where you can: stance games, balance challenges, "statue" holds.
- Focus on posture, stance and calm attention, which are the most valuable gifts the kata can give a child.

### Teaching adult beginners

- Teach the stance thoroughly before the arms, and the arms before the breath.
- Introduce the breath in the five stages of Chapter 7, never jumping straight to full ibuki.
- Introduce testing gradually, beginning with Level 1, and explain its purpose and rules.
- Give one correction at a time. A beginner told five things at once will remember none of them.
- Use positive corrections ("move into my hand") rather than negative ones ("don't lift your shoulder").

### Teaching older beginners

- All the adult beginner principles apply.
- Ask about health conditions privately and adapt accordingly.
- Use a higher stance and moderate tension from the start.
- Emphasise the benefits of posture, balance and breath, which older students often value most.

::: drill
**Drill 27.1 – One Correction**

*For teachers.* For one class, give each student only one correction during Sanchin practice: the one you judge most important for them. Give it clearly, using touch where appropriate. Check that they understand it. Then watch whether it has stuck by the end of the class.

*Why it matters:* Teachers, especially enthusiastic ones, tend to give too many corrections. Students cannot absorb them all and end up confused. One correction that sticks is worth ten that do not.
:::

## Gradings

In most Goju-Ryu associations, Sanchin is performed at gradings at every level. What examiners look for changes with grade.

**At junior coloured-belt grades,** examiners look for the correct sequence, a recognisable sanchin dachi, coordinated breathing without breath-holding and reasonable posture.

**At senior coloured-belt grades,** they look for a stable stance through steps and turns, a connected structure under light testing, good timing of breath and movement and a calm face.

**At black-belt grades,** they look for all of this plus a stance and structure that hold under firmer testing, smooth transitions, controlled tension that rises and falls appropriately and a sense of complete, unhurried composure.

**At senior black-belt grades,** examiners often look less at the details and more at the whole: the quality of attention, the economy of movement, the sense that the kata is fully the student's own.

::: fault
**Problem: "I always perform Sanchin worse at gradings than in class."**

*Cause.* Nerves (Fault 21.14). The stress response raises the heart rate, shortens the breath, tightens the shoulders and narrows attention.

*Correct.* Practise under observation regularly (Drill 21.3). Use the three breaths before (Drill 21.1). Arrive at the grading rested, following the advice in Chapter 24 on timing a lighter week. Perform at about two-thirds of your usual intensity on the day; nerves will add the rest. Focus on the breath and the gaze; let everything else look after itself.

*Confirm.* Ask your teacher for feedback after the grading, and compare it with their feedback on your class performances.
:::

## Keeping Sanchin Fresh

After a few years, and certainly after a few thousand repetitions, Sanchin can become routine. The body knows it so well that the mind drifts. The practice becomes a habit rather than an exploration. This is a real risk, because a Sanchin performed on autopilot teaches very little.

Here are some ways to keep it fresh.

**Change the focus.** Each time you perform the kata, choose one thing to explore: the left foot, the out-breath, the moment of turning, the space between the shoulder blades. You will find something new every time.

**Change the conditions.** Perform it with your eyes closed (carefully, in a safe space). Perform it barefoot outdoors on grass or sand. Perform it as slowly as you possibly can, taking ten minutes. Perform it with no tension at all. Each variation reveals something different.

**Teach it.** Explaining Sanchin to someone else, and watching them learn, shows you your own Sanchin in a new light.

**Test it.** Regular testing keeps you honest. A test that gives way after years of holding is a valuable discovery.

**Return to the basics.** Every so often, go back to Part Two of this book and rebuild your stance from the ground up, as if for the first time. Experienced practitioners almost always find something that has drifted.

**Keep a journal.** Write a sentence after each practice about what you noticed. Over months and years, the journal becomes a record of your Sanchin's growth.

## What a Lifetime of Sanchin Gives

Finally, it is worth asking what all this practice is for. Why spend years, decades, a lifetime, standing in an awkward stance, breathing strangely and moving slowly?

The answers people give vary, but certain themes recur.

**A strong, stable body.** Sanchin builds leg strength, a connected trunk, good posture and balance that last into old age.

**Composure.** Years of staying calm under pressure, breath steady and eyes level, carry over into ordinary life. Many long-term practitioners say this is the greatest benefit.

**Self-knowledge.** Sanchin shows you your habits, physical and mental, more clearly than almost anything else. It shows you where you tense, where you collapse, where you hurry and where you hide.

**A foundation for karate.** For a Goju-Ryu practitioner, Sanchin is the root from which everything else grows. A strong Sanchin makes every other kata and every technique better.

**A daily practice.** There is value simply in having something you do every day, with care and attention, for its own sake. Sanchin can be that.

**Connection.** Sanchin connects you to every teacher and student who has practised it before you, back through Miyagi and Higaonna to the halls of Fuzhou. When you stand in the stance and breathe, you are doing something people have done, in much the same way, for well over a century.

## A Last Word

This book has tried to give you a clear map of Sanchin: its history and principles, its stance, breath and tension, every frame of the kata, its testing, its faults, its conditioning and its applications. Maps are useful. But the territory is your own body, your own breath and your own mind, and nobody can walk it for you.

So close the book, step into sanchin dachi, set your feet, tuck your hips, drop your shoulders, lift your crown, fix your eyes on a point ahead and take one slow breath. Then begin. And tomorrow, begin again.

::: keypoints
### Chapter Summary

- Sanchin practice changes over a lifetime: learning, developing, maturing and, in later life, becoming gentler while the structure remains.
- Older practitioners should keep tension and breathing moderate, raise the stance slightly, prioritise mobility and balance and test gently.
- Many injuries and health conditions can be accommodated with sensible adaptations, guided by a health professional.
- Children should use natural breathing, light tension and Level 1 testing only. Adult and older beginners should be taught the stance first, the breath in stages and testing gradually, with one correction at a time.
- Examiners look for different things at different grades, moving from sequence and stance towards composure and ownership.
- Keep Sanchin fresh by changing the focus and conditions, teaching it, testing it, returning to basics and keeping a journal.
- A lifetime of Sanchin gives a strong, stable body, composure, self-knowledge, a foundation for karate, a daily practice and a connection to the tradition.
:::
